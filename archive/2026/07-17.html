LEAD STORY

Burnham took Labour with tax and climate tests waiting

Andy Burnham takes the Labour leadership today with a promise to give communities more control and spread growth, before becoming prime minister next week. A new cabinet is due on Monday, and the appointment process remains tightly held as Westminster waits for the Treasury and climate briefs to land. (The Guardian, BBC, The Guardian)

The first pressure points are already public. Business leaders warned against a summer of tax speculation, while climate campaigners are waiting for decisions on heat adaptation, energy prices, reindustrialisation and North Sea drilling after three UK heatwaves this year. (The Guardian, The Guardian)

NEWS

British Steel moved into public ownership

British Steel was formally taken into public ownership 15 months after ministers stepped in to prevent the Scunthorpe works from closing and to protect about 4,000 jobs. The state takeover removes the plant from Jingye and leaves compensation to be assessed by a valuer. (The Guardian)

ICC abuse allegations reached open court politics

Two women accusing Karim Khan, the International Criminal Court chief prosecutor, of sexual abuse spoke publicly about their claims. The allegations have surrounded the court for two years and now place one of its most prominent lawyers under sharper institutional pressure. (The Guardian)

MI5 was reprimanded over a neo-Nazi informant

A watchdog criticised MI5 for misleading the public about its relationship with a neo-Nazi informant who used his agency role to threaten his girlfriend with violence. The case centres on agent X and the security service’s handling of his status. (The Guardian)

US student visas moved under tighter federal control

Foreign students in the US will need federal permission to stay longer than four years under new Trump administration visa rules. Universities will also lose the power to extend student visas, shifting more control to Washington. (BBC)

Genoa bridge convictions closed a long case

Thirty-two people were convicted over the 2018 Morandi Bridge collapse in Genoa, which killed 43 people. Giovanni Castellucci, the former chief executive of Autostrade per l’Italia, received the longest sentence, at 12 years. (The Guardian)

Wildfire smoke spread across North America

Smoke from Canadian and Minnesota wildfires triggered air quality alerts in more than 20 US states, with unhealthy conditions stretching from Minnesota toward New York. Chicago briefly ranked among the world’s worst major cities for air quality. (The Guardian)

New Zealand confirmed its first H5N1 case

New Zealand confirmed H5N1 bird flu for the first time after a brown skua tested positive in Wellington. Officials urged the public to report clusters of sick or dead birds as conservationists watched the risk to native species. (The Guardian)

Brazil prepared reciprocity against US tariffs

Brasilia prepared to use its reciprocity law after Washington announced a new 25% tariff round on Brazilian products. The fight moved straight into the 2026 campaign, with Lula’s allies and Flavio Bolsonaro trading blame and a Genial/Quaest survey finding more voters blame Flavio than Lula for the tariffs. (Canal Meio, Folha)

Brazilian business groups warned that the political dispute had worsened the tariff outlook for companies, while the supreme court said it would not yield to external pressure after criticism from the Trump administration. (Folha, Folha)

London market pressure returned in three bids

Three UK-listed companies faced takeover bids in one day: Rotork from ABB for £4.1bn, Gooch & Housego from a US investment firm for £346m, and Ramsdens from another US buyer for £230m. The deals renewed concern over the hollowing out of London’s public market. (The Guardian)

Vodafone settled its franchisee dispute

Vodafone settled a High Court claim from 62 former franchisees who alleged the group enriched itself at their expense by up to £85m. The agreement was reached without admission of liability after a 19-month dispute. (The Guardian)

Trump’s crypto income drew fresh scrutiny

Donald Trump made at least $1.4bn from crypto in one year, putting the president’s digital-asset businesses back at the centre of questions over money, power and political access. Justin Sun’s role in the Trump family’s crypto orbit drew particular attention. (The Guardian)

TECHNOLOGY

Moroccan spyware use came into fuller view

A former Moroccan intelligence insider described widespread use of Pegasus and other hacking tools against journalists, human rights defenders, French politicians, Spanish ministers and police officers. Pegasus can open a target’s phone, read messages and activate its recorder or camera. (The Guardian)

A neural bypass restored movement and touch

Keith Thomas, paralysed from the chest down after a swimming accident, can feed himself and drink from a cup after a brain implant and months of training. The double neural bypass helped restore arm and hand movement and some sensation of touch. (The Guardian)

A nearby rocky planet showed an atmosphere

Scientists confirmed an atmosphere around LHS 1140b, a rocky planet 49 light years away in its star’s habitable zone. The finding makes the planet a sharper target in the search for worlds that could hold liquid water. (The Guardian)

Google workers demanded AI-era layoff protections

More than 4,500 Google workers signed a petition asking Sundar Pichai for layoff protections and buyout options while the company pours money into AI. The labour push came the same day Google renamed NotebookLM as Gemini Notebook and tied it more closely to Gemini and Search. (The Guardian, The Verge, MacMagazine)

Games and streaming added more generative AI

Roblox will let users create games with AI inside its mobile app, and Epic will let Fortnite creators publish experiences with AI-powered voiced characters from 30 July. Netflix said roughly 300 titles on its platform used generative AI, mostly in post-production. (The Verge, The Verge, The Verge)

Meta AI added a youth safety alert

Meta AI will alert parents when young users have conversations involving suicide after a separate parental-protection feature was introduced on Instagram. The change extends child-safety controls into the company’s chatbot layer. (MacMagazine)

CULTURE

Nolan’s Odyssey landed with rare unanimity

Christopher Nolan’s $250m Imax adaptation of The Odyssey drew near-universal acclaim as full reviews arrived, with several major outlets awarding five stars. The release put Homer’s epic back into mainstream film and reading culture at once. (The Guardian, Canal Meio, The Guardian)

Claudia Winkleman left her chatshow

Claudia Winkleman quit The Claudia Winkleman Show after one series and seven episodes. The Traitors presenter decided not to continue after finding the format too nerve-racking to enjoy. (The Guardian)

Ava DuVernay returned to constitutional history

Ava DuVernay made 14th, a Netflix documentary on the 14th amendment and birthright citizenship, for release later this year. The film follows her 2016 documentary 13th and arrives as Donald Trump targets protections tied to the amendment. (The Guardian)

Hal Williams died at 91

Hal Williams, known for Sanford and Son and 227, died at his home in California aged 91. His television career also included The Waltons and Private Benjamin. (The Guardian)

Rebecca Perry won Waterstones’ debut prize

Rebecca Perry won the 2026 Waterstones debut fiction prize for May We Feed the King, a novel about a present-day museum curator and a medieval monarch. The book was chosen from a six-title shortlist. (The Guardian)

CDs kept their physical-media rebound

US CD sales reached 16.3m in the first half of 2026, up 16% year on year, as fans looked for cheaper physical ways to support artists. The rebound sat alongside the broader vinyl and collector economy. (The Verge)

SPORTS

The World Cup final drew politics and smoke

Donald Trump will attend Sunday’s Spain-Argentina World Cup final in New Jersey. New York and New Jersey officials also issued an air-quality health alert because of wildfire smoke days before the match. (The Guardian, BBC)

England turned to Tuchel’s next repair job

Thomas Tuchel said he is fully committed to leading England at Euro 2028 after the World Cup semi-final defeat by Argentina. Some players were disappointed by the tactics, and the squad now turns toward qualification with questions over creativity and risk. (The Guardian, BBC, BBC)

Argentina’s Falklands banner went to Fifa

Keir Starmer supported a Fifa investigation after Argentina players displayed a banner asserting their country’s claim to the Falkland Islands following the semi-final win over England. Downing Street said the islands belong to the UK. (The Guardian)

Premier League business gathered pace

Arsenal moved close to a £34m agreement for Club Brugge forward Christos Tzolis, while Dominik Szoboszlai agreed terms on a new long-term Liverpool contract. Nottingham Forest’s Oliver Glasner set European football as the target after taking charge. (The Guardian, The Guardian, The Guardian)

LIFESTYLE

Energy drinks will be banned for under-16s

England will ban sales of high-caffeine energy drinks to under-16s from April next year. Lower-caffeine soft drinks will not be affected. (BBC)

Heat pushed Europeans toward portable cooling

A previously little-known Chinese portable air conditioner, the Midea PortaSplit, became a household name in Europe during the summer heatwaves. The surge shows how quickly domestic cooling habits are changing as extreme heat becomes more common. (BBC)

Britain’s food supply stayed below old self-sufficiency levels

The UK now produces about 57% of the food it consumes, down from more than 80% at an earlier point. The gap has moved food security into national-security language as climate, trade and land-use pressures mount. (The Guardian)

A TikTok-linked inquest was reopened

The inquest into the death of 14-year-old Jools Sweeney will be reopened after a High Court ruling allowed previously omitted social-media evidence. His parents believe an online blackout challenge may have played a role in his death. (The Guardian)
