LEAD STORY

Burnham moved against the jury-trial curb

Andy Burnham signalled he will scrap inherited plans to curb jury trials in England and Wales, putting juries back at the centre of the justice system after one of the largest proposed court overhauls of the Starmer years. The move is his clearest break yet with the government he replaced. (Guardian)

John Healey set 28 October for the autumn budget, giving the new government its first hard fiscal date. The chancellor now has to carry Burnham’s promises on devolution, households and regional investment while borrowing costs keep the fiscal rules tight. (Guardian)

NEWS

Labour held Greater Manchester after Burnham

Bev Craig won the Greater Manchester mayoral byelection and will succeed Burnham in one of England’s most powerful regional posts. Labour took back areas where Reform and the Greens had gained ground in May, and Craig secured nearly twice Reform candidate Sian Astley’s vote. (Guardian, BBC)

Iran hit tankers in the Strait of Hormuz

Iran said it struck two oil tankers trying to pass through the Strait of Hormuz under US military escort, while four other tankers changed course. The attack widened the pressure on energy prices as the resumed US-Iran conflict moved deeper into a key shipping lane. (Guardian)

Gaza talks stalled over Hamas disarmament

An Israeli official said troops would stay in Gaza without a Hamas disarmament process that Israel accepts as real. Donald Trump insisted Israel backed a proposal tying Palestinian weapons to an Israeli withdrawal, leaving the diplomatic test inside the details of enforcement. (BBC, Guardian)

Ceuta became Europe’s border crisis

At least 57 people died trying to reach Spain’s enclave of Ceuta after tens of thousands crossed from Morocco in a single day. Madrid said most had returned by Friday evening, while Italy suspended Schengen free movement with Spain as EU pressure grew around the episode. (Guardian, BBC)

A UK-linked Iran spying case reached Cyprus

Rashad Sultanov, a 44-year-old British-Azerbaijani man from London, was arrested in Cyprus on suspicion of conducting hostile surveillance on a British airbase and passing information to Iran’s Revolutionary Guards. Extradition proceedings are continuing. (Guardian)

Courts cleared one child-health trial as families sued a school

The high court cleared the way for a King’s College London clinical trial of puberty blockers after rejecting a legal challenge. In a separate case, ten families sued a school over calming rooms they say harmed children after earlier scrutiny of the practice. (Guardian, BBC)

Europe’s fire weather carried a climate fingerprint

A World Weather Attribution analysis found the hot, dry and windy conditions behind wildfires in Spain were made at least 20 times more likely by global heating, with the conditions in France made twice as likely. The finding put the continent’s fire season directly inside the climate account. (Guardian)

Brazil’s right lost alliance room

Lula admitted the risk that Donald Trump could interfere in Brazil’s election to help Flávio Bolsonaro. Flávio’s own coalition map tightened as the PP chose neutrality and Republicanos prepared to reject an alliance, removing Tereza Cristina from the vice-presidential picture and narrowing the appeal to the centre-right. (Folha, Folha, Folha)

Brazil’s investigations moved closer to the Planalto

André Mendonça authorised a Federal Police inquiry into Fábio Luís Lula da Silva, Lulinha, over suspected corruption and influence peddling tied to medical-cannabis authorisations. Police documents also pointed to gifts from a former Banco Master partner to politicians and 74 calls with senator Jaques Wagner. (Meio, Folha)

Petrol reached a new Iran-war high

UK petrol averaged 160p a litre at the pump, reversing the fall to 151p seen after the earlier Middle East ceasefire. Diesel was up 14.5p to 179p and was forecast to reach 185p, adding another summer cost to household travel. (Guardian)

Apple’s market value fell back below $5tn

Apple shares fell 7.35% to $308.91 after its latest results call, leaving the company valued at about $4.537tn only days after it crossed the $5tn line. Slower App Store and services growth, supply-chain strain and heavier AI spending expectations weighed on the stock. (MacMagazine)

Ben & Jerry’s became an ownership fight again

Ben Cohen pressed the case for freeing Ben & Jerry’s from corporate ownership, arguing that a boycott of Magnum brands could force a sale. The dispute keeps the ice-cream company’s politics, product identity and parent-company control in one consumer fight. (Guardian)

TECHNOLOGY

EU AI labels moved from principle to rule

From 2 August, companies in the EU must label artificially generated images, audio and text designed to look authentic. The rule puts realistic synthetic media, from fake political clips to generated audio, inside a mandatory disclosure regime. (Guardian)

Google pulled an Earth deepfake tool after one day

Google shut down a Google Earth feature that let users alter satellite, aerial and 3D imagery with text prompts. The tool had already produced reality-warping images of border scenes and Gaza locations, turning map data into an AI-image risk almost immediately. (The Verge, The Verge)

Tesla faced a 1.2m-car suspension probe

The US National Highway Traffic Safety Administration opened a preliminary investigation into nearly 1.2m Tesla vehicles after complaints of suspension failures that could cause a loss of directional control. The probe covers 2018-2020 Model 3 and 2021-2023 Model Y vehicles. (The Verge)

Platforms tightened their synthetic-content defences

Snapchat joined YouTube, LinkedIn and Substack in efforts to reduce low-quality AI-generated material. The move shows synthetic-media moderation spreading beyond election deepfakes and into everyday publishing, feeds and creator tools. (BBC)

Epic raised the Apple fight in Brazil

Tim Sweeney accused Apple, without presenting evidence, of lobbying the US government to damage relations with Brazil after Brazilian authorities forced changes to the App Store and iOS. The dispute extends the app-store fight into trade pressure and Brazilian platform regulation. (MacMagazine)

An app turned smart-glasses anxiety into a product

A paid iPhone app promising to detect nearby smart glasses climbed the US App Store rankings, using Bluetooth signals as its advertised method. The popularity of the tool shows how wearable cameras are creating a privacy market around Meta, Ray-Ban and Snap hardware. (MacMagazine)

CULTURE

Boy George left Jesus Christ Superstar

Boy George pulled out of the London Palladium production, where he was due to play Herod, days after releasing a song supporting Israel’s military campaign. The departure turns a West End casting into the latest cultural consequence of the Gaza war. (Guardian)

A $2m crime-novel deal collapsed over AI doubts

Agents withdrew Jerry Falade’s debut crime manuscript Call Me, I’ll Hide the Body after saying they could no longer authenticate how it evolved. The book had attracted an offer of more than $2m from Minotaur in a 14-way auction and had been planned for 2028 publication. (Guardian)

Massive Attack were banned from Singapore

Singapore told Massive Attack not to return after a pro-Palestine display at a Wednesday show. The decision puts the band’s long-running political stagecraft into a formal city-state sanction. (BBC)

Marvel’s Blade reboot stopped moving

Mahershala Ali confirmed the planned Blade reboot is no longer going forward after years of attachment to the role. The collapse ends, for now, Marvel’s attempt to bring the vampire-hunting character back with a two-time Oscar winner in front. (Guardian)

Nick Cave turned Brighton into a city event

Nick Cave and the Bad Seeds played a secret rooftop set in Brighton before their advertised Preston Park show, with a cinema takeover, pop-up shop and Red Hand Files postbox around the main performance. The weekend made a single concert feel like a local cultural festival. (Guardian)

SPORTS

Fifa pushed toward a 64-team World Cup

Fifa accelerated work on expanding the World Cup to 64 teams from 2030, opening a research tender with analysis due by 11 September and a decision possible this year. The plan landed during the same week as growing opposition to the governing body’s commercial-rights ambitions. (Guardian, BBC)

Chelsea took another regulatory hit

Chelsea were fined £10m by the FA and given a suspended transfer ban over agent breaches from the Roman Abramovich era, while Mykhailo Mudryk became free to play after an appeal against his doping ban. One Premier League club ended the day with punishment behind it and a player route reopened. (Guardian, BBC)

Premier League summers stayed unsettled

Arsenal began preparing their title defence with players carrying extra World Cup minutes and the Vinícius Júnior question still hovering over wage structure. Newcastle confirmed Eddie Howe’s resignation after nearly five years as head coach, leaving another major club in transition before the season. (Guardian, BBC)

LIFESTYLE

Supplement spending met a harder evidence test

Common supplements such as multivitamins, collagen, magnesium and creatine came under a practical evidence check, with the useful answer depending on diet, deficiency, training load and sleep. The clearest saving is still avoiding pills bought for vague reassurance. (Guardian)

Hobbies moved further into health care

Social prescribing kept turning sea swimming, painting, movement, nature and service into parts of everyday mental-health support. The practical advice is to choose activities that are repeatable, social enough to hold attention and low-friction enough to survive bad weeks. (Guardian)

Urban gardeners carried more of the city

Public-space gardeners are turning neglected urban corners into cooler, more biodiverse and safer places, even as parks and green spaces remain precarious local-authority services. The work makes ordinary maintenance part of the climate and wellbeing infrastructure. (Guardian)

Summer buying split between cooling and skincare

Ice-cream makers had their strongest case during weeks above 30C, from compressor models to the Ninja Creami, while LED face masks faced a dermatologist-led value check. The consumer lesson was simple: cooling gadgets are easy to understand; beauty tech still needs proof. (Guardian, Guardian)

Books kept shaping holiday routes

Reader travel choices ran from Dervla Murphy’s Northern Ireland to Elena Ferrante’s Naples, turning summer trips into extensions of the books that first fixed places in memory. It is a quieter kind of itinerary, led by scenes, writers and local texture. (Guardian)
