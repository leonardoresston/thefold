The questions around Reform’s candidates now reach Farage himself

The latest footage from ITV’s year-long undercover investigation of Reform UK has moved the argument about the party’s vetting from its local candidates to Nigel Farage’s own decisions. Chris Parry, a retired rear admiral selected for the Hampshire and Solent mayoral election, had said that David Lammy should return to the Caribbean and questioned the primary loyalty of several politicians from minority backgrounds. Ed Sumner, Reform’s head of communications, was recorded calling Parry clearly racist and saying he should be removed. Three weeks after the dispute began, Parry was still the candidate and Farage publicly said an apology could settle the matter. In a private conversation recorded by the reporter, Farage instructed staff to secure that apology; Sumner answered that the leader was protecting him. (Guardian)

Parry was eventually suspended in March after further remarks emerged, including his description of members of a Jewish neighbourhood watch group as cosplayers. The delayed action is harder to explain as a screening failure. Senior staff had identified the problem, advised the leader and watched the candidate remain in place. Labour has asked Farage to explain why he stood by Parry, while the full documentary, Undercover: A Year in Reform, is due on Sunday. It follows footage released a day earlier in which councillors and candidates made racist and misogynistic comments and discussed extreme violence. Several have since left the party or been suspended, leaving some able to continue as independent councillors. (Guardian)

A second recording also exposed the party’s internal culture from another direction. Sarah Pochin, then Reform’s only female MP, described male officials as chauvinistic and said women were excluded, while acknowledging that she presented a friendlier picture in public. Reform has attacked ITV and Hardcash Productions for using false credentials and gaining access to headquarters, computer systems and security information. It says there is no room for racism in the party and that live investigations require due process. Those objections may shape the debate about how the programme was made. The central political question is already plain: when senior colleagues asked Farage to remove a candidate over racist statements, he chose an apology first and suspension only after more evidence became public. (Guardian)

NHS England redraws the rules for wards and changing rooms

Trans women will no longer be placed on female-only wards under new NHS England guidance covering inpatient accommodation, toilets and changing rooms. Single-sex facilities are to be used according to biological sex, with trusts expected to preserve the privacy, dignity and safety of trans patients as well as everyone sharing their accommodation. Clinical need remains the priority when it conflicts with the same-sex rule. Hospitals can provide single rooms or mixed-sex facilities made up of individually lockable rooms, and organisations have been told to audit what they offer. The change replaces guidance that allowed patients and staff to use facilities in line with their gender identity. (Guardian, BBC)

The policy follows the Supreme Court’s 2025 judgment that the Equality Act’s references to women and sex mean biological women and biological sex, including where a trans woman has a gender recognition certificate. A Darlington employment tribunal has since ruled that nurses forced to share a changing room with a trans colleague faced a degrading environment. Sex Matters welcomed the clearer basis for single-sex spaces but asked how trusts would enforce it. TransActual and the Trans+ Solidarity Alliance warned that hospitals short of space and staff will struggle to provide safe alternatives and that some trans people may avoid care. The practical test begins at ward level, where managers must translate a national legal rule into decisions about frightened, ill and vulnerable people while suitable private rooms remain scarce. (Guardian)

Southern England enters severe drought with reservoirs near empty

Parts of Cornwall, Devon, Dorset, Somerset, East Sussex and West Sussex have been placed in severe drought after the hottest summer on record and the driest April-to-September period in the south-east and south-west for more than a century. Ardingly reservoir in West Sussex is at 19.4% of capacity and Wimbleball on Exmoor at 14.9%; the Environment Agency would normally expect reservoir storage to be around 75% at this point in the year. Wimbleball supplies roughly 300,000 properties across Devon, Somerset and Dorset, while Ardingly serves about 280,000 customers in Sussex. Other major reservoirs in Devon and Cornwall are between roughly a fifth and a little over a third full. (BBC)

The declaration increases daily monitoring, water-quality checks and enforcement of permits used by businesses that draw from rivers and groundwater. Drought orders already allow South East Water and South West Water to reduce by millions of litres the water they release from reservoirs into rivers, preserving supplies through winter while adding pressure to wildlife and downstream users. Southern Water has applied for restrictions in Hampshire and the Isle of Wight. Ministers say household taps should keep running, but recovery depends on sustained rain and lower demand. An English winter can refill reservoirs quickly; after six exceptionally dry months, water companies cannot plan on ordinary weather arriving on schedule. (BBC)

Brazil’s two-point election becomes a contest over daily life

Flávio Bolsonaro leads Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva by 51% to 49% among valid votes in the latest Datafolha poll, a statistical tie inside the survey’s two-point margin of error with ten days left before the presidential runoff. Rejection levels are equally tight: 46% say they would not vote for Lula and 44% reject Flávio. The government enters the final stretch with a 41% negative rating and 35% positive, while violence and health are the problems voters mention most often. Both campaigns therefore need a small group of persuadable people at a stage when most supporters have settled their choice. Interviews on national television and a Sunday debate now carry unusual weight because neither candidate has a safe lead to defend. (Folha, Meio)

Lula is trying to turn congressional victories into a direct household offer. A day after the Senate approved the end of the six-day working week, he promised a constitutional amendment guaranteeing free public transport at weekends. That would move the campaign from broad economic confidence to hours at work, the price of a journey and access to a polling station. Abroad, the result is attracting a different kind of attention. Donald Trump’s administration replied to US lawmakers who asked about Washington’s intentions toward Brazil without saying whether it would respect the election result. The omission will deepen concern about outside pressure and election legitimacy. Inside Brazil, the decisive arguments remain immediate: safety, public services, work and whether either candidate can convert a narrow coalition into a governable majority after 25 October. (Folha, Folha)

Economy

A small growth surprise meets a large hole in the Budget

Britain’s economy grew by 0.1% in August, confounding forecasts of a small contraction and giving John Healey one useful number before his first Budget on 28 October. Production rose 0.8%, supported by manufacturing and energy, while construction fell 0.2% and the consumer-facing part of the services economy shrank 0.3%. Food, drink, sport and recreation were among the weak spots as higher petrol, diesel and household energy costs restrained spending. Across the three months to August, GDP expanded 0.7%; output was 2.1% higher than a year earlier, the strongest annual rate since August 2022. Business investment in computing equipment linked to AI infrastructure has helped, though economists expect expensive energy and higher interest rates to slow the final months of the year. (Guardian)

Healey is preparing to ask banks for more money as the Middle East war raises inflation, government borrowing costs and the price of supporting households. The leading option would reverse Jeremy Hunt’s cut to the surcharge on bank profits, taking it from 3% towards its previous 8% and raising more than £2bn a year. HSBC, NatWest, Barclays and Lloyds together generated about £200bn in pre-tax profit over the past five years as higher interest rates widened earnings. Bank executives have lobbied against another rise and warn that a heavier tax burden could move jobs and investment overseas. Smaller lenders want the profit threshold at which the surcharge begins raised from £100m to £500m, an adjustment that could protect new competitors while the largest institutions carry the increase. (Guardian, BBC)

The arithmetic leaves little room for a comfortable compromise. Rising bond yields may have cut as much as half from the £24bn margin previously available under Labour’s fiscal rules. Healey wants to give households and businesses breathing space from energy prices, reassure lenders that debt will be controlled and avoid squeezing departments whose settlements already imply difficult choices. The IMF has upgraded its UK growth forecast and praised progress toward balancing the books, yet it also expects energy costs to bring slower growth in 2027. August’s expansion helps the story he can tell. It contributes only a fraction of the money he needs, which is why the Budget is turning toward a profitable and politically visible sector. (Guardian, Guardian)

Britain pays windfarms to stop while gas plants start

Balancing Great Britain’s electricity network cost £47.9m on Wednesday as the system operator paid windfarms and hydro plants to switch off in areas with excess generation, then paid gas and biomass stations to start where supply was tight. One half-hour period alone cost £1.6m. About nine gigawatts of renewable capacity was curtailed, enough to power more than eight million typical homes, while electricity was also imported through subsea cables. These payments are passed on through energy bills. September’s balancing bill reached a record £505m, and the annual total is expected to climb to £3.2bn. (Guardian)

The country has generating capacity; the difficulty is moving power from windy parts of Scotland to demand further south. The £150bn upgrade of transmission networks is failing to keep pace with renewable projects connecting to the grid. The National Energy System Operator recently asked France for emergency assistance on two days, although it says supplies remained secure and describes the requests as established tools, with no wider failure indicated. New lines could halve balancing costs by the end of the decade if they arrive on time. Until then, households are paying three times for the same bottleneck: to build renewable generation, to leave some of it idle and to run fossil-fuel stations closer to demand. (Guardian)

Technology

ChatGPT steps through the browser door

ChatGPT can now inspect the tabs already open in Google Chrome when its extension is installed and a user tags Chrome from the web or desktop app. OpenAI’s examples include comparing products across several pages, summarising research and bringing information from different tabs into one answer. A redesigned sidebar keeps the full conversation history available beside the current page. In ChatGPT Work, the connection goes further: the assistant can control tabs to send messages, complete forms, download files and group or organise the pages on screen. The change brings much of the abandoned Atlas browser’s ambition into the browser people already use. (The Verge)

Browser context makes the assistant more useful because the user no longer has to copy every relevant detail into a prompt. It also gathers sensitive material into a powerful new permission: personal accounts, draft messages, work documents and shopping decisions often sit open together. The value will depend on how clearly the extension shows what it can see, when control is active and which action is about to be taken. Sending a message or submitting a form carries a different cost from summarising a sofa listing. Moving the model into the sidebar removes friction from ordinary work; careful boundaries now have to become equally ordinary. (The Verge)

An 800-billion-parameter model fits on two desks

Meta has released the weights for Muse Spark Open, a multimodal mixture-of-experts model with more than 800bn parameters and 40bn active for each token. Apple and Meta worked together to optimise it for the M5 Ultra Mac Studio and MLX, Apple’s open-source machine-learning framework. Even compressed to four bits, the model needs 493GB of memory, so the practical setup uses at least two machines and divides the workload between them through RDMA over Thunderbolt 5. Apple’s tests found that linking systems can reduce the delay before a first token by as much as 1.8 times for very long contexts. (MacStories, The Verge, 9to5Mac)

This remains an enterprise experiment. Apple’s 512GB Mac Studio is due at the end of the year, while the current 256GB model with 2TB of storage already costs more than $11,000 and two machines are the minimum. The achievement is architectural: a frontier-scale open model can run locally on computers that sit on a desk and draw power from ordinary wall sockets. Organisations can keep private data on their own hardware, examine the model weights and avoid sending every query to a remote service. Today’s cost confines that freedom to well-funded researchers and companies. The direction is still important. Local AI is moving from small, specialised models toward systems that can challenge the capability of large cloud services. (MacStories, The Verge)

Croydon turns an awkward plot into Britain’s best building

Lion Green Road in Coulsdon has won the RIBA Stirling prize, making it only the second council-led housing project to be named Britain’s best new building. Croydon council originally asked Mary Duggan Architects and Ruff Architects for 80 homes on a steep, wooded site. The final scheme fits 157 flats into five star-shaped brick towers while retaining mature trees, bluebell woodland and a public path to the old railway embankment. Each home has light and ventilation from two sides, an inset balcony and views arranged by rotating the otherwise repeated tower plan. Three blocks provide affordable homes rented at local housing allowance rates; two are for private rent. Every entrance and lobby receives the same treatment, with no lesser route for lower-cost tenures. (Guardian, BBC)

The scheme survived 257 objections and only 18 letters of support when planning began in 2017, largely over parking. Its construction cost of about £2,500 a square metre made it the cheapest project on the Stirling shortlist. The award is also a postscript to Brick by Brick, Croydon’s council-owned developer, which was halted when the authority went bankrupt in 2020 after weak financial controls, difficult sites, Brexit and the pandemic damaged its plans. The company still completed 751 homes, more than half affordable. Lion Green Road shows what survived that failure: dense housing with shared gardens, play areas, allotments and privacy, delivered on public land without reducing every decision to the highest short-term return. As the government prepares £39bn for housing, the winner offers a model worth copying and a warning about the governance needed to carry it through. (Guardian)

AI speed overwhelms a community built on patient reverse engineering

Decomp.dev, a central index for video-game decompilation projects, is closing its main directory and will stop accepting new projects after a wave of AI-assisted ports disrupted the community it served. Decompilation is the painstaking work of reconstructing source code from a finished game, often line by line, so enthusiasts can understand how it works, preserve it and build careful ports. New models can accelerate parts of that process dramatically. The site’s administrator says opportunists have taken years of existing volunteer work, used AI to rush the final stretch and released unstable ports before the original teams were ready. Projects differ over legal risk, the ethics of AI and the quality of outside contributions, yet the same pattern has repeatedly denied their maintainers a meaningful choice. (IGN)

The website will remain online so existing projects can still be tracked. Its retreat will do little to slow the broader trend: Halo: Combat Evolved and Grand Theft Auto V have already appeared briefly as browser games before their publishers intervened, while stranger remixes circulate as demonstrations of what current models can assemble. The loss is social as much as technical. Traditional projects trained programmers, documented old systems and distributed credit through years of visible collaboration. A fast fork can produce a playable result while stripping away that apprenticeship and the trust that kept the work organised. AI has lowered the cost of finishing a port; decomp.dev’s closure shows that communities can pay the difference. (IGN)
