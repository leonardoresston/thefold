LEAD STORY

Trump reopened the Iran war and pulled markets back toward risk

Donald Trump ended the ceasefire with Iran and ordered strikes on 90 targets, saying the operation was aimed at reducing Tehran's ability to threaten shipping around the Strait of Hormuz. Iran answered with attacks on US military targets in Bahrain and Kuwait, turning a short diplomatic pause into a new phase of open conflict. (Canal Meio, The Guardian)

Inside Iran, residents described 48 hours of explosions around port cities after the renewed US attacks. The fighting kept oil, shipping and inflation risk close to the centre of the political day, while allies and opponents tried to read whether Washington was moving toward a limited campaign or a longer confrontation. (The Guardian, Canal Meio)

NEWS

Burnham tried to reset Labour on Gaza before taking over

Andy Burnham apologised for Labour's stance on Gaza and said the party had failed to get the issue right. The intervention landed as Keir Starmer neared his departure from No 10 and declined to rule out resignation honours, giving the leadership handover both a foreign-policy wound and a Westminster standards row. (The Guardian, The Guardian)

Reform faced a police inquiry over Farage-linked donations

Police opened an investigation into two Reform UK donations worth £500,000 from Fiona Cottrell, the mother of George Cottrell, a convicted fraudster and ally of Nigel Farage. The inquiry examines whether the gifts were structured to evade donation restrictions, adding legal pressure to a party already fighting a difficult byelection campaign. (The Guardian, The Guardian)

Gaza aid delivery came under new danger

A Palestinian driver bringing World Central Kitchen food aid into Gaza was killed by an Israeli soldier, according to witnesses and the local truckers' association. The association warned it could suspend operations, a direct threat to food movement in a place already short of reliable aid routes. (The Guardian)

Mexico opened a sovereignty fight over a cartel arrest

Mexico began investigating whether the US misled officials about its role in the capture and transfer of Sinaloa cartel figure Ismael "El Mayo" Zambada Garcia in 2024. The case could become a diplomatic breach if Mexican territory was used without consent in an operation Washington had long denied directing. (The Guardian)

Brazil's presidential field narrowed while Flávio Bolsonaro took more pressure

Aécio Neves abandoned a presidential run and said the PSDB would have no candidate for the Planalto. Flávio Bolsonaro returned from the US defending his tariff diplomacy, faced criticism over campaign command inside the PL, and remained under scrutiny from Rio investigations that could damage his national bid. (Folha Poder, Folha Poder, Canal Meio, Canal Meio)

Brazil's campaign rules tightened around Lula's ministers

The Lula government hardened instructions for ministers and federal officials during the election restriction period, reviewing social media posts that could breach campaign law. The move was designed to reduce openings for Flávio Bolsonaro to challenge government conduct at the electoral court. (Folha Poder)

Tesco's retreat kept investors onside

Tesco's overseas empire continued to shrink as the supermarket group concentrated on the UK and Ireland. Shareholders had little reason to object while the company stayed cash-generative, making retrenchment look like capital discipline after years of international sprawl. (The Guardian)

Capita counted the cost of a pension failure

Capita warned it expected to lose up to £40m over a pension scheme fiasco, another charge against a company still working through the consequences of years of operational strain. The hit keeps outsourcing risk close to the balance sheet and firmly outside the back office. (The Guardian)

German car workers pushed back against VW cuts

Volkswagen workers protested in Germany over proposed job cuts and factory closures. The dispute puts the pressure on one of Europe's industrial anchors as electric-vehicle competition, weak demand and cost targets move from strategy papers into plant-level negotiations. (The Guardian)

TECHNOLOGY

OpenAI moved GPT-5.6 into public release

OpenAI released ChatGPT 5.6 after a delay tied to White House cybersecurity concerns, with a staged rollout after government-approved access came first. The company also introduced ChatGPT Work, pushing the model release into the enterprise market as much as the consumer assistant market. (The Guardian, The Verge, MacMagazine)

The ChatGPT browser folded back into the app

OpenAI began shutting down Atlas less than a year after launch and added integrated browsing to the desktop ChatGPT app. The decision shifts the company away from a standalone browser fight and toward putting web tasks inside its main product. (The Verge, MacMagazine)

Apple's supply chain and AI plans moved together

India removed import tariffs on components used in iPhones, easing part of Apple's manufacturing cost base. Apple also assessed a startup partnership for running larger AI models on the iPhone, while its shares recovered from a late-June drop and reached a new record. (MacMagazine, MacMagazine, MacMagazine)

Microsoft prepared larger security updates

Microsoft's Patch Tuesday cycle is set to expand as the company folds AI security work into regular updates. The change turns model and assistant hardening into a recurring maintenance obligation for Windows customers and administrators. (The Verge)

CULTURE

Bonnie Tyler's death sent a Welsh voice back across pop history

Tributes followed the death of Bonnie Tyler, the Welsh singer whose rasping voice carried some of the biggest power ballads of the 1980s. Catherine Zeta-Jones led public condolences as Tyler's songs returned to the centre of a career remembered for scale, melodrama and durability. (BBC, The Guardian)

Graham Linehan received a Met apology and £25,000

Graham Linehan received compensation and an apology from the Metropolitan police after his arrest at Heathrow last year over gender-critical posts on X. The settlement leaves the public argument over speech, policing and online harm attached to a concrete institutional climbdown. (The Guardian, BBC)

Film's LGBTQ+ representation fell to a three-year low

Glaad's latest survey found LGBTQ+ inclusion in film at its lowest point in three years. The finding lands during a wider industry contraction, with studios spending more cautiously and representation gains again depending on which projects survive the pipeline. (The Guardian)

British pop lost Brian Potter

Brian Potter, the British songwriter linked to hits for Glen Campbell, Take That and others, died aged 87. His catalogue stretched across country-pop, mainstream radio and the professional songwriting era that supplied stars with songs built for long lives. (The Guardian)

SPORTS

France reached the World Cup semi-finals

France beat Morocco to reach the World Cup semi-finals, with Kylian Mbappé scoring after an earlier penalty miss and Ousmane Dembélé adding the second. Morocco's resistance broke under France's press and finishing power, sending one of the tournament favourites another round deeper. (The Guardian, BBC)

England's Norway tie turned into an injury and pressure story

Erling Haaland said the pressure was on England before the World Cup quarter-final against Norway. England also had Marc Guéhi managing a hamstring problem and Declan Rice ill with a bug, while Rice and Martin Ødegaard prepared to put their Arsenal partnership aside for a place in the semi-finals. (BBC, BBC, The Guardian)

Spain's defence became the quarter-final problem for Belgium

Spain reached its World Cup quarter-final against Belgium with Unai Simón still unbeaten in goal and opponents struggling to generate shots. In a tournament full of attacking stars, Spain's record made its defence one of the clearest tactical stories left in the draw. (The Guardian)

Premier League squad planning kept moving beneath the World Cup

Newcastle agreed a £51.5m deal for Johan Manzambi and insisted Bruno Guimarães was not for sale. Manchester United defended its new stadium plan as debt questions grew, keeping two Premier League clubs' summer business active while the World Cup held the main stage. (The Guardian, The Guardian)

Heat became a household and health calculation

Researchers and doctors warned that heatwaves hit women harder, calling for more targeted protection as extreme summer temperatures become normal planning conditions. In Britain, the practical question moved inside the home too, with air-conditioning costs and installation now part of ordinary household preparation. (BBC, BBC)

Summer coping moved from advice to routine

Readers shared practical ways to manage heat on trains, in homes and through the working day, while new wellness guidance looked at why hot weather can worsen mood. The useful answers were plain: shade, hydration, ventilation, slower schedules and a willingness to treat heat as an operating condition. (The Guardian, The Guardian)

M&S took its centenary to fashion week

Marks & Spencer prepared to celebrate 100 years of clothing at London fashion week, using the moment to underline its shift from frumpy high-street shorthand to a brand again trying to speak to younger shoppers. TikTok attention and stronger ranges have turned the anniversary into a retail repositioning test. (The Guardian)

Bishop Auckland built a spectacle economy

Bishop Auckland's cultural revival gathered pace around Auckland Palace and the new Kynren show, with birds of prey, Viking raids and large-scale performance pulling family tourism into a former mining town. The project treats spectacle as regeneration infrastructure as much as a day out. (The Guardian)
