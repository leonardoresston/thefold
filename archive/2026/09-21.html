Europe prepares for Russia to test the edge of Nato

European intelligence chiefs are warning that Russia may be preparing a more dangerous phase of its campaign against Nato, including sabotage aimed at defence industries, armed drones near civilian infrastructure and a possible limited incursion designed to test whether the alliance will act. Poland’s prime minister, Donald Tusk, told parliament that Russian drones or missiles could strike Nato territory within months. Emmanuel Macron called that assessment well documented and gathered French party leaders and presidential candidates for an emergency meeting. Michal Koudelka, who leads the Czech BIS security service, described a range of possible moves: a small invasion across a Russian border, a false-flag operation or a large influence campaign. His timetable was blunt: a test could come within months. (Guardian)

The assessments differ in urgency. Latvia’s security chief, Normunds Mežviets, sees preparations for more decisive action without evidence of an imminent attack. Baltic officials, who spent years urging western Europe to take Moscow seriously, now fear that public panic could deliver one of the Kremlin’s intended results before a weapon is fired. Estonia’s foreign ministry has urged people to remain calm and keep supporting Ukraine. Russia’s military resources are still stretched there, and the alliance’s deterrent is stronger than the public conversation sometimes suggests. Intelligence services cannot yet see an approaching invasion. They can see a campaign of coercion already under way. (Guardian)

That campaign is moving from opportunistic disruption towards targets connected with Europe’s support for Ukraine. Intelligence services are watching railways, arms factories and logistics networks. Sweden’s military intelligence chief, Thomas Nilsson, called the recent appearance of an armed drone at Leipzig airport a turning point because it placed lives and critical infrastructure at risk. Western agencies also attribute arson and sabotage plots across Europe to Moscow. Every unexplained fire or malfunction now absorbs investigative capacity even when Russia had no part in it, making uncertainty itself useful. The pressure is meant to frighten populations, split the alliance and make governments calculate that helping Ukraine carries too much domestic risk. (Guardian)

The context makes miscalculation easier. Ukraine launched one of its largest air attacks of the war against Moscow and other Russian regions on Sunday, while both sides intensify strikes before a fifth winter of full-scale war. American envoys have restarted contacts with Moscow and Kyiv, yet European officials see no evidence that the Kremlin is ready for a genuine settlement. Russia denies any intention to attack Nato, as it denied plans to invade Ukraine in 2022. Europe therefore has two jobs that must be done together: harden the vulnerable airports, railways and industries that Moscow can reach without starting a conventional war, and communicate the threat without amplifying every rumour. Deterrence depends on making Article 5 credible. Public resilience depends on refusing to let fear become Russia’s cheapest weapon. (Guardian)

News & Economy

Germany’s political centre takes two different blows

Friedrich Merz says he will remain German chancellor after his Christian Democrats suffered a historic collapse in two state elections. In Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania, preliminary results put the CDU at 4.9%, below the threshold for entering the state parliament and its worst state result on record. The far-right Alternative für Deutschland took 38.3%, more than twice its previous share. Berlin delivered a different rejection: the leftwing Die Linke was on course to win about 25%, five points ahead of the governing CDU, while the AfD also reached double figures. The firewall maintained by Germany’s other parties should keep the AfD out of the regional government, and a left-leaning coalition may retain power in Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania. The result still gives the party another first-place finish and deepens its claim to be the country’s principal opposition. (Guardian)

Merz entered office 16 months ago promising economic renewal and a smaller AfD. Germany instead faces deindustrialisation, weak growth and high energy costs, while the AfD leads national polling at about 30%. Only one in ten Germans is satisfied with the chancellor’s performance. Merz plans to continue reforms covering taxes, pensions, healthcare and bureaucracy, and Germany’s constitution makes it difficult to replace a chancellor who can still command a parliamentary majority. His party may press him to surrender the CDU leadership, which would weaken any attempt to run again in 2029. The wider European stakes are already visible. The continent’s largest economy is entering a period of political instability as France, Italy, Spain and Poland approach elections in which nativist parties expect gains. Berlin’s vote also shows that dissatisfaction is travelling in more than one direction: the centre lost to the far right in the east and to the far left in the capital. (Guardian)

The Middle East braces for another round of American bombing

Governments across the Middle East are preparing for fresh escalation after Iran said it had intelligence that the United States was planning renewed airstrikes. Donald Trump left Camp David early without an official explanation, and the State Department issued a new security warning for Americans across the region. He then described three possible courses towards Iran: military destruction, deeper economic pressure or an agreement. Tehran’s Revolutionary Guards answered that American bases and interests would become targets if an attack begins. The exchange comes before the UN general assembly in New York, where Trump is due to meet the six members of the Gulf Cooperation Council and has left open the possibility of seeing Iran’s president, Masoud Pezeshkian. Iranian officials continue to use mediators and reject direct talks. (Guardian)

The immediate trigger was a sustained Houthi missile and drone attack on Riyadh, including Saudi Aramco facilities and the export port at Yanbu. Saudi Arabia says it intercepted the barrage, though photographs showed smoke above an oil site. The US treats the Houthis as an Iranian proxy; Tehran denies directing them. The regional map is tightening around two shipping routes. Houthi advances on Yemen’s coast have increased pressure on the Bab al-Mandab entrance to the Red Sea, while the Strait of Hormuz is effectively closed to western traffic and Europe is taking emergency measures against fuel shortages. Washington has limited room for a wider campaign after years of unsuccessful bombing against the Houthis and pressure on weapons stocks from the Iran conflict. Gulf states are left balancing a demand for American protection against the risk that US action places their cities, bases and energy infrastructure in the line of retaliation. (Guardian)

Rape cases will receive fixed dates and specialist courtrooms

Every crown court in England and Wales is to have at least one courtroom designed for rape and serious sexual-offence cases within two years. The rooms will provide private entrances and waiting areas, facilities for evidence to be recorded in advance, and remote access to sentencing. Trials will be given fixed dates instead of floating-list slots that can move at short notice and add months to a wait. Court staff will receive trauma-informed training, and a pilot will give each survivor a single contact through the process. Labour MP Charlotte Nichols, who waived her anonymity after being raped, waited 1,088 days for her case to reach court. Her experience helped shape reforms intended to keep survivors engaged with a system that can compound the harm of the original crime. (Guardian, BBC)

The government is also putting £43m into rape and sexual-abuse support over two years and creating a taskforce to reduce postponed trials and challenge the myths that cause victims to withdraw. The victims commissioner, Claire Waxman, welcomed the design while warning that the backlog is much worse than when specialist courts were first proposed in 2021. Tens of thousands more people are waiting, and a well-equipped room is of little use if a case cannot reach it. The programme therefore has two measures of success: whether survivors can give evidence with greater safety and control, and whether the courts supply enough judges, lawyers and sitting days to honour the fixed dates. The first reform changes the experience of justice. The second determines whether justice arrives at all. (Guardian, BBC)

Severe mental illness is still shortening lives by two decades

Adults with severe mental illness are 4.8 times more likely to die before 75 than the wider population, and their lives are typically 15 to 20 years shorter. An analysis of NHS data by Rethink Mental Illness found that the largest part of the gap comes from physical conditions that can often be prevented or treated. People referred to specialist mental-health services are 6.3 times more likely to die early from liver disease, six times more likely from respiratory illness, 3.8 times more likely from cardiovascular disease and 2.3 times more likely from cancer. Cancer, heart disease, respiratory disease and liver disease together caused 56.5% of deaths among 18- to 74-year-olds with serious mental-health problems in England between 2022 and 2024. Suicide accounted for 7%. (Guardian)

Poverty, poor housing, smoking, inactivity and medication-related weight gain all contribute. Fragmented care adds another hazard: physical symptoms can be attributed to a psychiatric diagnosis and left without proper investigation, a failure known as diagnostic overshadowing. More than 24,000 working-age adults with mental illness died from cardiovascular disease in the latest three-year period, and more than 24,000 from cancer, both record highs. Britain already has evidence-based ways to narrow the gap, including routine physical checks, smoking support and services able to treat mental and physical health together. The government is preparing 100 community mental-health centres and a new strategy. Its test will be whether frequent contact with the NHS becomes an opportunity to find disease earlier. Too many warning signs currently disappear at that doorway. (Guardian)

Brazil’s courts move deeper into the election and the Master affair

Brazil’s election regulators are now handling disputes on several fronts as the October vote approaches. The Superior Electoral Court suspended former federal prosecutor Deltan Dallagnol’s campaign for a Senate seat in Paraná; he plans to appeal. The court also agreed to open a case brought by Flávio Bolsonaro against Lula over a Rio carnival parade, while Lula’s campaign has filed its own complaint alleging that a network of profiles was used to boost Flávio’s content. In São Paulo, governor Tarcísio de Freitas declined an invitation to debate Fernando Haddad on Record television, leaving Haddad to be interviewed alone. These decisions arrive as centrist leaders discuss which presidential candidate they would support in a possible second round, giving procedural rulings and television appearances unusual weight in the final stretch. (Folha, Folha, Folha)

The separate crisis around Banco Master continues to pull the judiciary and financial regulation into the same frame. Supreme Court justice Flávio Dino ordered a review of securities regulator CVM rules governing investment funds and money laundering after the case. Video also emerged of justice Alexandre de Moraes leaving an aircraft belonging to former banker Daniel Vorcaro, adding to scrutiny of their relationship and the court’s handling of the affair. Federal judicial spending reached a record R$164bn in 2025, giving fresh force to arguments over accountability at an institution already in political combat. The facts in the Master investigations still need to be separated from insinuation, particularly during a campaign. That makes disclosure, documented timelines and clear recusal rules especially important. Public confidence will depend on evidence that can be examined; competing partisan narratives about judges and bankers cannot provide it. (Folha, Folha, Folha)

Technology & Culture

Meta challenges the machinery of Britain’s online-safety law

Meta has begun a second legal challenge against Ofcom, appealing the decision to place WhatsApp and Instagram in a category with additional obligations under the Online Safety Act. Those duties cover fraudulent advertising, user controls, transparency and records of illegal content. The company’s earlier case disputes fees calculated from global revenue. TikTok and X are also in court over the information they must provide to the regulator. Ofcom can fine a service up to £18m or 10% of qualifying worldwide revenue, whichever is greater, and uses a levy on the industry to fund enforcement. Meta says it accepts the law and wants disputed questions of scope and process settled. The cumulative effect is still to make the first years of enforcement a contest between a public regulator and companies with larger legal budgets. (Guardian)

Ofcom describes the environment as highly litigious and says judicial review can punish any procedural risk. That creates a structural problem for regulation of fast-moving technology: careful decisions take time, while platforms, products and harms change during the argument. The government plans to update internet law more often, which could improve responsiveness and also produce more contested boundaries. The immediate question is whether WhatsApp and Instagram should carry the tougher category’s duties. The lasting question is whether a regulator can collect evidence, charge fees and impose penalties quickly enough for Parliament’s rules to have practical force. A statute becomes meaningful through implementation, and implementation is where the industry has chosen to fight. (Guardian)

The AI boom acquires a regulator and a debt problem

Donald Trump wants an “AI Force” led by a new federal AI czar as researchers, politicians and technology executives argue over systems capable of acting with less human supervision. The proposal follows calls for independent model testing and tighter limits on powerful agents, though its authority and relationship with existing agencies remain undefined. Nvidia chief Jensen Huang, whose company is one of the boom’s largest beneficiaries, put the chance of AI ending humanity at zero in a television interview. The debate is unfolding while ordinary security failures remain immediate: automated systems can scale weak-password attacks, and energy networks are still exposed through people, unpatched systems and poor operational controls. Any effective new body will need specific powers over evaluation, incident disclosure and critical infrastructure. A title and a taskforce cannot substitute for rules that survive pressure from the companies being examined. (Verge, Verge, Verge)

There is a second risk in the financing. Google, Amazon, Microsoft, Meta and Oracle are expected to issue about $132bn of debt this year to support datacentre construction. Demand for chips and power keeps the physical cost of AI high, while the price charged for processing keeps falling. A commercial index tracking the price of one million model tokens has more than halved since June to below $1. The business case therefore relies on huge revenue growth, even as providers cut fees to retain customers. Analysts have also identified roughly $1.5tn of commitments due when new computing capacity begins operation over the next two years. Regulation is urgent for public safety; it can also create a protective wall around today’s largest firms if compliance costs freeze out smaller competitors. Governments need to examine systemic debt and market concentration alongside model behaviour. The products, datacentres, power contracts and bonds belong to the same industrial project, and weakness in one layer can travel through the others. (Guardian)

Apple’s new intelligence reaches users before the rough edges are gone

Siri AI is reaching iOS 27 users through a waitlist with a beta label and an awkward mixture of powerful new abilities and regressions in familiar tasks. It can search messages and notes for personal context, interpret what is on screen and answer broader questions. Users are also encountering network errors without a useful diagnosis, failures to find alarms, reminders and HomeKit accessories, and requests that unexpectedly require an unlocked phone. Much of the processing runs through Apple’s Private Cloud Compute, which makes basic commands slower on weak connections. The delay is particularly visible on Apple Watch, where starting a timer can take several seconds. Some health queries and the old direct instruction to use ChatGPT are unavailable. The gap that matters is reliability: an assistant used while driving, wearing headphones or cooking has to complete small repeated tasks every time before its more ambitious intelligence feels like progress. (9to5Mac)

Inside the iPhone 18 Pro, iFixit found a similarly mixed picture. The new variable camera aperture uses six tiny blades moved by electromagnets, and damage would probably require replacement of the full camera assembly. Face ID’s infrared camera now sits beneath the display, hidden by removing alternating pixel rows above the sensor. A much larger vapour chamber cools the A20 Pro chip, and the battery retains a glue-free tray. The concern is the plastic frame around the screen, which cracked on three of the four phones iFixit opened. A damaged frame may prevent a replacement display from sitting flush and weaken water resistance, though other teardowns have not reproduced the problem. The phones received a provisional repairability score of seven out of ten. That score may change as parts arrive and more devices are opened; for now, the design shows how camera and cooling advances can coexist with a new vulnerability at the point where most major repairs begin. (9to5Mac)

Christopher Kane gives Mulberry its appetite back

Christopher Kane’s first Mulberry collection brought two recovering British fashion names together at London fashion week. Kane placed his label in administration in 2023; Mulberry endured collapsing profits and an unwanted takeover approach after price rises drove customers away. A new chief executive has returned the bag maker to British craft and lifted first-quarter sales by 23%. Kane’s appointment as creative director adds leather mini-dresses, sharply cut coats, loafers carrying the brand’s postman lock and heels shaped with miniature axes. He turned the scalloped flaps of the Bayswater bag into notched necklines and hems, using greys and coalminer orange drawn from the post-industrial Scotland of his childhood. The commercial logic is as important as the runway spectacle: Mulberry supplies factories, heritage and bags that sell, while Kane gains a stable platform for the mischievous design language that made his independent label matter. (Guardian)

Sports & Gaming

City go top through chaos, while Isak restores Liverpool

Manchester City lead the Premier League after a 5-3 win over Sunderland gave them 15 points from five matches, three ahead of Arsenal. Enzo Fernández and Rayan Cherki put City in front twice during the first half; Brian Brobbey answered both times before Antoine Semenyo made it 3-2. Semenyo scored again after the interval, Brobbey completed his hat-trick, and Erling Haaland ended the contest late. Brobbey became the first Premier League player since Roque Santa Cruz in 2007 to score three and finish on the losing side. City created enough to justify five goals and also conceded as many in one afternoon as they had across the previous run of clean sheets. Enzo Maresca played down the table after five rounds. The new leaders take a perfect league record into the international break, with Arsenal now forced to respond. (Guardian)

Liverpool recovered with a 1-0 victory at Bournemouth on Andoni Iraola’s return to the club he left in the summer. Cody Gakpo created the decisive opening near the hour, beating Adrien Truffert and rolling the ball across the six-yard area for Alexander Isak. It was Isak’s fourth goal of the season, and Gakpo has supplied all four. Bournemouth produced the first half’s best chance when Alisson stopped Evanilson at his near post, but another strong performance yielded no points for Marco Rose’s winless side. Liverpool remain unbeaten through seven matches in all competitions. The control was useful after recent turbulence, even if the performance lacked the pressure Iraola eventually wants. A narrow away win built on one clean move and a clean sheet gives him a better base than another afternoon of attacking drama. (Guardian)

Kojima changes platforms and keeps the argument alive

Kojima Productions is disputing suggestions that the studio is in trouble after Sony dropped Physint and the project moved to Xbox. Hideo Kojima says Sony’s decision followed disagreements over schedule, budget and the shape of the game, while the independent studio remains at work. The move is striking because Physint was announced as Kojima’s return to the espionage genre with PlayStation support, connecting it directly to expectations built around Metal Gear Solid. Development changes of this scale routinely produce rumours because publishers disclose little while teams and contracts are being rearranged. The useful test will be what survives the transition: the core design, the staff attached to it and a credible production timetable. Platform allegiance supplies the immediate argument; a finished game will settle whether the change gave the project room to work. (IGN, Verge)
