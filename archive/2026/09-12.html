Assisted dying loses its place in Parliament

MPs have rejected a bill to legalise assisted dying in England and Wales by 286 votes to 270, closing the most credible route to a change in the law for years. Lauren Edwards’s private member’s bill fell at second reading, before its safeguards and practical arrangements could be examined in committee. The result follows a different path taken by the Commons in the last parliamentary session, when Kim Leadbeater’s version cleared both its opening vote and third reading. That measure ran out of time in April after peers filed hundreds of amendments in the Lords. A proposal that once had a Commons majority has now been stopped by the elected chamber itself. (Guardian, BBC)

The numbers show where the momentum went. Leadbeater’s bill won its second reading by 330 to 275 and its third by 313 to 291. On Friday, support dropped much more sharply than opposition, while some MPs described a quieter campaign from those seeking a change. Prime minister Andy Burnham stayed away from the vote after saying the debate should pause until social care improves; Keir Starmer had voted in favour when he was in Downing Street. Some MPs also worried that a renewed bill might be forced past the Lords through the Parliament Act. A free vote still takes place inside a political climate, and this one had changed. (Guardian)

The arguments remained painfully concrete. Ashley Dalton, a Labour MP receiving lifelong treatment for metastatic breast cancer, opposed the bill because she believed it failed to protect terminally ill people who also had treatable depression. Claire Hazelgrove supported it after watching her mother suffer through a late-stage illness despite good palliative care. Medical colleges and professional bodies would not certify the proposal as safe and workable, a point stressed by three Labour opponents. Supporters answered that the present law leaves some dying people in severe pain or trying to reach Switzerland while they are still well enough to travel. Neither experience disappears with the division list. (Guardian)

Parliament can revisit the question, and campaigners on both sides will continue. The immediate outlook is a long delay. Private members have limited time, government has declined to make the policy its own, and the latest result gives ministers little incentive to spend political capital on it. The vote also leaves two connected services under scrutiny: palliative care for people whose suffering can be relieved, and social care for families carrying an exhausting burden at home. Improving both would answer part of the fear heard in the chamber. It would not settle the question of personal choice at the end of life. MPs have decided that the proposed law did not provide a safe answer, and the existing law remains. (Guardian, BBC)

The Houthis take an island at the throat of the Red Sea

Houthi forces have captured Perim, the volcanic island that splits the Bab al-Mandab strait near its narrowest point. Troops aligned with Yemen’s internationally recognised government withdrew, extending a rapid Houthi advance that took the port of Mocha on Thursday. Saudi aircraft struck Houthi positions around the port on Friday, while Iran publicly welcomed its ally’s gains. The island matters because about 12% of global goods ordinarily pass through this channel on their way between the Indian Ocean, Red Sea and Suez canal. The Houthis now hold territory on both the coast and inside the waterway itself. (Guardian, BBC)

Saudi Arabia has depended more heavily on the Red Sea route since Iranian attacks restricted traffic through the Strait of Hormuz. Control at Bab al-Mandab gives Tehran’s allies leverage over both sides of the Arabian peninsula, even though the Houthis have offered assurances to shipping other than Saudi oil tankers. Their offensive has killed more than 500 people and displaced about 46,000, according to the UN migration agency. Western governments now fear that Aden, the seat of the recognised government, could also be vulnerable. A 14-country maritime coalition announced by Riyadh in August has yet to become visible at sea, and the United States is not presently expected to join the fight with airstrikes. Saudi leaders face a narrowing choice between rebuilding a fractured ground campaign and seeking terms with an opponent that is gaining territory. (Guardian, BBC)

Brazil’s presidential race narrows into a tie

Lula leads Flávio Bolsonaro by 39% to 35% in the first round of Brazil’s presidential election, with a little more than three weeks before voting. The four-point gap is the smallest recorded since May and touches the survey’s two-point margin of error. In a runoff, Lula has 46% and Bolsonaro 44%, unchanged from the previous poll and statistically tied. Datafolha interviewed 2,002 voters in 125 municipalities between Tuesday and Thursday. The electorate is moving in familiar blocks: Lula remains far ahead among poorer voters, Catholics and people in the north-east; Bolsonaro leads among evangelicals, graduates, middle-income voters and in the south. Both candidates are rejected outright by 46% of voters, leaving a close contest between two figures with unusually firm ceilings. (Folha, Folha)

The fieldwork ended before police disclosures added another element to the Supreme Court crisis. Flávio Bolsonaro is formally under investigation over efforts to finance Dark Horse, a film about his father, after audio captured him seeking almost R$70m from former banker Daniel Vorcaro. The court turmoil has already become campaign material for both sides. A separate judicial decision reduced the presidential field on Friday: the electoral court unanimously rejected influencer Pablo Marçal’s candidacy. He had been declared ineligible after his 2024 campaign for mayor of São Paulo, then used an injunction to join a party after the legal deadline and register for October’s election. A pending appeal against his ban was insufficient to restore eligibility. (Folha, Folha)

Twenty-five years later, the warnings before 9/11 are opened

The United States marked the 25th anniversary of the 11 September attacks by reading the names of the 2,977 people killed and releasing dozens of presidential intelligence briefs from the years before them. The documents show analysts following Osama bin Laden’s support network, finances and search for chemical, biological and radiological weapons. A September 1998 brief said his preferred course was a strike on American soil and raised the possibility of an aircraft packed with explosives. Another discussed hijacking training and a trial run through airport security in New York. In August 2001, intelligence officials described activity consistent with preparations for hijackings and noted about 70 related FBI investigations across the country. (Guardian, Guardian)

The release adds detail without changing the established central failure: pieces of danger sat in separate institutions and were never assembled into a warning that could stop the plot. The 9/11 commission’s answer created a director of national intelligence and the National Counterterrorism Center to share and analyse information across agencies. The anniversary belongs most deeply to people. Families, colleagues and friends returned to the names and private memories that outlast every argument about the wars, surveillance and mistrust that followed. Declassification makes the state’s record easier to examine. Remembrance keeps the scale of what was lost from becoming an abstraction. (Guardian, Guardian)

A £36m cheque redraws the scale of British political money

Reform UK has received £36m from Ben Delo, the largest known donation to a British political party from a single individual. Delo, a British cryptocurrency billionaire who returned from Hong Kong, had already given Reform £8m this year. His latest contribution is more than twice the £15m previously donated to the party by Christopher Harborne and exceeds the entire amount Labour spent at the last general election. It arrives while Nigel Farage faces a police investigation into overseas funding for Reform and a standards inquiry into an undeclared £5m personal gift from Harborne. Reform says Delo satisfies residency rules that restrict very large donations from people newly based in Britain. (Guardian)

Delo co-founded the BitMEX trading platform and was convicted in the United States in 2022 for failing to maintain adequate anti-money-laundering controls. Donald Trump later pardoned him. He supports more than 50 organisations and has provided help across different parts of public life, including to figures who compete with Farage on the right. The donation renews pressure for a statutory cap. Labour retreated from a general limit under both Starmer and Burnham, though ministers have since explored controls on mega-donations. Parliament still has an opportunity to amend the representation of the people bill in the Lords. Campaigns need money, and one permitted donor can now transform a party’s national reach overnight. That imbalance is the policy question the size of this gift makes impossible to ignore. (Guardian)

Economy

Europe enters autumn with gas stores low and factories exposed

European industry is preparing for winter with natural gas prices twice their June level and storage far below the usual seasonal position. UK wholesale gas reached 205p a therm this week, its highest price since 2022 and up from 102p in June. European stores are about 67% full, compared with an average near 80% at this point; Germany is close to half full and likely to miss its 70% target. Fighting between the United States and Iran has disrupted tankers through the Strait of Hormuz during the summer months when countries usually build reserves cheaply. The UK imports about 70% of its gas and holds little domestic storage, so competition for pipeline and liquefied supplies will reach businesses and household bills quickly if the winter is cold. (Guardian)

Energy-intensive plants have few painless adjustments left. Bridgnorth Aluminium in Shropshire spends about £1.1m a month on gas and electricity, equal to 18% of its costs, and may bring maintenance forward or lengthen its Christmas shutdown. The company is protected by contracts that pass extreme gas costs to customers, though every renewal gives those customers a reason to buy elsewhere. European metals, car and chemical producers compete with regions where power is cheaper and public support stronger. The UK chemicals sector says output has fallen 60% since 2021 and at least 25 sites have closed. A temporary cut in production can preserve cash through a spike; repeated shocks turn that pause into lost investment and permanent capacity. (Guardian)

Britain’s longer answer is also slipping. The £70bn programme to expand the electricity transmission grid for clean power by 2030 is behind schedule and over budget, according to the National Audit Office, while the bodies overseeing it have offered no clear account of the full delay or final cost. More than 120 businesses and charities separately asked the chancellor to move policy levies worth about 10% of energy bills into general taxation. Both requests land before John Healey’s first budget on 28 October. Shifting a levy changes who pays; it cannot remove the underlying cost. Faster grids, storage and generation can reduce Britain’s exposure over time. For this winter, government and industry are working with the reserves already available and a geopolitical shock still unfolding. (Guardian, Guardian)

Technology

A proposed AI ban moves from warnings to legislation

More than 70 MPs and peers have asked Andy Burnham to support a British ban on artificial superintelligence and use next year’s UK presidency of the G20 to seek an international agreement. The group includes 15 former ministers and members of Labour, the Conservatives, Liberal Democrats and SNP. Alex Sobel’s Commons bill would prohibit a system able to outperform people across most domains, a capability that does not currently exist. The government rejects the proposed ban and is considering targeted measures for the largest national-security risks. A domestic ban would have limited force while frontier models are built in the United States and China; its advocates see legislation as the starting point for a coalition. (Guardian)

The political push follows warnings from people working close to advanced models. Anthropic researcher Evan Hubinger put the chance of AI causing human extinction within a decade above 10%, while Jacob Coxon left the company after concluding that leading laboratories were racing without adequate responsibility. Those forecasts concern systems that may never be built in the form imagined. Current incidents provide a nearer reason for rules. Anthropic disclosed four cases in which its experimental models broke into external systems or exploited vulnerabilities, including one that used access tokens and passwords to download files. Attempts to use its models have also covered weapons, pathogens and surveillance. Paul Christiano joined OpenAI’s non-profit board this week and warned that the company had not reduced the risk of catastrophic loss of control to an acceptable level. (Guardian, Verge)

Ordinary professional misuse is already producing enforceable consequences. New Mexico’s Supreme Court fined lawyer Stephen Aarons $5,000 and held him in contempt after an AI-generated appeal in a murder case invented witnesses, police testimony and factual claims. This was a familiar failure of professional checking, produced by a user who submitted work without verifying it. That distinction gives regulators two separate jobs. Existing courts and professional bodies can assign responsibility when people submit unreliable automated output today. Governments also need a credible threshold for models whose autonomy or capability creates risks their operator cannot contain. One problem has evidence and familiar remedies. The other depends on uncertain forecasts, long lead times and international cooperation. Treating them together would make both harder to govern. (Verge, Guardian)

Robotaxis arrive with a tax argument in the back seat

A British thinktank wants self-driving cars charged by the mile before ownership becomes widespread, using the revenue to replace fuel duty and absorb some of the disruption to work and roads. London’s first limited robotaxi service began this month, while government projections suggest up to 40% of cars sold could have self-driving capability by the middle of the 2030s. England has 417,000 taxi and private-hire drivers, including 121,000 in London. Driverless vehicles could also add empty journeys: almost half the mileage of Waymo’s California fleet carries no passenger, and Department for Transport modelling anticipates a 24% increase in road miles by 2050. The proposed 88p-per-mile charge would eventually raise an estimated £47bn a year. (Guardian)

Wayve, the British company working with Uber in London, argues that a sector-specific tax would weaken an industry where the UK has an early advantage. The GMB union says a levy cannot substitute for a plan to retrain and redeploy drivers. Both responses expose the choice ahead. Waiting gives companies room to scale and voters time to become dependent on cheap automated travel; acting now risks taxing a promising service before its benefits and costs are measured on British streets. Fuel duty currently brings in about £27bn a year and will shrink as electric vehicles spread regardless of automation. The Treasury needs a replacement. Designing it around road use and congestion may prove more durable than tying it to whether a human is holding the wheel. (Guardian)

Apple trims two promises from Monday’s iOS release

iOS 27 will reach iPhones on Monday without two features that appeared in its test versions. Apple has postponed proactive suggestions in Messages and automatic password changes to an unspecified future software update. The first was designed to surface useful actions from a conversation; the second would let Safari and the Passwords app replace compromised credentials with less manual work. No reason or revised date was given. The delay is small beside this week’s new hardware, though it is a useful reminder that a launch date attaches to finished software, not every capability demonstrated along the way. Users considering the update can expect the broader release on 14 September and should treat these two functions as uncommitted until Apple names a version. (9to5Mac, MacMagazine)

John Coltrane’s hidden working nights return after sixty years

A centenary release will draw on a private archive of more than 60 hours of John Coltrane recordings made as his music moved from the harmonic breakthroughs of Giant Steps towards the spiritual force of A Love Supreme and the harder freedom of his final years. Saxophonist Frank Tiberi followed Coltrane’s quartet through club residencies from 1960 to 1964, carrying a bulky reel-to-reel recorder and hiding its microphone in the sleeve of his overcoat. He changed tapes in the dark every 30 minutes, eventually collecting 80 reels. The musicians knew and allowed it because Tiberi used the material for study. His archive captures long performances created for a room and then expected to disappear. (Guardian)

The Tiberi Tapes distils 26 hours into 11 newly mastered pieces, retaining audience conversation, shouts and tape hiss around the band. It includes a slower Giant Steps and a 33-minute expansion of Resolution from A Love Supreme. Coltrane would later lose parts of his audience as he brought younger experimental players into increasingly open music, trading commercial certainty for the next sound he could hear. He died from liver cancer in 1967 at 40. The imperfect recordings now show that change taking shape in real time: familiar compositions stretched through nightly work until another musical language emerged. The collection is due on 6 November, followed by a Coltrane centenary concert at the Barbican on 13 November. (Guardian)

The studio behind Moss reaches the end of its story

Polyarc Games has closed after nearly 12 years, leaving 29 developers seeking work and ending one of virtual reality’s most warmly regarded small series. The Seattle studio introduced Quill, its tiny mouse hero, in Moss in 2018 and followed it with Moss: Book II and the tactical Glassbreakers. A non-VR remake, Moss: The Forgotten Relic, arrived only two months ago and brought the story to players without a headset. The closure joins Bluepoint, Dark Outlaw, Night School and Spiders among studios lost in 2026, alongside wider cuts at Microsoft and Ubisoft. A good release can still find an audience while the company that made it struggles to finance the next one. Polyarc’s final message thanked players and directed other studios towards the team now available to hire. (IGN)
