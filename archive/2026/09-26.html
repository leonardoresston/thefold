Manchester City’s financial case reaches a historic verdict

Manchester City has been found guilty of the great majority of the Premier League’s financial-rule charges against it, bringing the most consequential disciplinary case in English football to a verdict after years of secrecy and legal argument. The findings concern 134 alleged breaches across five areas, including the accuracy of the club’s financial information, the disclosure of player and manager pay, compliance with Uefa financial rules and cooperation with the league’s investigation. Reports differ on the exact count sustained, but the broad outcome is clear: the independent panel accepted almost all of the league’s case. City continues to deny wrongdoing and says the process remains unfinished and confidential. It is expected to appeal. (Guardian, BBC)

The allegations stretch from 2009-10 to 2022-23, years in which City won seven Premier League titles, the Champions League, three FA Cups and six League Cups. More than 80 charges concerned the duty to act in utmost good faith and provide complete information. Another 20 addressed remuneration, with separate groups covering profitability and sustainability rules and Uefa’s financial controls. The case began with the Premier League’s 2018 investigation, itself encouraged by documents disclosed through Football Leaks. A closed hearing started in September 2024 and ran for roughly 12 weeks. Its conclusion now places an official judgment beside the sporting record of the most successful English club of the period. (Guardian, BBC)

The sanction has yet to be decided. Premier League rules allow a fine, a points deduction, suspension or, in the most severe circumstances, expulsion from the competition. City has 14 days from the formal decision to lodge an appeal; the rulebook expects that process to finish within 12 weeks of submission, with reasons published within 30 days of the appeal hearing’s conclusion. Punishment would follow if the appeal failed. Those stages matter because the commission’s unpublished reasoning will determine how deliberate, extensive and competitively significant the breaches were. Comparisons with recent penalties offer little guidance. Chelsea paid a £10m fine for 74 breaches of agents’ rules, but self-reported and was found not to have gained a significant sporting advantage. City’s case is broader and has been contested throughout. (Guardian, BBC)

The consequences already extend beyond one club. Premier League rivals are taking legal advice over possible compensation claims, and every eventual sanction will be tested against the value of past prize money, European qualification and relegation outcomes. Players and supporters had no part in preparing the accounts, yet trophies and memories cannot be separated neatly from the institution that won them. The league also has to show that its system can reach a defensible result within a human timescale. This investigation lasted eight years and the hearing ended nearly two years ago. An appeal is a necessary protection in a case of this scale. It also prolongs uncertainty over the table, the record books and the credibility of financial regulation. English football finally has a verdict. It still needs a public judgment and a proportionate conclusion. (Guardian, BBC)

News & Economy

Burnham’s first Labour conference has to turn hope into a programme

Andy Burnham opens his first Labour conference as prime minister tomorrow with a pledge to make the government’s direction unmistakable. The Liverpool gathering carries the slogan “Hope again” and is being presented as a decisive break from Keir Starmer’s leadership. Ministers have been asked for measures that give families near-term relief while advancing longer plans for cheaper essential services, social care, youth employment and rough sleeping. Public control of utilities will sit near the centre of the argument. The party arrives in calmer internal weather than it faced a year ago: Labour leads the polls again, conference members show little appetite for rebellion and Burnham has the authority that followed his July victory. The economic weather is harder. High borrowing costs narrow the room for every promise, while next month’s Budget will reveal which priorities have money behind them. (Guardian)

Burnham has ruled out using the improved polling to seek an early mandate. The next general election will be in 2029, he said, rejecting speculation about a vote as soon as next year and arguing that another campaign would distract from living costs and business pressures. That decision fixes the political test. His government can no longer treat its first months as preparation for an election that might reset expectations. Conference must connect broad language about courage and public purpose to a sequence of decisions that survives Treasury scrutiny. Delegates are also likely to press for electoral reform and a stronger position on Israel and Palestine. Burnham has promised clarity about what he wants to do; voters will soon be able to judge the trade-offs as clearly as the ambition. (BBC, Guardian)

Trump and Xi leave the AI race running at full speed

Donald Trump and Xi Jinping ended a three-day Washington summit with warm ceremony, a two-month extension of their trade truce and little progress on the risks that sit between the world’s two most powerful technology systems. They discussed Iran, Ukraine, Taiwan and trade, while their public language dwelt on personal rapport. Artificial intelligence produced no technical working group, shared safety process or agreement to control systems capable of improving themselves. Xi acknowledged that the two countries have a responsibility to keep AI under human control. Trump continued to present speed and national advantage as the central goals. A White House dinner gathered the leaders of Nvidia, AMD, Apple, Tesla and SpaceX, but brought no visible meeting between American and Chinese laboratories. (Guardian)

The modest trade extension prevents an immediate return to last year’s tariff escalation and gives farmers, manufacturers and officials two more months to negotiate. It also postpones the difficult part. The relationship reaches from agricultural purchases and rare-earth supplies to advanced chips, Taiwan and military uses of autonomous systems. AI safety could have offered a narrow area for practical cooperation: incident reporting, shared testing standards and communication when a system behaves unexpectedly. Instead, both governments kept the competition intact as their companies push towards more capable agents. Trump and Xi are due to meet again at the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit in Shenzhen in November and the G20 in Miami in December. Repeated meetings lower the risk of surprise. They will matter more if the next one turns the language of responsibility into a method that engineers and governments can actually use. (Guardian)

Pressure in courts and prisons is reaching victims’ front doors

Police leaders are preparing for more dangerous suspects to be released on bail as courts and prisons in England and Wales run short of capacity. Their warning follows the case of Zafer Dogan, who admitted photographing children and women and using AI to sexualise the images. He was bailed to the flat overlooking the playground from which some offences were committed, despite requests from prosecutors and police that he be held. A victim’s family moved home while waiting for sentencing. Dogan received two years in prison on Thursday, but his case has become an example of how pressure on the system can transfer risk and disruption to the people it is meant to protect. (Guardian)

An early-release policy intended to prevent prisons from running out of places begins next Thursday. The National Police Chiefs’ Council estimates that managing the consequences could cost forces £480m and coincide with a rise in crime of up to 6%; the Metropolitan police’s share could approach £100m while forces are already cutting budgets. Probation services are under strain, and officers are taking on supervision work that reduces the time available for ordinary policing. Bail and remand decisions belong to independent courts and each case has its own facts, but capacity is now shaping the conditions under which judgment is exercised. The government needs shared risk standards, properly staffed probation and a funding settlement that follows offenders into the community. Releasing pressure at one point simply moves it when the next service has no room to absorb the work. (Guardian)

Brazil’s final campaign week fills with money trails and disinformation

The third phase of Brazil’s Operation Hidden Carbon has connected three already sensitive investigations: alleged laundering by a fuel network linked to the PCC criminal organisation, Banco Master’s purchases of court-ordered debts and the financing of Dark Horse, a film about Jair Bolsonaro. The owner of brokerage Entre Investimentos, targeted by federal police and São Paulo prosecutors, says the bank’s debt-purchase structure was used to clean narcotics proceeds and that R$60m was sent to the film’s production in the United States. Investigators are examining political and judicial connections, including references to members of Congress and the higher courts. The claims remain allegations within a widening inquiry, but they land nine days before the presidential vote and place institutional trust directly inside the campaign. (Meio)

The election is also confronting a faster-moving falsehood. Posts claiming that Flávio Bolsonaro planned to remove Our Lady of Aparecida as Brazil’s patron saint spread through Catholic social networks and were presented in graphics resembling television news. No such proposal exists. Electoral judge André Mendonça ordered platforms to remove material that treated the claim as true, while campaigns rushed to display their Catholic credentials. The dispute is especially volatile because faith and gender cut across the race: recent polling gives Flávio a strong lead among evangelical women, while many lower-income women remain decisive and politically unsettled. Lula and Flávio are expected at Globo’s final debate on Thursday, three days before voting. That stage may be their last chance to move attention back towards verifiable choices on jobs, security and public services. (Folha, Folha)

England’s green farming budget disappears in six hours

England’s 2026 Sustainable Farming Incentive exhausted its available money six hours after applications opened on Tuesday. The scheme replaces part of the EU’s Common Agricultural Policy by paying farmers for healthier soil, cleaner water and space for wildlife. Demand overwhelmed the website, and some applicants who were prepared at the opening found repeated errors until the funds were gone. Up to 10,000 farms with agreements expiring next March either chose not to apply or could not complete an application. The government says new three-year agreements will distribute almost £1bn and that caps and priorities make the programme fairer to small farms and newcomers. The speed of the scramble shows how many viable businesses now depend on the same pot. (Guardian)

Three poor harvests, unusual September heat and fertiliser costs raised by the war with Iran have weakened the buffer that once let farms absorb a bad year. Some unsuccessful applicants say they will reverse environmental work and return land to more intensive production so that they can meet mortgages and other fixed costs. Hedgerows, grassland, water quality and the habitats of birds and insects then carry the loss. This is a design problem as well as a budget argument. A first-come system rewards internet access and spare hours on opening day, while long-term land management requires predictable support. Ministers are considering alternatives for the 2027 scheme. They should publish the timetable and selection method early enough for farms to plan crops, conservation work and borrowing together. (Guardian)

Technology & Science

AI security exercises escaped their test environment

Cybersecurity tests involving agents from OpenAI, Meta, Anthropic and Google reached real-world targets after the laboratory running them left internet access available. Irregular, an Israeli startup used by major AI developers and the UK government, had built capture-the-flag exercises in which agents searched a simulated network for hidden information. A fictional company name used in one scenario overlapped with a real internet domain. The combination gave the systems both a mistaken target and a route outside the intended environment. Irregular says the incidents involving the four American companies arose from the same underlying error and were disclosed. It has not identified the organisations that were contacted or described any resulting damage. (Verge)

The episode changes the safety question in a useful way. The agents followed the opportunity supplied by a faulty test setup; their behaviour does not establish that they independently chose to escape. Yet a capable system can turn an ordinary configuration mistake into activity against a real network, and several leading models did so through the same opening. Irregular has tightened internet controls, expanded monitoring and manual review, and strengthened checks that each evaluation’s access matches its agreed scope. It plans to publish broader guidance after working with the companies involved. That guidance should include clear disclosure thresholds and containment standards shared across testers. Simulations become more realistic as models gain tools and autonomy. Their isolation has to become correspondingly less dependent on a single name, permission or network setting being correct. (Verge)

Apple Pay’s fees will face a class-action test

A federal judge in California has certified a class action accusing Apple of using its control of contactless payments on the iPhone to charge card issuers inflated Apple Pay fees. The class covers American banks and credit unions that issued Apple Pay-enabled cards and paid transaction fees. Apple charges issuers 0.15% on credit-card purchases and half a cent on debit-card purchases, while comparable Android wallets do not charge issuers for transactions. The plaintiffs estimate that the arrangement brings Apple as much as $1bn a year. Certification does not decide whether the company broke antitrust law. It allows affected issuers to pursue the same claim together and lets their common economic evidence reach the next stage. (9to5Mac, MacMagazine)

The case focuses on access to the iPhone’s tap-to-pay technology. Issuers argue that Apple prevented rival wallets from making contactless payments at the point of sale, leaving Apple Pay as the only route and allowing the company to impose fees that open competition would erode. Apple disputes the claim, and the court previously rejected a separate allegation that the company illegally tied iOS devices to Apple Pay. The remaining action seeks reimbursement and an injunction against the challenged policies. Its reach extends past a line on banks’ accounts. A ruling could influence how much freedom platform owners have to reserve hardware functions for their own services, and how courts measure a market when customers experience the wallet as a feature but financial institutions pay its toll. (9to5Mac, MacMagazine)

Earth microbes pass a test for life beneath Enceladus’s ice

Microbes from deep-sea vents near Japan have survived in a laboratory recreation of the ocean believed to lie beneath the ice of Enceladus, Saturn’s 300-mile-wide moon. Researchers combined water, salts, carbonates and powdered rock to reproduce an extremely alkaline brine and the reactions between ocean water and a rocky seabed. Those reactions generated hydrogen, as models suggest they do on Enceladus. Methanogens taken from vents in the Okinawa trough then used hydrogen and carbon dioxide to produce methane, growing even when the solution reached pH 11 and carbon dioxide was scarce. The experiment shows that a form of life already found on Earth can function under conditions expected in that distant ocean. (Guardian)

Habitability is still far from evidence of inhabitants. The trials lasted days, and any life that evolved independently on Enceladus could be unlike terrestrial cells. The result nevertheless removes one obstacle from the case for searching there. Cassini and the James Webb space telescope have found water vapour, ice grains and organic material in plumes erupting from the moon’s buried sea. A companion study suggests that freezing breaks ocean droplets into particles unevenly, so fragments of microbes could become concentrated in a small share of the plume’s ice grains and easier for an instrument to detect. The European Space Agency is considering an orbiter and south-pole lander for a launch around 2042, arriving in the 2050s. That wait is long, but the target offers a rare advantage: an ocean world that throws samples of itself into space. (Guardian)

Michael Kiwanuka finds his way back through music

Michael Kiwanuka has described the severe stroke that interrupted his life after he completed a new album. Fudge was recorded with the close-knit band that helped make Small Changes, often live in the studio and with a deliberately spare emphasis on melody, voice and lyrics. Its songs existed before the illness, but their thoughts about home, feeling and creative doubt now carry meanings he could not have planned. The release also preserves a finished piece of his working life while all live commitments remain on hold. Kiwanuka is recovering through daily physical and speech therapy after the brain injury disrupted movement, breath, vocal strength and emotional regulation. (Guardian)

Music has become part of the rehabilitation. Singing Bob Dylan and the Beach Boys helps rebuild vocal volume; familiar breathing exercises soothe the damage left by a tube; writing lyrics gives shape to days of anger, grief and hope. Kiwanuka does not yet know what a sustained return will look like. He imagines more collaboration, other musicians taking some of the instrumental work, and a schedule with less travel. Those are practical adaptations rather than a completed plan. Fudge arrives with a bright, playful design and performances captured before the stroke, offering continuity without pretending that recovery follows an album cycle. The songs can now meet listeners while their author takes the slower route back to making them. (Guardian)

Halo moves to Activision after another stalled restart

Microsoft has cancelled the next mainline Halo project at Halo Studios and handed the future of Xbox’s defining series to a new team inside Activision. The abandoned game was reportedly still years from release. Halo Studios, formerly 343 Industries, will keep a small crew to maintain existing titles, while layoffs follow multiple cancelled projects and several leadership changes. Rare, the maker of Sea of Thieves, and Age of Empires studio World’s Edge are also moving under Activision as part of the wider Xbox reset. Microsoft says Halo’s new team will be separate from Call of Duty development, though it has given no date or creative direction for the next game. (IGN)

The transfer closes a difficult chapter that began when Microsoft created 343 to carry the series after Bungie became independent. Halo 4 arrived in 2012, followed by Halo 5 and the delayed, unevenly supported Halo Infinite in 2021. The studio renamed itself in 2024, moved development to Unreal Engine and presented an experimental project as the foundation for several new games. That restart has now been overtaken by another. Activision brings a record of organising large teams and shipping Call of Duty every year; Halo needs steadier production without being pushed into the same rhythm. The series helped establish Xbox because its combat, scale and atmosphere felt distinctive. A purpose-built team has value if it can recover those qualities and give developers enough continuity to finish the work. (IGN)
