News

France confronts the damage after a weekend of unrest

Emmanuel Macron called for calm on Sunday after protests across France descended into rioting, leaving about 2,400 people under arrest. Smaller outbreaks continued in Paris and Lyon as shopkeepers cleared broken glass and cities assessed damage to public buildings. The interior ministry put the number of police injured on Saturday at about 1,000, including seven seriously. (BBC)

Around 550,000 people had joined largely peaceful demonstrations over fuel prices, living costs and deteriorating public services. Student protests that began last month expanded as trade unionists and other groups joined them. Macron acknowledged the protesters’ legitimate demands while condemning the destruction. Local prefects and mayors are to draw up measures to protect shops and civic buildings. (BBC)

The gutted 19th-century town hall in Limoges became a stark image of the weekend. Macron also promised transparency over policing, after officers used tear gas, water cannon and batons. The response has intensified political divisions ahead of April’s presidential election: Jean-Luc Mélenchon attacked the authorities’ handling, while Marine Le Pen condemned political encouragement of violence. The immediate task is to restore safety while responding to the school and cost-of-living grievances that brought hundreds of thousands onto the streets. (BBC)

Ukraine regains US missile warnings as the energy war continues

Volodymyr Zelenskyy said American intelligence warnings about Russian ballistic missile launches were working again after a three-day interruption. He also accused Washington of deliberately slowing Ukraine’s Starlink connections, with SpaceX carrying out the restriction. US treasury secretary Scott Bessent rejected that accusation, saying Donald Trump and Marco Rubio had denied any administration role. The dispute concerns systems Ukraine uses for frontline drones, air defence and warning civilians of incoming attacks. Zelenskyy said he remained in close contact with Trump’s envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner as the diplomatic argument continued. (Guardian)

Russia and Ukraine continued attacking each other’s energy infrastructure after the collapse of Trump’s proposed energy ceasefire. A Russian strike killed a woman in Zaporizhzhia and injured at least two people, including a six-year-old girl. Ukrainian drones hit targets around St Petersburg; regional officials described fires and electricity cuts. Lithuania temporarily urged residents to shelter and closed Kaunas airport after an unidentified object entered its airspace. Ukraine apologised for the possible diversion of its drones and suggested Russian electronic interference could have been responsible. The incident adds another cross-border risk to a war already disrupting power supplies as winter approaches. (Guardian)

Brazil’s campaigns fill the streets before the final week

Supporters of Lula and Flávio Bolsonaro mobilised on Sunday, a week before Brazil’s presidential runoff on 25 October. In Rio, a musical gathering backing Lula stretched along roughly a kilometre of Copacabana. Caetano Veloso and Paula Lavigne had called the event, with performers including Djavan, João Bosco, Simone and Fernanda Abreu. Earlier, Chico Buarque joined a student demonstration near the Candelária church. The gatherings put some of Brazil’s best-known cultural figures visibly into the closing campaign. (Folha)

Flávio rallied supporters on São Paulo’s Avenida Paulista, where governor Tarcísio de Freitas and other allies joined him. His message centred on removing the Workers’ party from office and presented victory as a continuation of his father Jair Bolsonaro’s political project. Lula’s supporters also gathered in Brasília, Salvador, Curitiba and Porto Alegre, with student organisations helping drive the mobilisation. Flávio finished ahead in the first round; both sides now need to turn public enthusiasm into votes from people beyond their committed supporters. Street crowds offer a measure of mobilisation, while the result will depend on the electorate nationwide. (Folha, Folha)

A separate development placed Flávio’s finances under renewed scrutiny. Federal police sent the supreme court a request for an inquiry into suspected money laundering involving lawyer Willer Tomaz, who is also investigated in the INSS pension-fraud case. Alexandre de Moraes was assigned the matter. Flávio’s campaign disputed the description of him as being under investigation in this case, saying the papers had yet to become a formal inquiry and there had been no prosecutorial opinion or judicial decision. Police linked their request to financial dealings and an offshore company; Flávio has described his relationship with Tomaz as a friendship. The allegations remain unproven. (Folha)

There is also a practical issue for voters. Updated electoral-court figures on Sunday put changes of polling address at 1,397 voting sections, affecting 402,599 people. That replaced a higher figure circulated earlier in the day. Weather damage and urgent building work were among the reasons. Lula’s coalition asked for wider publicity and help reaching replacement locations, arguing that relying on websites and apps disadvantages older voters and people with poor internet access. Voters can check their assigned location through the court’s self-service portal or e-Título app. The changes represent 0.27% of voting sections nationwide. Individual electoral zones make those decisions according to the buildings available, rather than receiving a single centrally imposed list from the national court. (Folha)

Britain seeks closer access to European policing data

Shabana Mahmood is due to meet EU home affairs commissioner Magnus Brunner on Monday for talks covering migration, borders, law enforcement and counter-terrorism. It will be their second meeting in two months. Prime minister Andy Burnham has made restoring access to European information systems part of the discussion ahead of November’s EU–UK summit, following Britain’s loss of access to some databases after Brexit. (Guardian)

Two systems illustrate what is at stake. The Schengen Information System holds alerts about missing people, fugitives, stolen property and vehicles. Eurodac contains biometric information on asylum seekers, alongside security information. Access arrangements would affect the everyday work of police and immigration authorities, from checking a number plate to identifying someone across borders. Brunner is also scheduled to meet MI5 director general Ken McCallum and officials working on counter-terrorism and child sexual abuse. The Home Office has yet to comment on the talks. (Guardian)

Economy

Home insulation returns to the centre of the energy argument

The government’s £15bn warm homes plan would cut household energy bills by about £1bn a year and support roughly 50,000 jobs, according to analysis by E3G. The thinktank estimates an annual £1.6bn boost to the economy, with work spread among small businesses across the country. The plan, which covers insulation and heat pumps, faces pressure as ministers seek budget savings. Its projected benefits are concentrated partly among households on low incomes. (Guardian)

E3G compared those figures with roughly 1,100 direct and indirect jobs at the proposed Rosebank oilfield west of Shetland. The estimates depend on different projects, assumptions and timeframes, so they are projections rather than realised outcomes. The political choice comes as ministers consider Rosebank and Jackdaw, developments already in the licensing system when Labour promised to halt new licences. New North Sea production would enter international markets; improvements to homes reduce the energy those households need to buy. Energy businesses are pressing the government for stable policy so suppliers can plan investment and staffing. E3G also expects the homes programme to draw in £15bn of private investment; the energy department declined to comment on its findings. (Guardian)

Microsoft withdraws from an Essex AI datacentre deal

Microsoft has pulled out of an agreement to use Nscale’s planned AI datacentre in Loughton, Essex, amid concerns about power availability. The project had been promoted by ministers as a major piece of Britain’s AI infrastructure and was meant to open next year. UK Power Networks has told Nscale that sufficient grid supply may be unavailable until the early to mid-2030s. Microsoft was the development’s only publicly announced customer. The company is understood to remain involved in a separate Nscale project in Norway, making the Essex withdrawal a specific setback for the British development. (Guardian)

Nscale says it remains committed to Loughton and is investigating alternative power sources, including generation on site, as well as other UK locations. It says its wider relationship with Microsoft remains strong and other contracts are unaffected. The company has cleared the former scaffolding yard, but a cleared site still needs a dependable electricity supply before it can become working computing capacity. The delay exposes a practical constraint on the government’s AI ambitions: announced investment must be matched by grid connections, construction and customers able to use the finished facility. (Guardian)

Condé Nast’s UK profits fall as publishing costs rise

Pre-tax profits at Condé Nast’s UK operation fell 36% last year to nearly £18m, excluding exceptional items. UK revenue declined 7% to £70.6m, while growth in international revenue kept the business’s total close to £100m. Restructuring costs rose from £1.9m to £4.3m, mainly because of severance payments. Staff numbers changed relatively little, falling from 466 to 461 as hiring continued alongside departures. (Guardian)

The publisher attributes the profit change to planned investment in services for clients, with pension projections and wage inflation also affecting costs. The comparison follows an unusually strong 2024, when the sale of Vogue House produced a large exceptional gain. Separately, the Newhouse family has ruled out selling the wider group, whose titles include Vogue, GQ and Vanity Fair. Across the international group, seven major brands account for 85% of revenue, while advertising and other services provide a much larger share of income than newsstand sales and subscriptions. The accounts capture the pressure on a luxury publisher trying to maintain valuable brands while advertising, subscriptions and the routes by which readers find its work continue to change. (Guardian)

Technology

The World Bank makes the case for smaller AI

The World Bank wants developing countries to build specialised AI systems that work on phones, in local languages and with weak internet connections. Its chief knowledge officer, Paschal Donohoe, pointed to Indian farmers needing soil advice through the bank’s AgriConnect programme. A narrow model trained for a specific task could help where there are too few agricultural specialists to visit every small farm. Governments, businesses and the bank could collaborate on such tools. Donohoe’s argument is that development gains depend on adapting the technology to an identifiable local need, with a focused dataset and an accessible way to use the result. (Guardian)

The proposal brings basic infrastructure into the AI discussion. Almost a third of rural schools in sub-Saharan Africa lack electricity, and more than two-thirds lack a consistent internet connection, according to the bank’s development report. Useful applications therefore need to fit the conditions in which people live and work. The IMF has also warned about disruption to entry-level jobs as routine tasks become automated. For governments, adoption involves language, skills, power and access, alongside the purchase of software. (Guardian)

A shared police login opened a surveillance system to ICE

A police officer in Savannah, Georgia, was dismissed after an internal investigation found she had shared access to the Flock number-plate surveillance system with her husband, an Immigration and Customs Enforcement agent. Savannah police do not share their Flock data with ICE. Former officer Amber Harpe’s husband, Tyler, made 983 searches relating to 97 plates during the unauthorised access; the vehicles were overwhelmingly registered to Hispanic drivers. (The Verge)

The couple defended the searches as serving law-enforcement purposes. Senior officers rejected an initial recommendation for an eight-hour suspension and dismissed Harpe. She was one of six department employees fired after an investigation into misuse of Flock; four were later arrested, though she was not among those four. The case shows how a department’s restrictions on sharing surveillance information can fail when an authorised user hands over credentials. The resulting access bypassed the boundary the city had drawn around the data. (The Verge)

Cities demand more control over driverless cars

American cities are pressing for tighter control of robotaxis as federal legislation remains stalled. Minneapolis is considering requiring a human monitor inside autonomous vehicles, a condition that would prevent fully driverless operation. San Diego has sought restrictions on Waymo at the airport and more local authority during emergencies. In Austin, firefighters want rapid towing arrangements and direct contact with human operators when a vehicle blocks their work. (The Verge)

The disputes extend beyond whether a car can drive safely. Councils also have to manage charging depots, noise, congestion and the ability of emergency crews to move a stalled vehicle. Companies increasingly favour states that limit cities’ power to prohibit their services, while pursuing expansion overseas, including Britain. The different layers of authority leave operators facing a patchwork of requirements. Local street management and emergency response will remain central to public acceptance as more driverless vehicles share roads with buses, cyclists and pedestrians. Some California proposals would let cities limit fleet sizes or operating hours. Other disputes concern whether emergency services should have physical override access or a remote way to disable vehicles. (The Verge)

Games face a new test over ownership and copying

US congressman Ritchie Torres has introduced the Power To The Players Act, which would ask the Federal Trade Commission to establish protections for people buying video games. The proposal includes an offline mode before supporting servers are closed. Torres highlighted NBA 2K25, which is due to leave online storefronts at the end of October and lose server support on 31 December. The bill has been introduced; its proposed guarantees are not yet law. Server-dependent games can lose important functions when their publisher stops running the service, even after someone has paid for a copy. (The Verge)

Bloober Team, the studio behind the Silent Hill 2 remake, wants to keep producing physical editions for as long as it can. Chief executive Piotr Babieno acknowledged the lower costs and commercial attractions of digital distribution, while describing collecting as an important bond between players and studios. The company is working on a remake of the original Silent Hill, which has no release date. Physical editions remain part of its plans where production and platform support allow them. (IGN)

A smaller developer is confronting a different question about control. Alliance Arts and Who You cancelled today’s planned public Steam demo for Drunken Goddess Reflux because they fear AI-assisted copies could reach the market before the finished game. A limited closed play session will replace it, with participants agreeing to rules on posting and streaming. The developers also said they lack the resources to manage those restrictions for a public demo. The PC release remains scheduled for 8 December. The decision removes a usual route for prospective players to try a game before buying it. (IGN)

Sports

Madueke supplies Arsenal’s breakthrough at Forest

Arsenal beat Nottingham Forest 1–0 at the City Ground through Noni Madueke’s deflected 80th-minute strike. The half-time substitute took Declan Rice’s pass, cut inside and saw his shot divert off Jair Cunha beyond Matz Sels. It was Arsenal’s only effort on target in a match dominated by patient possession and Forest’s compact defending. The home side remain without a league goal at their own ground this season. (Guardian)

Mikel Arteta’s changes again proved decisive. Madueke replaced Eberechi Eze at the interval, and Jurriën Timber, Mikel Merino and Viktor Gyökeres followed in a triple substitution midway through the second half. Forest had late openings, but Rice and Ezri Konsa made important defensive interventions. Arsenal had arrived six points behind Manchester City and needed a result after losing their previous away league match at Brighton. (Guardian)

Madueke’s opportunity has widened with Christos Tzolis sidelined by a hamstring injury. He has now followed an encouraging start against Leeds and an assist against Lille with the winning goal at Forest. Arsenal’s substitutes have contributed to 26 Premier League goals since the start of last season, more than those of any other club. Arteta’s squad is finding ways through games in which the starting attack struggles to create clear chances. Madueke has started only one league match this season, so his recent contributions strengthen his case for more minutes. He can play on either wing, giving Arteta an option as injuries alter the front line. The manager says Arsenal have won 10 of their 11 competitive fixtures, with different players and approaches delivering results. (BBC)

Brighton score five; United’s wait goes on

Brighton overwhelmed Crystal Palace 5–0, with Diego Gómez scoring from near halfway. An early Jaydee Canvot own goal set the tone, before Gómez caught Walter Benítez out from 51 metres. Yasin Ayari, Charalampos Kostoulas and Jack Hinshelwood completed the scoring. Palace manager Pierre Sage apologised to supporters after another damaging defensive performance. Brighton’s win strengthened their promising start and gave Fabian Hürzeler a very different derby experience from the home defeat his side suffered in February. (Guardian)

Manchester United drew 1–1 at Leeds, leaving Michael Carrick’s team with seven points from seven league games and one win. Bryan Mbeumo put United ahead before Dominic Calvert-Lewin equalised after the break. Carrick took encouragement from his players’ intensity following a difficult week that included defeat at Atlético Madrid. Leeds manager Daniel Farke felt his side could have taken all three points. (Guardian)

Bournemouth secured their first league win under Marco Rose, beating Sunderland 2–0. Evanilson created Marcus Tavernier’s opener, drew the foul that brought Kevin Danso a 17th-minute red card and then scored himself. Sunderland have now lost four league games in succession as they juggle domestic football with European travel. Both clubs have had encouraging Europa League results, but Sunday brought Bournemouth some badly needed relief in the Premier League. (Guardian)
