News

France counts the cost after a weekend of street fury

French cities spent Sunday clearing shattered glass and assessing burnt-out civic buildings after one of the most destructive weekends of civil disorder in recent decades. President Emmanuel Macron appealed for calm as smaller clashes and looting persisted in parts of Paris and Lyon. Across the country, police made roughly 2,400 arrests over the weekend, while the interior ministry counted around 1,000 injured officers on Saturday alone, seven of whom required hospital treatment for serious wounds. (BBC)

The demonstrations began as a broad movement over living costs, underfunded public services and fuel prices, after petrol reached a record €2.40 a litre late last month. What started among secondary school pupils in September grew rapidly once trade unions, university students and former yellow-vest protesters joined the marches. Between 550,000 and 800,000 people turned out across France on Saturday. Most marched peacefully, carrying union banners and calling for public investment in schools and relief from inflation, before black-clad groups and rioters clashed with police lines. (BBC, Guardian)

In Limoges, fire tore through the ornate 19th-century town hall on Saturday night, bringing down its clock tower and roof and leaving residents staring at the blackened shell on Sunday morning. Widespread looting also hit commercial streets in Paris, Marseille and Lille. Macron instructed local prefects and mayors to draw up security plans to protect shops and municipal buildings, while acknowledging that many demonstrators had marched over legitimate economic grievances. (BBC)

Policing tactics have become a second flashpoint. Riot officers used teargas, stun grenades, water cannon and batons to clear squares, and anger over an earlier incident in Lens, where a police grenade severely injured a 15-year-old pupil’s hand, helped draw younger crowds on to the streets. Macron promised full transparency over the conduct of the security forces. With the April presidential election six months away, Marine Le Pen has sought to link the government’s fiscal policies to the breakdown in order, while Jean-Luc Mélenchon and the left have focused on police violence and the social pressures driving the protests. (BBC, Guardian)

Ukraine regains US missile warnings as grid strikes escalate

Volodymyr Zelenskyy confirmed on Sunday that the United States had restored intelligence sharing on Russian ballistic missile launches after a three-day pause that left Ukrainian cities without advance warning of incoming strikes. Even as that data resumed, Zelenskyy maintained his accusation that Washington had intentionally slowed Ukraine’s Starlink satellite internet connections through SpaceX to pressure Kyiv during diplomatic disagreements. US treasury secretary Scott Bessent denied any administration involvement, stating that Donald Trump and Marco Rubio had nothing to do with satellite speeds, while Zelenskyy noted that talks with American envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner were continuing. (Guardian)

The dispute unfolded alongside heavy reciprocal attacks on power and industrial infrastructure following the collapse of Trump’s proposed energy ceasefire. Russian strikes killed a woman in Zaporizhzhia and wounded at least two others, including a six-year-old girl. Overnight, Ukraine launched more than 1,000 drones against Russian oil depots, warehouses and datacentres. Fires broke out at an oil terminal and industrial sites in St Petersburg, where strikes also hit an Ozon logistics hub and a VK datacentre, while authorities in the surrounding Leningrad region reported 145 downed drones and power cuts affecting 20,000 residents. (Guardian)

Sunday’s barrages also sent an alert across Nato’s eastern border. Lithuania advised residents in central and northern areas to take shelter for roughly an hour and temporarily closed Kaunas airport after an unidentified object entered its airspace. Nato fighter jets scrambled to the area were unable to spot the object through thick cloud. Ukraine’s foreign ministry apologised for the possible diversion of its drones into Lithuanian skies, pointing to Russian electronic warfare as the likely cause and pledging to share flight data with Vilnius. (Guardian)

Footage counters Israeli doubts over West Bank diplomat assault

New video and eyewitness evidence from the Palestinian village of Taybeh has contradicted attempts by Benjamin Netanyahu’s office to cast doubt on Saturday’s settler attack against European diplomats and French visitors. After masked men armed with sticks assaulted the group during the olive harvest, knocking Bernard Baudoux, the mayor of Aulnoye-Aymeries, to the ground and smashing a reinforced window on a diplomatic vehicle, the Israeli prime minister’s office issued a statement questioning the sequence of events and warning of a campaign to smear Israel. Footage recorded moments before and during the confrontation shows the visitors retreating peacefully toward cars bearing diplomatic plates as masked settlers charged up the hill and targeted Baudoux while he wore his tricolour mayoral sash. (Guardian)

The assault came a day after American photojournalist Heidi Levine was hospitalised following an attack by Israeli militants at another West Bank olive harvest, an episode Netanyahu’s office had similarly described as disputed. On Sunday, more than a dozen former senior Israeli diplomats, including two former directors general of the foreign ministry and several former ambassadors, staged an unprecedented demonstration outside the ministry in Jerusalem. Holding banners addressed to Netanyahu and foreign minister Gideon Sa’ar, the retired officials demanded immediate state action to stop violent attacks against Palestinians and foreign representatives in the occupied territory. (Guardian)

Brazil’s rival camps fill the streets before the final week

One week before Brazil’s 25 October presidential runoff, supporters of Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva and Flávio Bolsonaro staged competing street demonstrations across major cities. Along Rio de Janeiro’s Copacabana beachfront, a musical rally convened by Caetano Veloso and producer Paula Lavigne drew crowds across roughly a kilometre of the avenue, with performances by Djavan, João Bosco, Simone, Marina Sena and Fernanda Abreu. Earlier in the day, Chico Buarque joined a student march organised by the National Union of Students near the Candelária church in central Rio, while pro-Lula demonstrations also took place in Brasília, Salvador, Maceió and Curitiba. (Folha, Folha)

In São Paulo, Flávio Bolsonaro gathered supporters across eight blocks of Avenida Paulista alongside state governor Tarcísio de Freitas. Researchers from the University of São Paulo and Cebrap estimated the peak crowd at 71,900 people. Flávio kept his remarks brief to preserve his voice for Sunday night’s televised confrontation on Band, the first head-to-head debate between the two candidates of the campaign. He promised supporters that his father, former president Jair Bolsonaro, who is serving a 27-year sentence for an attempted coup, would hand him the presidential sash if he defeats the Workers’ party next Sunday. (Folha, Folha)

Behind the street mobilisation lies a shifting electoral map. An analysis of first-round returns shows that Lula’s margin over the Bolsonaro ticket narrowed in 98% of municipalities across the north-east compared with 2022. While Lula still won 21.5 million votes across the nine north-eastern states, Flávio rose to 10.4 million, an 18.3% increase on his father’s total four years ago, driven by small and medium-sized interior towns linked to agribusiness and local mayors allied with centre-right parties. Separately, Brazil’s electoral court confirmed that 1,397 voting sections affecting 402,599 people have changed address because of storm damage or building works, prompting Lula’s coalition to ask for wider publicity and transport support. (Folha, Folha)

Flávio’s campaign also faced new legal questions after federal police asked the supreme court to open an inquiry into suspected money laundering involving lawyer Willer Tomaz, a target in the wider investigation into pension-deduction fraud at the National Social Security Institute. Police cited records showing that Flávio held master-user operational access to a bank account belonging to an offshore company, Alcazar Holding, in which Tomaz is a partner, until May 2025. Justice Alexandre de Moraes has been assigned the request; Flávio’s lawyers noted that no formal inquiry has been opened, and the senator maintains that his relationship with Tomaz is strictly personal. (Folha)

Whitehall presses for EU crime databases and winter NHS capacity

Home secretary Shabana Mahmood meets European home affairs commissioner Magnus Brunner on Monday for talks on border security, migration and counter-terrorism, as prime minister Andy Burnham seeks to reconnect Britain to European law-enforcement databases lost after Brexit. The discussions precede next month’s EU–UK summit and coincide with Frontex figures showing a 43% drop in small-boat crossings across the Channel. British officials are particularly eager to regain access to the Schengen Information System, which handles roughly 20 million daily checks on wanted fugitives, missing people and stolen vehicles, alongside the Eurodac biometric database used to track asylum claims and security alerts. Brunner will also hold talks with MI5 director general Ken McCallum during his London visit. (Guardian)

Domestically, health secretary Yvette Cooper announced £140m in capital funding for urgent care across England to prepare hospitals for a difficult winter. The money will fund 14 regional schemes, including expanded ambulance fleets, same-day emergency care and upgraded A&E departments, after summer heatwaves drove a record 7.3 million emergency attendances between June and August and early autumn flu admissions climbed sharply. The Royal College of Nursing welcomed the immediate relief while warning that extra rooms and vehicles cannot compensate for chronic nursing shortages ahead of the budget. (Guardian)

Hospitals are simultaneously navigating new NHS England guidance requiring single-sex wards, toilets and changing rooms to be allocated by biological sex following last year’s supreme court ruling on the Equality Act. An internal NHS England impact assessment concluded that while the policy supports privacy and legal clarity for many patients, transgender patients could face treatment delays, greater risk of being cared for in corridors if side rooms are unavailable, or distress that deters them from seeking care. In Westminster, an expected Liberal Democrat showdown failed to materialise after a meeting of the party’s 72 MPs ended without a challenge to Ed Davey, despite grumbling over his conference pledges on income tax cuts and the pension triple lock. (Guardian, Guardian)

Economy

Grid delays cost Essex a flagship Microsoft AI datacentre

Microsoft has withdrawn from an agreement to serve as the anchor customer for Nscale’s planned artificial intelligence datacentre in Loughton, Essex, dealing a blow to a project ministers promoted last year as the largest facility of its kind in Britain. Although the British developer has cleared the former scaffolding yard and hoped to open the site next year, electricity distributor UK Power Networks informed Nscale that sufficient grid capacity may remain unavailable until the early or mid-2030s. While Microsoft continues to partner with Nscale on a separate datacentre in Norway, the prospect of waiting years for power prompted the US company to walk away from the Essex deal. (Guardian, BBC)

Nscale insisted that it remains committed to developing the Loughton site and is exploring on-site power generation alongside alternative locations across the UK. The setback highlights a physical bottleneck confronting the government’s industrial strategy: attracting capital commitments from technology companies is far easier than securing high-voltage grid connections and building power infrastructure quickly enough to run energy-hungry computing clusters. (Guardian)

Home insulation weighed against North Sea oil in budget debate

Preserving the government’s £15bn warm homes plan would cut household energy bills by roughly £1bn a year, boost the economy by £1.6bn annually and support around 50,000 jobs across small installation firms, according to an analysis by the E3G thinktank. The programme, which funds home insulation and heat pumps with a focus on low-income households, is expected to draw in a further £15bn in private investment, yet faces potential reductions as ministers search for fiscal savings ahead of the budget. (Guardian)

E3G contrasted those domestic gains with the roughly 1,100 direct and indirect jobs projected for the proposed Rosebank oilfield west of Shetland, where oil would be sold on international markets rather than lowering British utility bills directly. Ministers are weighing consent for Rosebank and the Jackdaw gasfield, both of which held licences before Labour halted new exploration permits. Energy companies and trade bodies have urged Whitehall to maintain consistent retrofit funding so suppliers can train installers and scale up domestic supply chains. (Guardian)

Condé Nast UK profits drop and doorstep roofing scams surge

Pre-tax profits at the British arm of Condé Nast fell 36% last year to nearly £18m, excluding exceptional items, as domestic revenue slipped 7% to £70.6m. Strong growth in international income, which rose 18% to £29.7m, kept total turnover at the publisher of Vogue, GQ and Tatler steady at roughly £100m. Restructuring costs more than doubled from £1.9m to £4.3m due to severance payouts, even as overall staffing edged down by only five roles to 461. The figures follow an exceptional 2024 bolstered by the £64m sale of Vogue House in Hanover Square, and arrive shortly after the billionaire Newhouse family ruled out selling the global publishing group. (Guardian)

For British households, a more immediate financial hazard has appeared on the doorstep. Citizens Advice recorded more than 2,200 complaints about suspected roofing frauds in England and Wales in the year to August, a 10% rise that made bogus roof repairs the charity’s most commonly reported consumer scam. Three-quarters of cases begin with cold callers claiming to have spotted a loose tile before inventing costly gutter or timber defects and demanding immediate bank transfers for work that is never carried out. (Guardian)

Technology

The World Bank backs small AI as researchers test self-replicating worms

Speaking at the annual meetings of the World Bank and the International Monetary Fund in Bangkok, World Bank chief knowledge officer Paschal Donohoe urged developing nations to build specialised, compact artificial intelligence models capable of running on mobile phones in local languages with minimal internet connectivity. Rather than chasing sprawling consumer chatbots, Donohoe pointed to tools such as the bank’s AgriConnect scheme in India, which gives smallholders localized soil advice where agricultural specialists are scarce. IMF managing director Kristalina Georgieva warned the same gathering that while basic electricity and digital infrastructure could democratise AI adoption, roughly 40% of jobs worldwide face augmentation or elimination, threatening the routine entry-level roles that young workers traditionally use to start their careers. (Guardian)

At the other end of the computing spectrum, researchers at Anthropic have demonstrated a prototype self-replicating AI worm capable of finding software flaws and spreading autonomously across connected devices. Because everyday internet-connected appliances from smart fridges to routers rarely receive timely security patches, security analysts warn that adaptive malware could eventually exploit unpatched networks much faster than traditional computer viruses. Other security researchers countered that current prototypes move slowly and remain easy to block with strong passwords and routine patching. (Guardian, Guardian)

Cities push back on robotaxis and a police login leaks plate data

With federal autonomous-vehicle legislation stalled in Congress, American municipal governments are using street-management and zoning rules to curb driverless car fleets. Minneapolis is weighing an ordinance requiring human safety monitors inside autonomous vehicles, San Diego has moved to restrict Waymo pickups at its airport, and Austin firefighters have demanded rapid towing protocols and direct communication lines when driverless cars block emergency scenes. California bills would let cities cap fleet sizes or operating hours, while Santa Monica and Culver City have used zoning and noise rules to curb charging depots. Facing a local regulatory patchwork, operators such as Waymo and Uber are prioritising US states that prohibit municipal bans while preparing expansions in London, Manchester, Tokyo and Munich. (The Verge)

Municipal oversight of civic technology also came under scrutiny in Savannah, Georgia, where police commanders dismissed officer Amber Harpe after discovering she had shared her login for the city’s Flock automated licence-plate reader network with her husband, an Immigration and Customs Enforcement agent. Although Savannah police policy bars sharing Flock data with federal immigration authorities, Tyler Harpe used the account to run 983 searches across 97 vehicle plates, overwhelmingly registered to Hispanic drivers. Senior commanders overruled a recommended eight-hour suspension and fired Harpe alongside five other department employees caught misusing the surveillance system. (The Verge)

In consumer hardware, supply-chain reports indicate that Apple could unveil a redesigned MacBook Pro featuring an OLED display and touchscreen support as early as 6 November, alongside a next-generation HomeView smart display mounted on a motorised robotic arm slated for next year. Meanwhile, in Washington, Representative Ritchie Torres introduced the Power To The Players Act, directing the Federal Trade Commission to require offline play modes before publishers shut down servers for purchased video games, while Silent Hill 2 remake studio Bloober Team pledged to keep releasing physical discs and publisher Alliance Arts cancelled a public Steam demo for Drunken Goddess Reflux to prevent AI-generated clones. (9to5Mac, 9to5Mac, The Verge, IGN, IGN)

Shakespeare and Marlowe shared credit on Edward III, study finds

Computerised linguistic analysis has concluded beyond reasonable doubt that the Elizabethan history play Edward III, published anonymously in 1596 and omitted from the 1623 First Folio, was written primarily by William Shakespeare in collaboration with Christopher Marlowe. Researchers Rory Loughnane at the University of Kent and Brett Greatley-Hirsch at the University of Leeds compared the text against works by 10 dramatists from the late 1580s and early 1590s, examining word-frequency patterns and grammatical markers across individual scenes. Their tests attributed 23 segments of the play to Shakespeare and nine segments, including the closing martial speech, to Marlowe. (Guardian)

Because Marlowe was killed in a Deptford lodging-house brawl in May 1593 at the age of 29, the findings date the collaboration to around 1592, when both playwrights were in their late twenties. Following earlier scholarship linking Marlowe to the Henry VI trilogy, the study portrays the two dramatists as regular working partners rather than distant theatrical rivals. Separately, an exhibition opening at the Fitzwilliam Museum in Cambridge on 23 October restores Morris & Co designer Kate Faulkner to prominence, displaying her 6.3-metre silvered-gesso altarpiece, tiles, wallpapers and Red House embroidery. (Guardian, Guardian)

In Bristol, the Old Vic is countering tight school arts budgets by taking Muneera Pilgrim’s play Protests, Hymns and Cries of Triumph directly into 19 secondary schools, reaching roughly 4,000 pupils for free. (Guardian)

Sports

Madueke breaks the deadlock as Arsenal grind past Forest

Noni Madueke came off the bench at half-time to score an 80th-minute winner as Arsenal beat Nottingham Forest 1–0 at the City Ground. In a tight, low-tempo contest where Arsenal failed to register a first-half shot on target for the seventh time in 10 matches this season, Mikel Arteta replaced Eberechi Eze with Madueke at the break before introducing Jurri&ebreve;n Timber, Mikel Merino and Viktor Gyökeres in a 66th-minute triple change. Ten minutes from time, Declan Rice intercepted a loose clearance and fed Madueke, who cut inside and saw his strike deflect off Forest defender Jair Cunha past goalkeeper Matz Sels for Arsenal’s only shot on target of the afternoon. (Guardian, BBC)

The victory keeps Arsenal within reach of leaders Manchester City and extends the club’s remarkable reliance on its bench: Arsenal substitutes have contributed 26 Premier League goals and assists since the start of last season, more than any other side in the division. With summer signing Christos Tzolis sidelined by a hamstring injury, Madueke has seized his chance across both wings, following an impressive display against Leeds and a Champions League assist against Lille with Sunday’s decisive goal. Oliver Glasner’s Forest side, led energetically up front by Liam Delap, have still yet to score a home league goal in five attempts under their new manager. (Guardian, BBC)

Brighton hit five past Palace while United draw again

Brighton matched their record 5–0 derby victory over Crystal Palace, racing into a three-goal lead inside the opening 10 minutes at the Amex Stadium. After Palace defender Jaydee Canvot headed a Pascal Gross free-kick into his own net, Diego Gómez lobbed goalkeeper Walter Benítez from 51 metres, the longest-range Premier League goal since Wayne Rooney scored from 58 metres in 2017. Yasin Ayari added a deflected third before the interval, and second-half goals from Charalampos Kostoulas and substitute Jack Hinshelwood left Palace manager Pierre Sage apologising to supporters in shame after his side’s heavy defeat. (Guardian)

At Elland Road, Manchester United drew 1–1 with Leeds as Dominic Calvert-Lewin’s second-half strike cancelled out Bryan Mbeumo’s opener, leaving Michael Carrick’s side with seven points and a single victory from seven league matches. Bournemouth earned their first league win under Marco Rose by beating 10-man Sunderland 2–0 at the Vitality Stadium, where Brazilian striker Evanilson set up Marcus Tavernier’s opener, drew the 17th-minute foul that saw Kevin Danso sent off, and rounded Robin Roefs for the second. In the Women’s Super League, Lauren James and Ellie Carpenter each scored twice as Chelsea beat Tottenham 6–2, while Manchester City defeated Brighton 3–2 to stay two points clear. (Guardian, Guardian, Guardian)
