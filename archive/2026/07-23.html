LEAD STORY

Burnham’s first week turned from bills to prisons

Andy Burnham ordered a review of September prisoner-release plans after Lissie Harper condemned the possibility that two of PC Andrew Harper’s killers could be freed early. The decision puts Downing Street between overcrowded prisons, victims’ families and a justice timetable inherited by the new government. (Guardian, BBC)

The same day brought a cost-of-living move, with most bus fares in England due to be capped at £2 from January. Burnham’s opening week is already carrying the strain of prisons, transport bills, tax signals and a promise that government can move quickly without losing control of the details. (BBC, Guardian)

NEWS

Brazil’s campaign moved into a voting-system fight

Flávio Bolsonaro repeated false claims about electronic voting machines in a meeting with diplomats and asked foreign governments to send observers for the 2026 election. The PT, PV and PCdoB federation went to the TSE over the statements, while electoral courts were told to seek AI specialists to speed up checks on manipulated campaign content. (Meio, Folha, Folha)

Ukraine’s new commander faced the culture test

Mykhailo Drapatyi’s arrival at the top of Ukraine’s armed forces brought hopes that he can break old command habits after years of criticism from soldiers and reformers. Ukrainian drones also hit Wildberries logistics hubs in Krasnodar and Stavropol for the second time, keeping pressure on Russian supply networks away from the front. (Guardian, BBC)

France counted thousands of heatwave deaths

France recorded 5,764 excess deaths between 17 June and 2 July during a severe heatwave, a 36% excess mortality rate. The figures arrived as southern Europe entered another stretch of record temperatures, wildfires and health warnings. (Guardian)

The River Tyne spill reached a billion pellets

About a billion plastic nurdles entered the River Tyne after two ships collided, with up to 25 tonnes of raw plastic pellets spreading farther than first feared. The spill gives the north-east a long clean-up and another test of shipping, port safety and plastic pollution controls. (Guardian)

Farage’s £5m gift reached HMRC

The Conservatives asked HMRC to examine whether Nigel Farage should have paid tax on a £5m gift from crypto billionaire Christopher Harborne. The question is whether the money was a personal gift or compensation linked to Farage’s return to parliamentary politics. (Guardian)

A water boss became a criminal defendant

Former Southern Water chief executive Matthew Wright and three other former employees face criminal proceedings over allegations that sewage testing results were manipulated to avoid large fines. Wright is the first water-industry boss to face a criminal charge over sewage discharges. (Guardian)

Segro put another London listing in play

FTSE 100 warehouse landlord Segro moved toward accepting a US takeover approach after initially looking ready to resist. The potential loss of a large London-listed property company lands inside a wider argument over the depth and pull of the UK market. (Guardian)

Oil profits rose with the Iran war

Norway’s state oil company Equinor nearly doubled quarterly profits to $11.5bn after higher oil and gas prices followed the war on Iran and disruption around the Strait of Hormuz. The company is also the UK’s biggest gas supplier. (Guardian)

Brazil prepared more tariff relief

Brasília discussed further measures for sectors hit by the United States’ 25% tariff on Brazilian products, after already announcing extra credit for affected companies. The trade dispute is now part of the election economy as well as foreign policy. (Folha, Meio)

JBS faced a court demand over expansion

Greenpeace asked Dutch courts to force JBS to disclose how a $6bn global expansion plan fits legal duties on climate, nature and human rights. The case puts the world’s biggest meat company’s growth plans inside a European accountability test. (Guardian)

TECHNOLOGY

Samsung widened the foldable line

Samsung opened preorders for the Galaxy Z Flip 8, Z Fold 8 and Z Fold 8 Ultra, with the devices due on 7 August and prices starting at $1,199.99, $1,899.99 and $2,099.99. The launch also brought updated watches and a clearer look at Samsung’s fall smart-glasses plans with Google. (The Verge, MacMagazine, The Verge)

Apple financing code pointed to a harder lock

Code in an iOS 27 beta would let Apple place a financed iPhone into Restricted Mode after missed payments, according to developer findings tied to the expected Apple Upgrade programme. The feature would make device finance part of the operating system’s enforcement layer. (The Verge, MacMagazine)

Apple’s budget MacBook moved toward a refresh

Apple is testing an updated MacBook Neo with more memory, a new processor and new colour options after the budget laptop’s strong first run. Separate supply-chain chatter points to a busy Mac cycle across Mac mini, MacBook Pro, MacBook Air and Mac Studio lines. (The Verge, MacMagazine)

Claude Code added simulator work

Claude Code for Mac gained a simulator button that can run iOS, iPadOS, visionOS and watchOS simulators inside the desktop workflow. The feature moves agentic coding closer to a live app-test loop for Apple-platform developers. (MacStories, MacMagazine)

Meta avoided the next addiction trial

A 15-year-old Florida plaintiff dropped a social-media addiction lawsuit against Meta days before a Los Angeles bellwether trial was due to begin. The move delays the next courtroom test of claims that platform design harmed younger users. (BBC, The Verge)

CULTURE

Tate chose Jessica Morgan for its reset

Jessica Morgan will return from New York’s Dia Art Foundation to become Tate director, replacing Maria Balshaw at a difficult moment for the four-gallery institution. Her appointment brings a former Tate curator back into a job carrying pressure over audiences, exhibitions, money and direction. (Guardian, Guardian)

Charles Gaines left bodybuilding’s origin story

Charles Gaines, author of the 1974 book Pumping Iron, died at 84. The book and later documentary helped bring professional bodybuilding into the mainstream and introduced Arnold Schwarzenegger to a much wider public. (Guardian)

The Odyssey argument moved to AI

Christopher Nolan’s The Odyssey kept its box-office momentum despite an online backlash, while Elon Musk said Grok Imagine would make a full-length AI version of Homer’s epic before the end of the year. The fight around one blockbuster now stretches from casting politics to synthetic cinema. (Guardian, Guardian)

Books became a fashion signal

Celebrity book clubs, literary parties and fashion products built around classics have turned reading into a visible lifestyle marker. Dior’s Book Tote variants and Coach’s Penguin bag charms are part of a wider commercial rush around literary taste. (Guardian)

Ray Exworth’s hidden sculptures emerged

Ray Exworth spent six decades making large sculptures in sheds on a Cornish smallholding, with only a tiny circle seeing the work during his lifetime. After his death, the collection has become a rare discovery story in British sculpture. (Guardian)

SPORTS

Saliba’s World Cup injury hit Arsenal

Arsenal expect William Saliba to miss an extended period with a back injury suffered during France’s World Cup run. The centre-back does not need surgery, but the Premier League champions begin the new season with a major defensive absence. (Guardian, BBC)

Liverpool’s minority sale drew Bezos talks

Jeff Bezos held talks about joining Amit Bhatia’s consortium seeking about 30% of Liverpool from Fenway Sports Group. The provisional £1.35bn approach would put a major new valuation marker on one of the Premier League’s biggest clubs. (Guardian, Guardian)

Foden committed to Manchester City

Phil Foden signed a new four-year Manchester City contract, with an extra 12-month option that could keep him at the club until 2030. The deal gives Enzo Maresca a familiar centrepiece as City move on from the Guardiola era. (Guardian)

Morocco’s 2030 World Cup work started early

Morocco’s football authorities turned quickly from the 2026 tournament to the cost, infrastructure and legacy pressures of co-hosting the 2030 World Cup. The next cycle already carries questions familiar from previous host nations: stadiums, transport, public money and what remains after the final. (Guardian)

LIFESTYLE

Cutting ultra-processed food became practical work

Families trying to reduce ultra-processed food face a food system built around convenience, shelf life and cheap treats. Practical switches around snacks, baking, labels and cooking routines are becoming part of everyday health planning. (Guardian)

Mogadishu’s cafes changed women’s work

As fear and fighting ease in Mogadishu, a growing cafe scene is giving women jobs as baristas and creating new social spaces in the city. Coffee shops are becoming small civic rooms as well as businesses. (Guardian)

Japan tried a personal cooling booth

A new Japanese cooling booth promises a 10-minute chill during extreme heat, selling itself as a personal alternative to air-conditioning or a fan. The device reflects how hotter summers are pushing daily life toward more individual cooling habits. (Guardian)

Group hiking found its summer audience

Community walking trips in Slovakia’s High Tatras show how outdoor travel is leaning into shared routes, mountain scenery and easy company among strangers. The appeal is simple: movement, altitude and a day that feels larger than the itinerary. (Guardian)
