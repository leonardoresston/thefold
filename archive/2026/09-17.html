America turns the rate dial back up

The Federal Reserve has raised interest rates for the first time in more than three years, reversing the direction that policymakers and borrowers had expected at the start of 2026. Its rate-setting committee voted unanimously for a quarter-point increase, taking the benchmark range to 3.75%–4%. Chair Kevin Warsh said the summer’s inflation readings had failed to show a convincing improvement. The move reaches far beyond trading desks: American mortgages, car finance, student debt and business loans all take their cue from the cost of money set in Washington, while governments and companies around the world price borrowing against US markets. (Guardian)

The backdrop changed quickly. After rates peaked at 5.25%–5.5% in 2023, cuts through 2024 and 2025 appeared to have brought the long inflation fight towards a calmer phase. This year began with officials contemplating another reduction. Renewed war between the United States and Iran then pushed energy costs sharply higher. American petrol is averaging about a dollar a gallon more than a year ago, diesel has reached a record $6.31, and the ten-year Treasury yield touched a 19-year high this week. Higher fuel prices move through freight, food and manufacturing before households see them in the final bill. (Guardian)

New Fed projections suggest the increase may be the beginning of a short cycle. A majority of officials expect another rise before the end of the year, and four put the rate at 4.25%–4.5% in December. Their forecasts remain relatively positive on growth and employment, though inflation may take until 2029 to return to the 2% target. That combination explains the choice: the economy still has enough strength to absorb tighter credit, while waiting risks allowing higher energy prices to settle into wages and expectations. Real hourly earnings were already down 0.1% from a year earlier in August, and consumer confidence has weakened as price expectations climb. (Guardian)

The decision also opens another institutional fight. Donald Trump demanded rates of 1% or lower immediately after the announcement, while Warsh defended the central bank’s authority to judge inflation independently of trade and fiscal policy. The president wants cheaper borrowing before November’s midterm elections and has promised a $5,000 dividend to Americans if Republicans keep control of Congress. The national debt passed $40tn last month, which makes both that promise and a sharp rate cut harder to reconcile with the inflation problem the Fed is trying to contain. Voters now face a familiar squeeze in a new form: daily costs remain high, pay has lost ground, and the remedy makes credit more expensive. (Guardian)

News

Britain’s EU reset arrives on the factory floor

The next UK-EU summit may be delayed unless Brussels agrees to discuss rules that could exclude British companies from major European contracts. The Industrial Accelerator Act, usually called “Made in Europe”, is meant to reduce the bloc’s dependence on Chinese manufacturing by favouring EU suppliers in sectors including cars and defence. Britain fears the same barrier could shut its products out of billions of pounds of procurement. Ministers will hold back from setting a summit date until the issue joins negotiations already covering food and drink trade, a youth mobility programme and student fees. A meeting discussed for early November could now slip towards the end of the month or later. (Guardian)

The politics on each side are understandable. European factories face a trade deficit with China running at roughly €1bn a day, and France wants the act completed by year’s end. Germany, Italy, Poland and the Netherlands are among countries sympathetic to including Britain, but some governments believe London would receive more than it gives. UK ministers see inclusion as a test of whether close security and economic ties can produce practical benefits after Brexit. The negotiation will therefore decide more than the wording of an industrial rule. It will show whether the reset can handle the difficult cases in which cooperation brings a visible advantage to one side and a domestic argument for the other. (Guardian)

Sunderland gives the dispute a physical address. Nissan plans to invest £170m there to build the hybrid Kicks SUV, adding a fourth model to Britain’s largest car factory and helping protect 6,000 jobs at a plant running at half capacity. The commitment depends partly on the government weakening the zero-emission vehicle mandate from an 80% sales target in 2030 towards the 50% Nissan is seeking. The company is also lobbying for Britain to be included in Made in Europe and has warned that exclusion could ultimately threaten the factory. A proposed production deal with China’s Chery could help fill spare capacity. Sunderland now sits where three policies meet: Britain’s electric transition, Europe’s answer to Chinese competition and the local need to keep a huge manufacturing base working. (Guardian)

The money trail through Britain’s slave economy

New historical research has traced the proceeds and machinery of the slave trade more deeply into Britain’s financial system. Historians compiling the Register of British Slave Traders identified 24 Bank of England directors who invested in the trafficking of enslaved Africans. Four were founding directors when the Bank was created in 1694, 20 served over the following century, and nine also became governor. At least 30 of the Bank’s original subscribers had slave-trade investments, including William III and Mary II through their shares in the Royal African Company. The finding places this wealth inside the institution’s initial capital and leadership, where it shaped the Bank from its earliest years. (Guardian)

Private banks, insurers and public corporations provided credit, payments and risk cover for voyages that carried millions of people across the Atlantic. The Bank itself served clients including the Royal African Company and South Sea Company. Separate research has found that it owned 599 enslaved people on two plantations, while earlier work documented 16 governors and 26 directors with interests in plantations and enslaved labour before abolition in 1833. The abolition settlement paid £20m to enslavers, an amount estimated at £23bn today by one measure, and nothing to the people whose lives and work had been taken. The Treasury and the Bank administered those payments. (Guardian)

Institutions began acknowledging individual connections more openly after the Black Lives Matter protests of 2020, and the Bank apologised for the involvement of former governors and directors. The register adds a network view. It follows people, investments and corporate roles across shipping, banking, insurance and government, allowing the history to be measured through records as well as notorious biographies. That matters for the continuing reparations debate because it identifies the structures that accumulated value and survived long after trafficking ended. Modern institutions have changed enormously since their seventeenth-century foundations. Their accounts, authority and inherited wealth still make the past legible. The next argument will concern what acknowledgement requires once the evidence has been assembled. (Guardian)

Women reach the same diagnosis and receive different care

A review of 38 international studies has found that women with the same conditions as men are consistently less likely to be offered active treatment. Researchers at the University of St Andrews saw the pattern in 33 studies. For heart attacks, heart failure and irregular heart rhythms, women were more likely to receive medication while men were more likely to receive a bypass, stent or another procedure. Men with Parkinson’s were referred more often for deep brain stimulation; men with liver failure were more likely to receive a transplant. Women on kidney dialysis spent longer using temporary catheters instead of receiving permanent access and were less likely to receive strong pain relief. (Guardian)

The review found no significant sex difference in treatment for stroke or diabetes, and women were more likely to receive treatment for dementia. Those exceptions make the broader result more useful: they show that the analysis was capable of finding equal or reversed patterns when the evidence supported them. None of the clinical guidelines examined recommended different treatment by sex. Researchers pointed instead to assumptions about symptoms and suitability, including a tendency to attribute women’s complaints to anxiety even when tests were positive. Treatment decisions can then diverge at several points: how a symptom is interpreted, which intervention is discussed, who receives a specialist referral and how risk is explained. The practical response begins before a national strategy or a new service. Clinicians can record which options were offered, why one was preferred and whether comparable patients are being sent down different paths. Routine audits can compare those decisions across sex, age and ethnicity, making patterns visible before they become accepted habits. Unequal care becomes harder to overlook when the decision itself is visible. (Guardian)

Technology & Gaming

Scotland pauses the AI building boom

Holyrood has voted to suspend decisions on new AI datacentres for up to a year while Scotland writes national planning guidance. More than 20 large projects have been proposed, including a development at Auchtertool in Fife billed as the world’s second largest. MSPs also backed mandatory environmental assessments covering energy, water, emissions and effects on neighbouring communities. Britain has encouraged developers to look north as it expands AI capacity, but the applications moved faster than a shared definition of a green datacentre or a plan for the electricity demand. The pause makes Scotland an early test of whether infrastructure policy can catch up before construction fixes the answer in concrete. (Guardian)

The waste at the other end of the system is becoming clearer too. The Basel Action Network estimates that AI-related equipment retired between 2025 and 2050 could total 395m–617m tonnes, enough in its upper scenario to fill 23m forty-foot shipping containers. Its calculation includes power distribution, cooling, backup systems and networking gear alongside servers and graphics processors. Earlier estimates concentrated on the computers and missed most of a datacentre’s electro-mechanical infrastructure. BAN expects AI to account for 15%–20% of global electronic waste by mid-century as total annual waste rises towards 211m tonnes. (Verge)

Less than a quarter of today’s 68.3m tonnes of annual e-waste is formally collected and recycled. The remainder often enters informal systems where equipment is burned or buried, exposing workers and communities to lead, chromium and other toxic materials. Scotland cannot settle that global problem through planning law, and a moratorium will not reduce demand for computation. It can require developers to state the full resource cost, the jobs created, the lifespan of installed equipment and the route by which it will eventually be reused or recycled. Datacentres are frequently described through their future benefits. The vote asks each proposal to account for its physical present and its afterlife as well. (Guardian, Verge)

Apple considers putting its chips in the server room

Apple is exploring a return to enterprise servers, fifteen years after it discontinued the Xserve. The proposed systems would be sold to AI developers, companies and governments, with a smaller model clustering two future M8 Ultra chips and a larger version using four. Nvidia’s NVLink Fusion could connect the processors so that they work as a single system. The plan has been under development for about a year, remains subject to cancellation and is unlikely to produce a commercial machine before 2029. That distant date is important: Apple has only just introduced its M6 generation, so both the silicon and the market it would enter are still moving. (9to5Mac, Verge, MacMagazine)

The opening comes from efficiency. Mac Studios and Mac minis have become useful small clusters for running local models, sometimes selling out as developers search for alternatives to conventional server hardware. Apple’s Arm-based processors promise substantial computing power within a lower energy envelope, while Nvidia supplies the networking technology required to scale beyond one box. The partnership would be striking after years of a cool relationship between the companies, though Apple is already using Nvidia chips for its rebuilt Siri infrastructure. Selling a server would take Apple into support, procurement and upgrade cycles far removed from consumer devices. It would also place the company on both sides of the AI boom: building its own services and selling the machinery used by everyone else. (9to5Mac, Verge)

Two game studios meet the edge of the runway

Heart Machine has laid off nearly its entire staff after the publisher financing an unannounced game withdrew. Founder Alx Preston said the studio would help affected workers find new roles and that the remaining team would keep trying to build a path forward, though its survival is uncertain. Heart Machine’s 2016 debut, Hyper Light Drifter, became a cult favourite through its bright ruined world and demanding combat. Its most recent game, Possessors, arrived last year. A respected back catalogue could not bridge the sudden disappearance of project finance, which shows how quickly a studio organised around one future release can lose the income supporting all of its present work. (IGN)

Developers at Build A Rocket Boy announced layoffs on the same day, deepening questions around a company that grew beyond 500 employees while developing MindsEye. The action game began as part of Everywhere, a creation platform led by former Grand Theft Auto design chief Leslie Benzies, before the studio concentrated its effort on the standalone release. Its troubled reception left a large organisation without the momentum expected from its flagship project. Heart Machine and Build A Rocket Boy reached this point by different routes—one lost a publisher before release, the other expanded around a game that failed to establish itself—but workers carry the immediate cost in both cases. (IGN)

The wider industry continues to produce hits, yet success is increasingly concentrated and difficult to forecast. Multiplayer newcomer Wardogs is already outpacing the launch sales of Arc Raiders and Helldivers 2 on Steam, while established teams cut jobs or disappear between projects. Publishers can see a crowded release calendar and withdraw early; independent studios then need enough cash to reach another deal, launch or acquisition. Players encounter the result as delayed games and shuttered names. Developers experience it as a financing system in which years of specialised work can be separated from employment by a single cancelled contract. (IGN)

Culture

Bonnie Greer’s generous public argument

Bonnie Greer, the British-American playwright, novelist and cultural commentator, has died at 77 after a brief illness. Born on Chicago’s South Side as the eldest of seven children, she studied playwriting with David Mamet, worked in New York theatre and moved to Britain after bringing a play to the Edinburgh fringe in 1985. She became a British citizen in 1997. Her books ranged from the novel Hanging By Her Teeth to studies of Barack Obama’s Chicago and Langston Hughes, while her plays and radio dramas returned to the lives of Black people and women across American and British history. She was made an OBE in 2010 and a fellow of the Royal Society of Literature in 2022. (Guardian)

Greer’s public presence joined that creative work to institutions and argument. She served on the boards of the British Museum, Royal Opera House and London Film School, and as chancellor of Kingston University. On Question Time in 2009, she sat alongside British National Party leader Nick Griffin and later transformed the experience into the opera Yes. Her radio play Marilyn and Ella, about Marilyn Monroe helping Ella Fitzgerald secure a nightclub booking, moved from the BBC to stages in Edinburgh and London. Those projects reveal her characteristic method: take a disputed or half-remembered public episode, place human relationships at its centre and let art reopen the conversation. Tributes have remembered her precision, courage, warmth and capacity to bring people together. (Guardian)

Tove Jansson’s private lines come to London

More than 100 works by Tove Jansson, including drawings never previously displayed, will open at the Quentin Blake Centre for Illustration in November. Tove Jansson and her Families is the first UK exhibition devoted to her illustration and will bring together early handmade booklets, photographs, films, correspondence and original art from The Moomins and the Great Flood. A draft cover showing the sea-loving Moominpappa with a giant shell recently emerged from the artist’s prolific archive. The finished books, written and illustrated between 1945 and 1977, have sold as many as 30m copies and been translated into more than 60 languages. (Guardian)

The exhibition widens the familiar view of Jansson. She illustrated Lewis Carroll and Tolkien, worked as an anti-fascist political cartoonist during the second world war and launched the first Moomin newspaper strip in London’s Evening News in 1954. One early strip sketch survived only because another cartoonist rescued the coffee-stained sheet from the waste-paper bin. Her characters made room for eccentricity, solitude, family tension and the unsettling force of nature inside stories children could enter easily. The show’s title reflects that range of belonging: biological family, chosen companions and the shifting groups that gather in Moominvalley. It runs in Clerkenwell from 21 November to 9 May, offering a close look at the lines through which that world first took shape. (Guardian)
