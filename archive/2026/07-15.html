LEAD STORY

Hillsborough law cleared the Commons in Starmer's final days

The Hillsborough law cleared the House of Commons in Keir Starmer's final days as prime minister, giving bereaved families a long-fought accountability bill before Andy Burnham takes office. The bill puts public bodies under a stronger duty of candour after major disasters and creates offences for officials who obstruct the truth. (The Guardian, BBC)

The vote arrived beside the Covid inquiry's procurement findings. Heather Hallett found Britain entered the pandemic dangerously unprepared, leaving health and care workers without adequate protection, while flawed purchasing arrangements wasted £10bn of PPE spending. (The Guardian, BBC)

NEWS

Widdecombe killing put MP security back before Westminster

Counter-terror police are treating Ann Widdecombe's killing as a targeted attack and have obtained a warrant to hold a 28-year-old suspect for up to seven days. Detectives are examining whether leftwing or single-issue hostility to her views, or to Reform UK, was a factor, while Burnham said MP security needs a fresh review. (The Guardian, BBC, BBC)

Older teenagers face a default overnight social media block

Sixteen- and 17-year-olds will be encouraged to follow a midnight-to-6am social media curfew from next spring. Covered apps will switch the block on by default, though teenagers can override it, extending Labour's online-harms plan beyond the under-16 ban announced last month. (The Guardian)

Child health indicators sent a warning before the next government

Leading paediatricians found UK child health outcomes had declined or stalled across 12 indicators. Falling vaccination rates, rising hospital admissions for asthma, obesity and mental health disorders left doctors warning that today's children could become one of the unhealthiest generations in decades. (The Guardian)

Spain's Almería wildfires left Britons among the dead

Seven Britons were among 12 foreign nationals killed in the wildfires that swept southern Spain. Authorities said 13 people died in all after postmortem examinations, with the fire breaking out last Thursday in Almería and spreading through parts of Andalusia. (The Guardian)

Hormuz remained the weak point in the US-Iran pause

The Strait of Hormuz became the faultline in the faltering US-Iran ceasefire, with control of the waterway again shaping the energy and shipping risk around the conflict. The route remains the pressure point where military escalation turns quickly into market and supply-chain stress. (BBC)

Brazil's campaign fight narrowed around Flávio Bolsonaro

Justice Alexandre de Moraes barred Flávio Bolsonaro from visiting Jair Bolsonaro for 90 days after finding that a published letter could bypass restrictions on the former president's political communication. Flávio accused the court of electoral interference, while Republicanos signalled national neutrality even as state-level negotiations kept a possible vice-presidential deal alive. (Canal Meio, Folha Poder)

Brazil's amendment crackdown reached party machines

Justice Flávio Dino said the outsourcing of congressional amendment nominations is illegal and ruled that only sitting parliamentarians can indicate the funds. The order followed federal police findings on Valdemar Costa Neto and Eduardo Cunha, including alleged Republicanos amendments directed by Cunha to 29 cities in Minas Gerais. (Folha Poder, Folha Poder)

US inflation cooled while oil moved back up

US inflation eased to an annual 3.5% in June after the brief US-Iran ceasefire pulled energy prices lower. The month-on-month CPI fall of 0.8% was the largest since April 2020, but renewed strikes have pushed oil and petrol prices back into the risk column. (The Guardian)

IBM's selloff shook software stocks

IBM shares fell more than 25% after a profit warning and weak preliminary second-quarter figures. Revenue for the three months to June reached $17.2bn, up just 1% year on year, and the selloff spilled into the wider software sector. (The Guardian)

Tariff politics kept moving through courts and capitals

The US government has paid $81bn in refunds this fiscal year after the supreme court ruled Donald Trump's tariffs illegal. In Brasília, Lula's government prepared separate responses to the White House's proposed tariffs on Brazilian imports. (The Guardian, Folha Poder)

The India-UK trade deal reached consumers

The India-UK free trade agreement between the world's fifth- and sixth-largest economies came into effect, moving the deal from diplomacy into prices, shelves and customs rules. Scotch, textiles and consumer goods are now part of the first test of whether the agreement changes shopping costs. (BBC)

TECHNOLOGY

OpenAI's first hardware moved toward a speaker

OpenAI may make its first device a screenless ChatGPT smart speaker, with camera and sensor hardware intended to understand its surroundings. The hardware push comes as the company faces Apple's trade-secret lawsuit over a planned AI device. (The Verge)

Grok Build exposed full repositories

SpaceXAI's Grok Build coding tool was found packaging and uploading users' whole codebases to Google Cloud, including files it had been told not to open. The company turned off the behaviour after the finding became public. (The Verge)

Meta faced AI-layoff bias claims

Twenty-six former Meta employees sued the company over allegations that internal AI tools helped target workers on leave during mass layoffs. The case turns workplace automation into an employment-law test for performance data, leave status and algorithmic selection. (The Verge)

Publishers took Gemini training to court

Major book publishers sued Google in New York, accusing the company of using millions of copyrighted books to train Gemini without permission. The case extends the AI copyright fight from images and journalism into the book trade's backlist. (The Guardian)

CULTURE

Sam Neill's public life settled into local memory

Sam Neill's co-stars and neighbours marked the actor as a practical joker, unpretentious craft worker and community figure after his death at 78. In rural New Zealand, the star of The Piano and Jurassic Park was remembered as a local presence who raised money for the cinema and kept ordinary routines. (The Guardian, The Guardian)

Broadway's Cats closing became a warning

Andrew Lloyd Webber warned that Broadway is in danger after Cats: The Jellicle Ball announced an early closing. The composer placed the decision inside a wider concern that theatres could follow Hollywood soundstages into underuse. (The Guardian)

Crime fiction's bestseller table had a pattern

Nine of the 10 bestselling UK fiction paperbacks this week featured the murder of a woman. The list turned a commercial habit into a cultural argument about the appetite for dead women as the engine of popular fiction. (The Guardian)

Dua Lipa backed Albania's flamingo protests

Dua Lipa gave public support to demonstrations in Albania against a €1.6bn resort backed by Jared Kushner. The protests entered their sixth week, and the singer's Kosovan Albanian roots turned a local development fight into a global culture story. (The Guardian)

SPORTS

Spain reached the World Cup final

Spain beat France 2-0 in Dallas to reach Sunday's World Cup final, with Mikel Oyarzabal and Pedro Porro scoring. Luis de la Fuente's side now wait for England or Argentina after smothering France's attack and turning the semi-final into a statement win. (The Guardian, BBC)

England and Argentina carried history into Atlanta

England's semi-final against Argentina places Harry Kane opposite Lionel Messi in a week that could define Kane's place beyond English football. The rivalry brings 1986, 1998 and 2002 back into the build-up, while Argentina's camp tried to keep the focus on the match itself. (The Guardian, The Guardian, The Guardian)

Fifa stretched the final's television window

Sunday's World Cup final is expected to have a half-time break of 20 to 25 minutes, while Tom Cruise was among the names set for the closing ceremony. The final is becoming a longer broadcast event as well as the last football act of the tournament. (BBC, The Guardian)

United added Tielemans as Chelsea moved for Malard

Manchester United confirmed Youri Tielemans from Aston Villa for about £35m on a five-year deal. Chelsea also moved close to signing Melvine Malard from United for a fee that could reach about £850,000, a club-record sale for the Manchester side. (The Guardian, The Guardian)

LIFESTYLE

Bali's water stress became a tourism story

Bali's rice terraces are losing water as tourism draws more heavily on springs and local supplies. The pressure is eroding a centuries-old irrigation culture built around shared water, leaving farmers with dry fields beside expanding holiday infrastructure. (The Guardian)

Britain's crisis checklist moved into ordinary homes

Downing Street urged the public to take small steps to protect water, power supplies and phone signal during severe weather, cyber-attacks or other national crises. The guidance puts household preparation into the same summer conversation as heat, floods and infrastructure strain. (The Guardian)

Summer travel got another consumer warning

An EasyJet Holidays customer who booked a £1,070 wellness retreat in Crete found the advertised spa and gym were not on site. The dispute is another reminder that package-holiday labels can hide the distance between brochure language and usable facilities. (The Guardian)
