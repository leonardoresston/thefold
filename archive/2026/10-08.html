Brazil’s runoff will be decided beyond the loyal camps

Brazil’s presidential runoff is settling around an electorate that has resisted belonging to either side. Flávio Bolsonaro led Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva by 1.87 percentage points in the first round, a narrow advantage inside a result that also left millions of votes available. In the north-east alone, the campaigns are pursuing about nine million people who chose defeated candidates and eight million who did not vote. Many of the voters now receiving daily appeals have changed their preference during the campaign and feel little obligation to a party or government. Their decision on 25 October is likely to turn on a more practical question: which candidate understands how ordinary life feels now. (Meio, Folha)

Bolsonaro’s coalition continued to grow on Wednesday. Republicanos formally endorsed him, joining the União Brasil–PP federation, Podemos and Ronaldo Caiado in a rapidly consolidating bloc; most state branches of the MDB are also moving towards support, while ACM Neto promised to mobilise allies in Bahia. The candidate is tying that electoral machine to the right’s new strength in Congress. He has spoken of using majorities in the Chamber and Senate to rewrite the constitution, reform criminal law and the judiciary, and “redemocratise” the country, while leaving the scope of those changes unclear. The Centrão’s increased willingness to discuss impeaching a supreme court justice gives that language immediate institutional weight. (Folha, Meio, Folha)

Lula has begun to answer a weaker first round with visible changes in style. He told allies that the campaign had been his least organised presidential effort since 1989, an assessment accidentally broadcast on the PT’s YouTube channel. Later he published a direct-to-camera video asking supporters to take the campaign into the streets. Younger PT deputies have pressed him to spend less time on his biography and make a sharper offer for the future, while his communications team faces criticism over a digital operation that allowed Bolsonaro to set the pace. The new format matters because Lula needs to reach people who know him chiefly as a permanent figure in the system, rather than through the trade-union story that shaped his first campaigns. (Folha, Folha, Folha)

The contrast is becoming clearer. Bolsonaro can point to endorsements, a strengthened PL and a legislature already leaning towards his programme. Lula has incumbency, social programmes and a long political record, but must translate all three into a credible promise about income, work and security for the next four years. The movable voter sits outside the emotional core of both campaigns and may distrust their largest claims. Winning that voter requires detail and presence: what will become cheaper, what will improve locally, which constitutional changes are intended, and who would share the benefits. A close first round has made Brazil’s least partisan citizens the most powerful people in the campaign. (Meio, Folha)

Badenoch leaves Birmingham in command of a smaller Conservative party

Kemi Badenoch ended the Conservative conference with authority inside her party and a difficult route beyond it. Her programme in Birmingham included exempting homes from inheritance tax, extending free childcare to households earning more than £100,000, opposition to net zero policy and a large reduction in human-rights protections. The inheritance-tax proposal is most relevant to homes worth more than £500,000, concentrating its benefit in London and the south-east. Some Conservatives see a way to recover prosperous voters lost to the Liberal Democrats; others worry that a collection of expensive promises for wealthier households leaves the cost of living in the background. (Guardian, BBC)

The party remains near 20% in national polling, yet Badenoch’s own position looks more secure than it did a year ago. Departures to Reform UK have removed several internal opponents, and a much smaller parliamentary party has fewer organised factions. YouGov research cited at the conference found that 90% of voters recognise Badenoch; only 22% recognise her new shadow chancellor, Andrew Griffith. That gap has produced a disciplined, highly personal opposition in which policy carries the leader’s stamp and colleagues struggle for attention. Birmingham gave the Conservatives unity and a recognisable voice. The next test is whether those assets can reconnect the party with voters who still place it behind Labour and Reform. (Guardian, Guardian)

Three years after 7 October, remembrance meets a stalled future

Israel marked the third anniversary of the Hamas-led attacks with memorial ceremonies for about 1,200 people killed on 7 October 2023, most of them civilians. Families and communities gathered while the country continued to live with the political and human consequences of the day that began the war. In Gaza, the subsequent Israeli offensive has killed about 73,000 Palestinians, most of them civilians, and wounded many more. The scale of those losses can make the anniversary feel numerical from a distance. On both sides it remains intimate: a missing relative, an empty room, a place where a familiar routine stopped. (BBC, Guardian)

A ceasefire has been in place for a year, although Gaza’s death toll has risen by about 1,400 during that period and airstrikes continue. Most residents remain inside a landscape of damaged homes and weak services. Water, nutritious food and medical care are scarce; infectious disease spreads in crowded displacement sites; reconstruction has barely begun. Hamas controls the part of Gaza outside the Israeli military’s zone and provides fragments of administration, while the plan for disarmament, Israeli withdrawal and rebuilding has stalled. People preserve pieces of their former lives through objects and routines: a schoolbag, a house key, battered plastic chairs, a haircut prepared for a wedding in a tent. Those details carry the shape of a normality that diplomacy has yet to restore. (Guardian)

Gene therapy gives ten blind patients a measure of sight

A small clinical trial has shown that optogenetic gene therapy can safely restore limited visual function for people with advanced retinitis pigmentosa. All ten participants received a single injection into their worse eye. A harmless synthetic virus carried instructions that made a ring of surviving retinal ganglion cells responsive to light. Special goggles then captured the scene ahead and converted it into pulses of a single wavelength, allowing the treated cells to send a monochrome impression to the brain. The method works around the many different genetic faults that can cause the disease because it targets cells that usually survive after the retina’s natural light receptors deteriorate. (Guardian)

Six participants achieved a clinically meaningful improvement in light sensitivity. With training, some could find and touch a notebook, locate a door or follow a line on the floor, and the benefit remained stable for four or five years. They still could not recognise faces, and the researchers describe high-resolution sight as a goal for the next five to ten years. One serious eye-related side effect resolved within minutes, with no treatment-related effects elsewhere in the body. The advance is deliberately modest: reliable shapes, obstacles and light where there had been almost none. For someone navigating a room, those gains can change the boundaries of an ordinary day. (Guardian)

Britain’s living-standards squeeze could become a record decline

The Joseph Rowntree Foundation estimates that average household income after housing costs will be £440 to £770 lower in real terms in 2029–30 than when Labour entered government in 2024. Even the smaller fall would be the largest recorded over a parliament since the modern series began in 1961. The forecast combines weak income growth with energy and housing costs that have moved towards the Bank of England’s adverse scenario. Five-year fixed mortgage rates reached an average of 6% this week, while some projections put a typical annual dual-fuel bill near £2,000 in the first quarter of next year. More than seven million families already go without essentials such as food or basic toiletries. (Guardian)

The budget on 28 October will show how much immediate relief the government is prepared to provide. Chancellor John Healey is considering an extra £100 for households receiving the £150 warm home discount, funded through taxation, as part of an energy package worth more than £1 billion. The foundation wants cheaper baseline units of gas and electricity for every household, with additional support for people in greater need, and it has also proposed reconnecting housing allowance to rents and raising universal credit. First secretary Louise Haigh is leading two cross-government workstreams, one on near-term housing and energy help and another on structural measures such as public control. Their recommendations are intended to feed a ten-year plan later this autumn, making the October budget an opening instalment rather than a complete answer. Each measure reaches a different part of the squeeze. A discount can soften the winter bill; housing and benefit reform would decide whether the same households begin the next winter with any more room to breathe. (Guardian)

Boots returns to the Weston family’s British high street

Canada’s Weston family has agreed to buy Boots for $8.9 billion, or about £6.74 billion, bringing one of Britain’s best-known retailers under the same holding company as Loblaws supermarkets and Shoppers Drug Mart. Wittington Investments will acquire Boots’ 1,800 UK and Irish stores, its opticians, the No7 beauty brand and its Thai franchise business, with backing from Fairfax. The Nottingham-based group employs about 50,000 people. Regulatory approval is still required, and the deal is expected to close in the first quarter of 2027. It also ends two decades in which Stefano Pessina remained involved through a succession of private and public owners. (Guardian)

The promise is stable ownership and fresh investment after years of uncertainty over a sale or stock-market flotation. Boots has closed more than 300 outlets in recent years, and many remaining shops need modernisation while online competitors and discount chains take a larger share of health and beauty spending. The estate still offers something hard to reproduce on a website: trusted pharmacists, convenient town-centre locations and a large loyalty scheme that connects routine purchases to health services. The Westons sold Selfridges in 2022 and are returning to the UK high street with a business whose social role is broader than ordinary retail. What they invest in stores, staff and pharmacy capacity will matter more than the familiarity of the new owner’s name. (Guardian)

Technology

ChatGPT starts building an interface around each answer

OpenAI has begun rolling out GPT-6 with an “Intelligent UI” that can answer with graphics, forms, charts, buttons and interactive tools alongside ordinary text. The system chooses a format from the question: a comparison can become a side-by-side view, a technical explanation can produce a diagram, and a practical request can create a working bill splitter, savings calculator or small game in the conversation. A library of native components keeps the designs familiar, while a compiler assembles and streams the interface as the model produces it. The answer can appear in stages, giving a reader useful material while the model continues to reason or search. (OpenAI, Verge, MacMagazine)

The launch begins with Plus, Pro, Business and Enterprise accounts, followed by Free and Go accounts from today; workplace availability also depends on administrator settings. OpenAI says an internal test found GPT-6 Instant begins answering web-search questions 44% sooner on average than GPT-5.6 Instant. The more consequential change is the move away from a fixed chat box as the only shape of AI software. A generated control can make a model’s reasoning easier to inspect by letting someone change an input and watch the output respond. It also increases the importance of design judgment: the model must know when interaction clarifies an answer and when a plain paragraph remains the most useful interface. (OpenAI, 9to5Mac)

Microsoft builds a Surface for local AI, then gives Copilot more reach

Microsoft’s Surface Laptop Ultra will go on sale on 16 October as a high-end Windows machine built around Nvidia’s Arm-based RTX Spark platform. The range starts at $2,599 with 24GB of memory and 512GB of storage and rises sharply for larger configurations. A 15-inch mini-LED touchscreen sits above a practical selection of USB-C, USB-A, HDMI, SD-card and headphone ports. Charging now uses a magnetic USB-C connector, preserving the easy breakaway behaviour of the old Surface plug while adopting a standard cable. Microsoft is pitching the machine at developers and creators who want generative models and video tools to run locally, including people who might otherwise choose a MacBook Pro. (Verge, MacMagazine)

The hardware arrived with a broader idea Microsoft calls Hybrid Intelligence. New Windows features will let Copilot combine cloud models with local processing, search files on the PC and take actions across the operating system. A demonstration showed an agent collecting documents requested by an accountant for a tax return. That kind of access can remove tedious transfers between email, folders and applications, while also putting more private material and consequential actions inside an automated workflow. The value will depend on clear permissions, a reliable record of what the agent changed and an easy way to stop or reverse it. A powerful local processor can keep more work on the device; the software still has to make its boundaries visible. (Verge)

Two successful games expose the insecurity behind their launches

Bit Reactor has begun bringing back employees who were furloughed shortly before Star Wars Zero Company launched in August. About 80% of the studio was reportedly placed on unpaid leave despite strong reviews for the tactical game. Chief executive Greg Foertsch described the recalls as a step towards continued support, and staffing has now recovered to more than half its pre-furlough level. A substantial group remains without pay. The sequence is unusually stark: years of specialist work, a well-received release and an employment crisis arrived together, leaving the people who made the game waiting to learn whether its success would secure their jobs. (IGN)

At Microsoft’s The Coalition, the release of Gears of War: E-Day has produced similar anxiety. Xbox has cut nearly 2,000 jobs this financial year, closed or reorganised studios, and laid off the game’s story director a week before early access. Staff have reportedly postponed any sense of celebration until several months after launch, expecting another round of cuts. Studio head Mike Crump offered no guarantee, though he said the new game provides a foundation that should make the next Gears faster to produce. Shared technology can shorten development. It does not answer the immediate question facing both teams: whether acclaimed work still creates durable employment in an industry reorganising faster than its games can be made. (IGN)

Sports

Arsenal secure the manager who ended their title wait

Mikel Arteta has signed a four-year contract worth more than £20 million a season, making him the Premier League’s best-paid manager and tying him to Arsenal until June 2030. The agreement rewards a season in which Arsenal ended a 22-year wait for the league title after three consecutive second-place finishes, then reached the Champions League final before losing to Paris Saint-Germain on penalties. Arteta took charge in December 2019 and is now the division’s longest-serving manager following Pep Guardiola’s departure from Manchester City. His coaching staff have extended their deals too, keeping the structure that rebuilt the side around a consistent style and a young core. (Guardian)

Declan Rice is also close to agreeing a new long-term contract, continuing the club’s effort to settle key futures before negotiations become distractions. Arsenal have shelved a major expansion of the Emirates Stadium after concern about the disruption of a temporary move, so the immediate investment remains concentrated on the team. Saturday’s home match against Leeds offers a quick return to ordinary competitive pressure: the champions need to respond to a 3–0 defeat at Brighton that ended their perfect league start. The new contracts express confidence over years. Their first demand is simpler, restoring rhythm after one poor afternoon. (BBC, Guardian)

The women’s Club World Cup collides with the domestic season

Arsenal and Chelsea could disappear from the Women’s Super League for as long as a month next season if the inaugural women’s Club World Cup goes ahead in the United States from 5 to 30 January 2028. Arsenal are guaranteed a place as the 2025 Champions League winners. Chelsea are likely to qualify through the European coefficient, although Manchester City would take England’s second slot if they win this season’s Champions League. A run deep into the tournament could force the English clubs to miss four WSL weekends and the fourth round of the FA Cup, leaving a lopsided table and a backlog just as the European knockout stage begins. (Guardian)

Moving the WSL winter break offers little relief because transatlantic travel and recovery would consume most of January. The calendar tightens again in August, when the Los Angeles Olympics will feature an expanded 16-team women’s tournament with senior internationals eligible to play, shortly before European domestic seasons resume. European Leagues has added the women’s competition to its complaint to the European Commission over Fifa’s use of power in setting the calendar. A global club tournament can give women’s football a valuable stage and new revenue. Its credibility also depends on allowing the best players to arrive healthy and return to domestic teams without turning spring into an exhausting sequence of make-up fixtures. (Guardian)
