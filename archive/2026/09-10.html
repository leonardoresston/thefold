Brazil’s chief justice stops the court’s chain reaction

Edson Fachin has stepped into the struggle that was pulling Brazil’s Supreme Court and federal police in opposite directions. The court’s president suspended the conflicting orders through which André Mendonça removed federal police director-general Andrei Rodrigues and Flávio Dino reinstated him less than a day later. Rodrigues remains in his post, as does intelligence director Leandro Almada. Fachin also froze Mendonça’s investigation involving Alexandre de Moraes and required any measure that could open an inquiry into a Supreme Court justice to pass through the presidency of the court. His ruling brings the police command back under one authority while the judges address the allegations among themselves. (Folha)

Fachin described the accumulation of overlapping decisions as a serious injury to public order and the integrity of the court. The sequence began with messages in which former Banco Master owner Daniel Vorcaro sought information from Moraes about investigations into the bank and asked whether he should leave Brazil. Mendonça disclosed the messages and advanced an inquiry touching Moraes; Moraes used the long-running fake-news inquiry to request an investigation into Mendonça; Mendonça then suspended the two senior police officers; Dino reversed him through a separate case. Several justices were deciding related questions from common evidence, and each new order created another procedural front. Fachin has now concentrated those fronts in his own office. (Folha, Meio)

The intervention also ends Moraes’s seven-year control of the fake-news inquiry, one of the widest and most disputed instruments the court has used against threats, disinformation and attacks on democratic institutions. Fachin will take over its remaining work and send pending matters to the prosecutor-general, who can bring charges or seek closure. Earlier acts will stand, and Moraes keeps the criminal cases already opened from the inquiry. The full court will meet in an extraordinary session on Tuesday to decide what to do with the police report about Moraes and Vorcaro. It may authorise an investigation or accept the prosecutor-general’s argument that the report is invalid. That public timetable gives the institution a chance to replace individual orders with a collective decision. (Folha, Folha)

The crisis is landing inside the final month of a presidential campaign that has suddenly tightened. A Meio/Ideia poll puts Lula and Flávio Bolsonaro level at 46% in a second-round choice. In the first-round prompted question Lula has 38.4% and Bolsonaro 37.3%, within the 2.5-point margin of error; a month ago their figures were 43% and 35%. Lula also recorded his lowest prompted and spontaneous first-round numbers in the poll’s nine editions, while Bolsonaro reached his highest. The survey of 1,500 voters was conducted from 4 to 7 September. Fachin’s order settles who runs the police today. Tuesday’s hearing must show voters that the court can test allegations against its own members without allowing its internal divisions to decide the investigation first. (Meio, Folha)

Reform UK’s funding questions become a criminal inquiry

Metropolitan police have widened an existing investigation into Reform UK’s political funding after an undercover operation raised allegations of illegal foreign support. Two close advisers to Nigel Farage, Dan Jukes and James Orr, resigned last week after they were filmed discussing arrangements with a reporter posing as the British son of a wealthy American. The supposed overseas donor paid more than £30,000 for polling intended to benefit Reform, which was published without being declared to the Electoral Commission. The footage also appeared to show discussion of routing a contribution through the donor’s UK-based son. Foreign donations are prohibited, and support provided as goods or services can count as a donation in kind. (Guardian, BBC)

The Met’s specialist team will fold the new material into its inquiry over donations made before the 2024 election, including £500,000 from Fiona Cottrell. She and her son George have previously been interviewed under caution. Detectives are obtaining the broadcast footage and working with the Electoral Commission and Crown Prosecution Service; nobody has been arrested or interviewed under caution over the latest allegations. The commission is considering its own regulatory steps. Reform denies wrongdoing and says it will cooperate. Police will have to establish whether the polling or any proposed arrangement crossed the line into an impermissible donation, and who knew how the assistance was funded. (Guardian, BBC)

Britain’s air-traffic failure moves from disruption to accountability

More than 2,000 flights were cancelled after the flight-processing system at the Swanwick air-traffic centre failed for four hours on Tuesday. Transport secretary Heidi Alexander told Parliament that a cyberattack had been ruled out and that she did not consider the breakdown unavoidable. She has asked the Civil Aviation Authority for an independent investigation, with an initial update due within six months, while Nats must deliver its own account within a week. Flights resumed, but aircraft and crews were left in the wrong places and passengers faced a second day of cancellations at Heathrow, Gatwick, Manchester, Birmingham and other airports. (BBC, Guardian)

This was Nats’s third major technical fault in three years. A 2023 shutdown disrupted about 700,000 passengers, and another outage led to 150 cancellations in 2025. Airlines are again responsible for food, accommodation and alternative travel even though the system failure sits outside their control; passengers are unlikely to receive statutory compensation because the event will probably be treated as an extraordinary circumstance. Carriers want the forthcoming Civil Aviation Bill to place financial consequences on the organisation responsible. The investigation now has two practical questions: why a system essential to national transport failed again, and whether its incentives reward the resilience airlines and travellers depend upon. (BBC)

The fight over Hormuz widens across ships, ports and oilfields

Iran says it attacked two American vessels and eight oil tankers around the Strait of Hormuz after the United States destroyed five Iranian tankers overnight. US Central Command says the attempts against its ships failed. At least one sailor was killed and another is missing, while a drone struck a tanker carrying two million barrels of fuel oil in Iraqi waters without causing a reported leak. Iran also launched missiles towards American bases in Jordan; authorities there said 18 were intercepted and two fell in unpopulated areas. The exchange is the sharpest in weeks of a six-month war increasingly organised around control of the waterway. (Guardian)

Washington has adopted a policy of hitting Iranian tankers whenever Iran targets a US warship. Tehran promises a much larger answer for each tanker it loses and plans to extend the area where ships need its permission to travel. Before the war, as much as one fifth of the world’s oil and gas passed through Hormuz. The oil price has now moved above $100 a barrel for the first time since July, adding inflation to the conflict’s military risks. The pressure is spreading west: Houthi forces struck four Saudi cities and oil installations on Tuesday, injuring 73 people, after fighting resumed with the Saudi-backed government in Yemen. A contest over one strait is becoming a struggle over two export routes and the economies that rely on them. (Guardian)

Germany’s chancellor draws a harder boundary around the AfD

Friedrich Merz has accused Alternative für Deutschland of pursuing ethnic cleansing through its policy of “remigration”, his most direct confrontation with the party since it won almost 44% in Saxony-Anhalt on Sunday. The AfD finished three seats short of an outright majority, the strongest result for a far-right party in a German state since the Nazi era. Its candidate Ulrich Siegmund is seeking support from other parties to become state premier. Merz told the Bundestag that mass expulsions based on origin or skin colour would tear through the workforce sustaining hospitals, care homes, trades and restaurants. His government still intends to deport people without a right to remain, while keeping a firm distinction for immigrants who work and have lawful residence. (Guardian)

Donald Trump congratulated the AfD and blamed Germany’s immigration rules for the result. Merz cancelled a planned call with the US president, with his office pointing to the chancellor’s objections to foreign intervention in domestic elections. The episode exposes how little room Merz has left between policy and politics. His coalition with the Social Democrats has lost support amid weak growth and high living costs, and the AfD now leads national polling at 28% to 29%. Elections in Berlin and Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania will follow this month. Mainstream parties can still deny the AfD office by refusing cooperation, yet Sunday’s vote shows that exclusion alone has not stopped its constituency from growing. (Guardian)

Economy

Two investment promises produce very different kinds of jobs

McLaren will create 1,000 positions through a £450m expansion of its technology centre in Woking, close to the plant where every McLaren road car is built. The company currently employs 2,500 people, and the new total includes agency and indirect work. Its owner, the Abu Dhabi state investment company CYVN Holdings, plans to put $2bn into the loss-making carmaker over five years after combining it with electric-vehicle startup Forseven. A product overhaul is expected to take McLaren beyond its narrow range of performance cars, with an SUV among the possibilities under chief executive Nick Collins. The commitment arrives days after Jaguar Land Rover confirmed 4,000 job losses over two years, most of them among UK salaried and management staff. (Guardian)

The investment gives Britain’s automotive sector a valuable project while its European competitors cut capacity and Chinese brands gain market share. Manufacturers still face unresolved policy costs. Electric cars exported from Britain to the EU will face a 10% tariff from early next year, proposed European subsidies exclude UK-made vehicles, and ministers are consulting on softer domestic electric-vehicle sales targets. McLaren’s plan is a wager on British engineering, software and specialised production during an industry contraction. The wider pressures remain. (Guardian)

Britain’s rapid datacentre buildout offers a sharper debate over what investment returns locally. The environmental thinktank Verdant estimates that all projects in the pipeline will directly employ 10,400 permanent workers, a quarter of the industry projection repeated by government. Its analysis of planning applications, accounts and public statements suggests existing facilities support fewer than 4,400 direct jobs and that larger future centres will need fewer staff for each megawatt of power. TechUK stands by an estimate of 43,500 current jobs and 40,000 more by 2035, based on broader economic effects. Ministers say datacentres bring computing sovereignty and secure capacity for health and defence; opponents point to water use, grid constraints and modest permanent staffing. Planning decisions need both numbers separated clearly: the temporary labour that builds a centre, and the smaller workforce that remains once its servers start running. (Guardian)

Technology

Apple unfolds its biggest iPhone gamble in years

Apple’s first foldable phone is called the iPhone Duo, a book-shaped device that opens into the company’s largest phone display and folds to roughly the size of a passport. Prices begin at $1,999 in the US and £1,999 in Britain, with the highest configuration reaching $3,200. The launch is the first major test for John Ternus, who became chief executive last week after Tim Cook stepped down and spent years leading Apple’s hardware organisation. Ternus presented the Duo as a single coherent machine inspired by the iPad, with software designed to move between its outer and inner screens. It reaches shops in October, entering a foldable category that currently represents about 2% of smartphone sales. (BBC, 9to5Mac, MacMagazine)

The familiar phones also became more expensive. The iPhone 18 Pro begins at $1,199 and the Pro Max at $1,299 in the US, with an A20 Pro chip built on a two-nanometre process, a revised rear design, improved cooling and a camera with variable aperture. A new Reference Image system can attach a cryptographic signature to photographs made by the Pro cameras, allowing recipients to inspect the original and see whether later AI edits changed it. The feature uses Apple’s Private Cloud Compute and travels with shared images, though it will not be offered in the European Union or China. Apple’s own generated or extended images will carry SynthID markers. Authentication cannot establish the circumstances outside a frame, but it gives ordinary photographs a portable record of their digital history. (9to5Mac, 9to5Mac, MacMagazine)

Apple also tied artificial intelligence more closely to devices worn and carried all day. Siri AI will search across messages and email, answer questions from personal information and take actions from the iPhone 18. Apple says much of that processing stays on the device, with protected cloud servers used when more computing power is needed. The Watch Series 12 and Ultra 4 add heart-rate readings every five seconds, readiness scores based on activity, sleep and vital signs, sound recognition, and an optional 15-second audio rewind that can transcribe something a wearer just heard. They launch on 18 September at unchanged starting prices of $399 and $799. The product case depends on convenience; the trust case depends on people understanding when microphones, memories and remote processing are active. (BBC, 9to5Mac, Verge)

AI music reaches its first industry-approved model

Suno has begun rolling out v6, its first music generator trained with the participation of major record companies. Its data includes licensed material from Warner Music Group, BMG and Believe, along with user data. The company says the system was trained from the ground up on a different collection from its earlier models, though it has not established that every disputed source has disappeared. Three versions divide the work: a free, faster mini model; a standard model; and a “wild” option intended to introduce more surprises. Users can now alter a lyric or instrument through a chat without rebuilding an entire song, combine pieces from their existing Suno library, and begin with an image, video or audio clip as well as text. (Verge)

The licensing marks a practical change after years in which generative-music companies and rights holders met mainly through litigation. It gives participating labels a negotiated route into the product and gives Suno a clearer basis for new training, while questions about user material and older systems remain. Early tests found better recognition of genre and more precise editing, alongside the synthetic vocal artefacts and overly polished timing that still distinguish generated tracks. V6 will eventually replace Suno’s previous models. The larger experiment is whether an authorised catalogue can support a mass creative tool while returning enough value and control to the people whose recordings make that tool capable. (Verge)

Nintendo gives Switch 2 a busy road into 2027

Samus Aran will return in Metroid Ravenous, a side-scrolling sequel to Metroid Dread due on Switch 2 on 28 January for $59.99 digitally. Spanish studio MercurySteam appears to be back, placing the new game at the end of Metroid’s chronology and giving Samus an ability to draw energy from enemies after she is stranded and badly injured on a moon. Nintendo will accompany it with three amiibo figures and a special edition. The company also closed its September Direct with Kirby and the World Beyond, a more open 3D adventure planned for spring 2027, the character’s 35th-anniversary year. (IGN, IGN)

The nearer releases fill out the console’s autumn. Capcom’s remakes of Resident Evil 2, 3 and 4 arrive digitally on 16 October with their expansions, followed by physical editions in January. A free Mario Kart World update is already adding ten courses drawn from the Super Nintendo original, two Knockout Tour routes and a spectator mode. Hyrule Warriors: Age of Calamity receives a Switch 2 edition in February with improved visuals, 60-frame-per-second play, more enemies, all previous downloadable content and transferable saves. The Direct mixed one substantial new sequel with ports and upgrades, a schedule that keeps current owners occupied while Nintendo’s larger 2027 games take shape. (IGN, IGN, IGN)

Sports

Ødegaard finds the opening Arsenal had been waiting for

Arsenal began another Champions League campaign with a 1–0 win at Napoli, earned when Martin Ødegaard struck off the post with 15 minutes left. Christos Tzolis and Ben White built the move before the captain supplied the precise finish that a succession of earlier chances had lacked. Bukayo Saka had an overhead kick saved, Mikel Merino missed from close range and Piero Hincapié put a free header wide. Kai Havertz later had a goal disallowed for offside. Napoli occasionally escaped through Kevin De Bruyne and Matteo Politano but spent much of the second half behind a back five as Arsenal tightened their control. (Guardian, BBC)

Ødegaard now has four goals in five matches after scoring once in all competitions last season. His return to the decisive spaces around the penalty area gives Mikel Arteta another source of goals while several new combinations settle. Arsenal won all eight league-phase fixtures last year and reached the final; an awkward away victory is a useful first step towards repeating that run. It also followed Tuesday’s air-traffic failure, which delayed the team and hundreds of supporters travelling to Naples. The journey was disorderly. Once the match settled, Arsenal waited, kept the pressure in Napoli’s half and left with the result they had come for. (Guardian, BBC)

Liverpool also opened with a recovery, beating Atlético Madrid 2–1 at Anfield after Marcos Llorente gave the visitors an early lead. Dominik Szoboszlai equalised before half-time, and Alexis Mac Allister drove the winner from 20 yards after Rio Ngumoha’s cross reached him through a deflection. The midfielder entered the game unhappy that Liverpool had renewed other contracts but not his; his performance shifted attention back to the field. Andoni Iraola’s side needed time to adjust to Atlético’s 3-5-2, then controlled the second half through Florian Wirtz’s invention, Alexander Isak’s work and a more disciplined defence. Two English contenders have started with three points. Arsenal’s came through patience, Liverpool’s through a response. (Guardian, BBC)
