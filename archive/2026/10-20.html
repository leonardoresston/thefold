Burnham orders radical public-control options for Thames Water and sets new rules for private car parks

Prime Minister Andy Burnham has instructed civil servants to tear up existing contingency plans for Thames Water and return with far bolder proposals to bring Britain's largest water supplier under public control. Shortly after taking office with a pledge to end four decades of neoliberal orthodoxy, Burnham rejected the cautious regulatory playbook he inherited in Downing Street, telling officials that decades of balance-sheet engineering and light-touch oversight had failed households and rivers alike. Thames Water, which serves 16 million customers across London and the Thames Valley, is currently asking creditors for an extra £2.4bn to stay afloat while carrying roughly £100m in unpaid regulatory fines for sewage pollution and leakage failures. (Guardian)

Among the options now under active examination in Whitehall is converting those unpaid penalties directly into a government equity stake, giving the public a foothold on the board without handing a bailout to existing institutional shareholders. Environment Secretary Angela Eagle is simultaneously preparing a water bill that will strip out a Thatcher-era statutory cap restricting how much of a privatised utility the state can own. By pairing debt-for-equity conversion with primary legislation, ministers want to avoid a prolonged legal battle with bondholders while establishing a template for utilities that fail basic public-service obligations. (Guardian)

That willingness to intervene in everyday household grievances also shaped the government's new statutory private parking code of practice, which will be laid before Parliament on Tuesday across England, Scotland and Wales. Under the rules, drivers entering a private car park will have a mandatory five-minute grace period to read the terms and decide whether to stay, plus a 10-minute window to return to their vehicle after a paid session expires. Charges for honest mistakes—such as typing a single wrong character into a registration keypad or forgetting to display a valid permit—will be capped at £20, a single independent appeals tribunal will oversee disputes, and debt-recovery firms will remain barred from adding up to £70 in extra collection fees. Consumer group Which? welcomed the protections against hair-trigger cameras after private operators issued a record 16.9 million tickets last year, a 17% jump, though the group criticised ministers for leaving the headline maximum fine at £100 rather than reviving an earlier proposal to halve it to £50. (Guardian, BBC)

While Downing Street sought to project domestic momentum—including plans led by former health secretary Alan Milburn to redirect welfare spending into guaranteed jobs and apprenticeships for under-25s—Reform UK spent Monday on the defensive. Undercover footage recorded by the investigative group Verbatim showed Dan Jukes, a senior aide to Nigel Farage, acknowledging that Farage had broken the party's official press line when he described a £5m personal gift from Thailand-based crypto billionaire Christopher Harborne as a reward for delivering Brexit. Reform's press office had insisted the transfer was strictly to fund Farage's personal security, and the contradiction deepened scrutiny at Westminster just as Reform MPs stayed off the broadcast round following an ITV investigation into racist and misogynistic remarks within the party's ranks. (Guardian, Guardian, Guardian)

France imposes night curfews in Paris and regional cities after police admit losing control

France's Prime Minister, Sébastien Lecornu, authorised local and partial night-time curfews across the country on Monday after a weekend of anti-government marches degenerated into the worst urban disorder since the summer of 2023. Following an emergency cabinet meeting at the Élysée Palace, Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez conceded that security forces had lost control in several cities after intelligence services severely misjudged the scale of Saturday's turnout. Domestic intelligence had briefed prefects to expect roughly 200,000 demonstrators nationwide; instead, more than 500,000 people took to the streets in protest against living costs, austerity budgets and political paralysis, overwhelming thin police cordons after nightfall. Nuñez offered his resignation to President Emmanuel Macron on Sunday evening, but the president refused to accept it. (Guardian, BBC)

In central Paris, the police prefecture introduced an immediate curfew from 11pm to 6am covering the Place de la République and the Les Halles shopping district, while four northern municipalities—including Lille—placed residents under stricter 8pm to 6am curfews. Prefects in Marseille, Bordeaux, Nice and Lyon rolled out targeted evening bans on public gatherings and transit stoppages after municipal officials counted hundreds of ransacked premises. In Marseille alone, local business associations estimated that more than 600 shops had their shutters forced or windows smashed over two nights of unrest, while verified footage showed groups of masked looters stripping display tables inside Apple's flagship stores on Rue Sainte-Catherine in Bordeaux and in central Lille. Lecornu promised emergency legislation to make convicted rioters financially liable for damaged municipal property and commercial stock, while trade union confederations refused to suspend their campaign against public-spending cuts and called a fresh national day of strikes and demonstrations for 5 November. (BBC, BBC, MacMagazine, Guardian)

Brazil's presidential runoff reaches a statistical dead heat after a bruising televised debate

Brazil's presidential race has narrowed to a statistical tie less than two weeks before the 25 October runoff. A BTG/Nexus survey published on Monday put Senator Flávio Bolsonaro on 50.3% of valid votes and President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva on 49.7%, shrinking the gap between the two candidates from 3.2 percentage points a week earlier to 0.6 points, well inside the two-point margin of error. Detailed precinct and polling breakdowns show that almost no voters are crossing the aisle—roughly 98% of those who backed either man in the 4 October first round remain locked into their choice. Instead, the contest is being decided by two contrasting pools: first-round supporters of third-party contenders Ronaldo Caiado and Augusto Cury are breaking heavily toward Bolsonaro, while Lula has clawed back ground by mobilising a portion of the 6.5 million voters who backed him in 2022 but stayed home three weeks ago. (Folha, Meio)

That tight arithmetic set the tone for a combative head-to-head debate on the Band network on Sunday evening, where policy discussion gave way to mutual accusations of family corruption and influence peddling. Bolsonaro repeatedly pressed Lula over ties between his son Fábio Luís, known as Lulinha, and lobbyist Roberta Luchsinger, as well as alleged favours involving Daniel Vorcaro, the former owner of Banco Master whose R$134m financial dealings are under federal investigation. Lula fired back by branding the senator a "malandro carioca", reviving questions over the Bolsonaro family's property purchases and accusing his challenger of hiding behind parliamentary privilege. Tensions spilled beyond the lecterns: Band production staff reprimanded First Lady Rosângela "Janja" da Silva after she spoke aloud from the studio audience during Bolsonaro's closing remarks, while allied federal deputy André Janones was admitted to the intensive care unit at São Paulo's Hospital Vila Nova Star with cardiac arrhythmia shortly after the broadcast. (Folha, Folha, Folha, Meio)

By Monday afternoon, the confrontation had shifted to the Superior Electoral Court (TSE) and the federal police. Campaigning in Porto Alegre, Lula announced he would formally ask the electoral court to lift banking secrecy for himself and all five of his children, challenging Bolsonaro to sign an identical waiver. Simultaneously, Lula's legal coalition filed a petition seeking the disqualification of Bolsonaro's ticket over alleged algorithmic manipulation on Instagram and Facebook, prompting TSE justice Marco Aurélio Bellizze to give Meta 24 hours to demonstrate that its recommendation systems treat both campaigns equally, while federal police searched the home of a former Superior Court of Justice clerk suspected of leaking draft decisions to lawyer Willer Tomaz, a close associate of the senator. (Folha, Folha, Folha, Folha)

Houthi missiles kill 53 in Yemen as Israel detains West Bank settlers and Kyiv warns over US missile alerts

At least 53 people were killed and more than 200 wounded on Monday when three Houthi ballistic missiles struck the al-Alam military training camp in Yemen's southern Shabwa province, marking the heaviest single loss of life since full-scale fighting resumed in mid-September. Medical officials and local commanders said the missiles hit as hundreds of newly arrived recruits were lined up on the parade ground in morning formation, overwhelming field clinics across the oil-producing province. The barrage came 24 hours after a Saudi airstrike killed nine people in Houthi-held territory, accelerating a tit-for-tat escalation that has unravelled years of fragile de-escalation talks along the Red Sea corridor. (Guardian)

Further north, Israeli police carried out a rare enforcement raid in the occupied West Bank, detaining 10 Israeli settlers after two consecutive days of high-profile attacks on foreign nationals near the Christian town of Taybeh. An Israeli magistrate's court extended the custody of six suspects following Friday's assault on veteran US photojournalist Heidi Levine, who was beaten with clubs while documenting the olive harvest and suffered four fractured vertebrae, a broken rib and a torn finger, and Saturday's attack on a delegation of European diplomats and a French mayor visiting the same hillside. In Ukraine, defence ministry adviser Serhii Beskrestnov warned that irregular and incomplete early-warning data from US satellites—compounded by lingering restrictions on Starlink terminal usage near the front—was leaving cities with only seconds to react to incoming Russian ballistic missiles after two overnight strikes hit Kyiv, while Ukrainian medics completed their first successful evacuation of a wounded soldier from a pinned-down trench using a heavy multi-rotor cargo drone. In Mombasa, meanwhile, a Kenyan court convicted self-proclaimed pastor Paul Mackenzie and 28 co-defendants on 191 counts of murder—190 of them children—over the Shakahola forest starvation deaths, where followers of his Good News International Church were ordered to fast to death in remote bushland settlements. (Guardian, BBC, BBC, BBC, Guardian)

UN biodiversity summit opens in Yerevan as UK energy grid, North Sea rigs and Thames wreck face strain

Delegates from nearly 200 countries gathered in Yerevan on Monday for the opening of the UN COP17 biodiversity summit, confronting stark evidence that governments are set to miss almost every one of the 23 nature-recovery targets agreed in Montreal four years ago. Roughly halfway to the 2030 deadline to protect 30% of the planet's land and seas, national progress filings show that the UK is on course to meet only three of the goals, with global conservation funding still tens of billions of dollars short of promised levels. The diplomatic stocktake coincided with a century-long European study confirming that wild bee populations have declined across every bioregion of the continent except the high Alps, driven by intensive agriculture, pesticide use and fragmented habitats. (Guardian, Guardian)

At home, Britain's energy and maritime infrastructure faced a trio of operational tests. An independent investigation by law firm Eversheds Sutherland into whistleblower allegations at the National Energy System Operator (Neso)—triggered after June's heatwave brought the country close to rolling blackouts—cleared senior managers of falsifying grid records, but uncovered high stress, weak governance and a culture inside the control room where engineers felt unable to challenge commercial assumptions. Separately, the Unite union warned that 48-hour strikes starting this week by hundreds of Wood Group and Apache offshore workers could disrupt British fuel supplies ahead of the 28 October budget, shutting down production across five North Sea platforms connected to the Forties Pipeline System, which carries 30% of UK crude oil. In the Thames estuary near Sheerness, maritime minister Keir Mather confirmed that specialist divers and engineers had completed a £9.5m operation to cut away all three decaying steel masts from the sunken Second World War cargo ship SS Richard Montgomery, relieving structural pressure on a hull that still holds 1,400 tonnes of unexploded munitions. (Guardian, Guardian, Guardian)

CoreWeave bonds fall into distressed territory as regulators probe private credit and Amazon funds nuclear power

Financial tremors in the artificial intelligence build-out sharpened on Monday after corporate bonds issued by CoreWeave, one of the largest specialist cloud providers renting Nvidia graphics chips to AI labs, slipped into distressed territory. CoreWeave's debt traded at yields more than 1,000 basis points above US government benchmarks while its shares fell by more than 20%, reflecting bondholder unease over how quickly depreciating server clusters can pay down more than $35bn in high-interest loans. The company has been lobbying rating agencies for an investment-grade classification that would allow it to tap deeper pools of capital from pension funds and insurers, and the sell-off highlighted how heavily the AI infrastructure boom relies on leveraged private credit. (Verge)

That same credit market is drawing closer scrutiny in Threadneedle Street. The Bank of England's Prudential Regulation Authority opened a review into whether Barclays improperly accounted for £300m of total return swap derivatives linked to its £15bn private credit portfolio, knocking 3.5% off the bank's share price ahead of its third-quarter results on Thursday. While lenders and regulators wrestle with the balance-sheet risks of data-centre financing, utilities are struggling to supply the physical megawatts. A joint study by the Environmental Defense Fund and Atlas Public Policy found that construction of fossil-fuel power plants in the United States has reached a 10-year high as grid operators turn to natural gas turbines to meet immediate data-centre demand. Seeking a longer-term alternative, Amazon signed an agreement with reactor developer X-energy and utility American Electric Power to finance a 960-megawatt small modular nuclear plant on the site of the retired Breed coal station in Fairbanks, Indiana, deploying 12 high-temperature gas-cooled Xe-100 reactors to feed its midwestern cloud regions. (Guardian, Verge, Verge)

Apple sets 28 October 'Pro and tell' hardware event, details iPhone Duo battery and buys AI audio startup

Apple sent invitations on Monday for a special hardware showcase in Los Angeles on 28 October under the tagline "Pro and tell", scheduling its second major product event in less than two months. The presentation is expected to mark the biggest redesign of the MacBook Pro in five years, introducing a thinner chassis fitted with an OLED touchscreen and a pill-shaped Dynamic Island cutout at the top of the display, alongside M6-powered 14-inch MacBook Pro and iMac updates and an OLED iPad mini. Ahead of the event, Apple released the final release candidate builds of iOS 27.2, iPadOS 27.2 and macOS 27.2 Golden Gate to developers, while European Union energy-labelling filings disclosed the exact battery architecture inside the newly launched foldable iPhone Duo: a dual-cell pack combining 2,962mAh and 1,921mAh modules for a total capacity of 4,883mAh, sitting squarely between the eSIM-only iPhone 18 Pro (4,288mAh) and the iPhone 18 Pro Max (5,567mAh). (Verge, MacStories, MacMagazine, 9to5Mac)

Separate regulatory disclosures filed with the European Commission under the Digital Markets Act confirmed that Apple has acquired Samplab, a Zurich-based artificial intelligence audio startup whose software decomposes recorded music into individual instrumental stems and allows producers to edit notes inside audio files as easily as MIDI data. The deal—Apple's second AI audio move in a week following its acqui-hire of the team behind podcast generator Huxe—points to deeper machine-learning tools inside Logic Pro and Final Cut Pro. On the legal front, haptics patent holder Taction Technologies asked a federal judge in California to award ongoing royalties through 2035 on every iPhone 18 Pro and Apple Watch sold, seeking to build on a $5.7bn jury verdict over the Taptic Engine vibration mechanism. (9to5Mac, MacMagazine, 9to5Mac)

OpenAI bugging suspect linked to Saudi officials as AI models estimate cancer survival and beat Minecraft

Federal prosecutors in California opposed bail on Monday for Abdulrahman Alfozan, a former OpenAI infrastructure engineer arrested after security staff discovered a hidden audio recorder taped behind a television inside a San Francisco conference room where company executives held sensitive meetings with US government officials. In a detention filing, the Department of Justice alleged that searches of Alfozan's devices uncovered direct communications with members of the Saudi royal household and showed that a Saudi military official had paid for a one-way flight to Doha for his family days before his arrest. Elsewhere in applied artificial intelligence, clinicians at Mass General Brigham published a study in The Lancet Digital Health detailing FAHR-FaceAge and FAHR-FaceSurvival, deep-learning models trained on 40 million facial photographs that estimate a patient's biological age and survival odds across multiple cancers from a single smartphone selfie, outperforming standard clinical frailty scores. (BBC, Guardian)

Autonomous systems also crossed a threshold in virtual worlds as four Google Gemini 4 Argon agents working together inside Minecraft defeated the game's final boss, the Ender Dragon, in just over 27 hours of continuous play without human prompts. Connected through the Mineflayer programming interface, the agents divided up resource gathering, smelting, Nether exploration and combat, writing 2,783 JavaScript routines and recovering from 605 deaths along the way. In human-made games, former Rockstar North developers reflected ahead of the 19 November launch of Grand Theft Auto VI on how the studio's decentralised culture lets individual engineers slip unscripted simulation details into the open world without central sign-off, while players of the newly released Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 4 discovered an in-game memorial honouring series co-creator Vince Zampella, and Japan's Mars Moons Exploration spacecraft launched toward Phobos to return 10 grams of Martian moon dust after arriving in August 2027. (IGN, IGN, IGN, BBC)

Secret Elizabeth Holmes documentary breaks box-office records as Apu speaks again on The Simpsons

You Can See Everything, a three-hour documentary directed by Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim and distributed by A24, took $15m across 855 North American cinemas over the weekend—the largest theatrical opening for a documentary since Michael Moore's Fahrenheit 9/11 in 2004. Kept under wraps until days before release and scheduled to open in UK cinemas on 20 November, the film centres on months of footage shot with convicted Theranos founder Elizabeth Holmes and her family before she reported to federal prison in Texas, gradually turning into a hall-of-mirrors inquiry into reality television, performance and self-deception. On the small screen, The Simpsons ended an eight-year vocal silence for convenience-store proprietor Apu Nahasapeemapetilon on Sunday night, introducing Indian-born actor Sunil Malhotra as the character's new voice alongside Janina Gavankar as his wife Manjula in an episode written with South Asian consultants, six years after Hank Azaria stepped aside. (Guardian, Guardian, Guardian, BBC)

In broadcasting and music, Christiane Amanpour announced she will leave CNN in June after 43 years as the network's defining foreign correspondent and chief international anchor, while Brazilian musicians paid tribute to samba pianist and singer Benito Di Paula, whose syncopated 1970s hits such as Retalhos de Cetim and Charlie Brown brought piano-led samba to a mass audience. Across London's autumn museum season, Dulwich Picture Gallery opened Britain's first complete exhibition of all 46 woodblock prints from Hokusai's Thirty-Six Views of Mount Fuji, Tate Modern unveiled Light and Magic, a global survey of turn-of-the-century pictorialist photography, and Senate House Library paired Charles Booth's colour-coded Victorian poverty maps with contemporary testimony from Londoners navigating temporary accommodation and private rents. (Guardian, Meio, Guardian, Guardian, Guardian)

Sports

Arsenal relish Bayern Munich test in the Champions League as Tottenham claim their first league win

Arsenal fly to Germany on Tuesday ahead of Wednesday night's Champions League meeting with Bayern Munich at the Allianz Arena, arriving with 10 victories from 11 matches across all competitions this season. Defender Ezri Konsa, who joined Mikel Arteta's side from Aston Villa for £51m in the summer, said the squad viewed the fixture as a measure of their European maturity as they prepare to mark England captain Harry Kane, who has scored 155 goals in 157 appearances since moving to Bavaria. Bayern warmed up for the tie by dismantling RB Leipzig 6–1 in the Bundesliga thanks to a hat-trick from former Liverpool forward Luis Díaz, though Vincent Kompany will be without French playmaker Michael Olise through suspension. Elsewhere in the competition, Manchester City prepare to host AEK Athens on Tuesday night short of attacking cover after forward Antoine Semenyo was ruled out for eight weeks with a knee ligament injury while Erling Haaland manages a bruised foot. (Guardian, Guardian, Guardian)

In the Premier League, Tottenham Hotspur climbed off the foot of the table on Monday night by beating Frank Lampard's Coventry City 3–1 in north London to record their first league victory of the campaign under Roberto De Zerbi. Playmaker James Maddison marked his first start in 536 days following knee surgery by opening the scoring early on, before Omar Marmoush and veteran defender Ben Davies sealed the points to ease the immediate pressure on De Zerbi. Off the pitch, England women's head coach Sarina Wiegman signed a contract extension with the Football Association through the 2029 European Championship, the Portuguese Football Federation handed Cristiano Ronaldo a 30-day suspension and a €255 fine for walking out of the national squad's training camp after a dispute with manager Jorge Jesus, and UEFA general secretary Theodore Theodoridis gave FIFA until 2 November to answer more than 50 governance questions and release financial documents on Gianni Infantino's proposal to sell a 20% stake in World Cup commercial rights to private investors. (Guardian, BBC, Guardian, Guardian, Guardian)
