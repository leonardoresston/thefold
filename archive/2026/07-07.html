NEWS

Reform's Cottrell problem moved from private help to public access

George Cottrell's repeated presence around Reform UK gave Farage's opponents a sharper line of attack after earlier questions about staff, security and accommodation. The party says Cottrell has no official role. The political difficulty is now practical: voters are being asked to accept a boundary between personal friendship and party influence while the same ally appears at political events and foreign travel. (The Guardian, BBC)

NHS consultants gave ministers a fresh strike deadline

Senior doctors in England voted for a mandate to strike over the next 12 months. Consultants are seeking higher pay and a shorter working week, creating a new labour problem for the health service after another doctors' dispute had just eased. The vote gives the British Medical Association leverage and leaves hospitals planning again for possible disruption, although stoppages are not immediate. (The Guardian, BBC)

Gaza and Iran put transition and retaliation in the same frame

Hamas offered to transfer Gaza's governing authority to a US-backed interim administration after two decades in power, while leaving open the central dispute over weapons. Israel and the US still want disarmament. In Iran, millions joined the mourning for Ali Khamenei as the former supreme leader's funeral became a display of endurance, anger and state messaging after months of conflict. (The Guardian, The Guardian)

Brazil's campaign season tightened around courts, tariffs and Congress

The PGR backed a Federal Police hearing for Flávio Bolsonaro in a slander inquiry tied to Lula, while his campaign prepared Fernanda Bolsonaro for a larger role with women voters. A judge in Goiás cut Ronaldo Caiado's security detail from 51 to four military police officers. At the STF, Alexandre de Moraes, Flávio Dino, Cristiano Zanin and Gilmar Mendes ordered state courts to explain alleged breaches of rules limiting judicial perks. (Folha Poder, Folha Poder, Folha Poder, Folha Poder)

EasyJet's takeover premium sharpened the London listings debate

EasyJet shares jumped almost 10% after the airline accepted a £5.5bn offer from Castlelake at £6.90 a share. The board is recommending the fifth approach from the US investment firm. Analysts read the deal as another sign that UK-listed companies are being bought cheaply by private capital, with a flagship low-cost carrier now heading toward the private market. (The Guardian)

Work and trade pressure stayed close to election politics

Brazilian business figures entered a US tariff hearing worried that Flávio Bolsonaro and Paulo Figueiredo could inflame negotiations over a proposed 25% levy on Brazilian products. In Brasília, Lula's new Senate leader said the debate over ending the 6x1 workweek would be a stronger campaign issue for the left than impeachment pressure against STF ministers would be for the right. (Folha Poder, Folha Poder)

Media consolidation kept moving toward fewer roofs

ITV's path toward sharing ownership with Sky captured the force of streaming economics on older British broadcasters. Netflix, Disney+ and the global platforms have made scale a survival question for companies built around national advertising markets and scheduled television. The deal marks another step away from the old commercial-TV order that kept British broadcasters largely separate. (The Guardian)

TECHNOLOGY

Microsoft's Xbox cuts became a studio sell-off

Microsoft is cutting 4,800 jobs, with more than 30% of the losses in Xbox, and is spinning off Compulsion Games, Double Fine Productions, Ninja Theory and Undead Labs. Double Fine and Compulsion are expected to keep their games as they go independent. The restructuring turns the gaming downturn into a question of ownership: studios that gave Xbox its creative depth are being pushed outside the company that bought them. (The Verge, The Verge)

Apple's beta day put Siri, privacy and supply chains in view

Apple released third betas of iOS 27, iPadOS 27, macOS Golden Gate 27, watchOS 27, tvOS 27 and visionOS 27. The iOS beta lets users personalise Siri's voice, while watchOS gains an assistant app and macOS adds new wallpapers. Broadcom also extended its Apple chip-supply agreement to 2031, and Apple renewed a China privacy campaign focused on permission controls. (MacMagazine, MacMagazine, MacMagazine, MacMagazine)

Nintendo and smart glasses showed hardware's next constraint

Nintendo will stop selling the original Switch in Europe next year as it updates Switch 2 hardware around replaceable batteries. The change points to a wider shift in device regulation and consumer expectations: longevity, repairability and privacy are becoming product features. That same pressure sits over AI smart glasses, where cameras, microphones and always-ready assistants are turning wearable convenience into a surveillance argument. (The Verge, The Verge)

CULTURE

Romantasy left BookTok and opened a shop door

Britain's first romantasy bookshop opened in Oxford with readers queuing for a genre built on romance, fantasy and online fandom. The physical shop gives BookTok's fastest-moving category a fixed address, and it also gives publishers a clearer sign that the audience wants community as much as recommendation. (The Guardian)

A cruise ban turned culture into a border dispute

Turkish authorities blocked the Scarlet Lady cruise ship from docking in Kusadasi, citing moral values as the vessel carried about 2,000 LGBTQ+ passengers and Broadway performer Patti LuPone. The decision turned a holiday itinerary into a public test of where performance, identity and local politics are allowed to meet. (The Guardian)

Fringe comedy and pop memory kept cult status alive

The Underground Monk Show returned as an Edinburgh fringe curiosity built around late-night ritual, word of mouth and comedy that works before the audience can fully explain it. Haircut 100's Fantastic Day received the opposite treatment: a bright pop single reconstructed from memory by the people who made it, including the unease of being defined by a song that carried them farther than expected. (The Guardian, The Guardian)

SPORTS

Spain ended Ronaldo's World Cup with Merino's late run

Spain reached the World Cup quarter-finals after Mikel Merino scored in stoppage time against Portugal. The goal ended Cristiano Ronaldo's hopes of another World Cup run and pushed Spain through a knockout night that had waited until the final minutes for its decisive break. (The Guardian, BBC)

England moved on from Mexico with Norway waiting

England's 3-2 win over Mexico at the Azteca left Thomas Tuchel praising belief and criticising unreliable refereeing. The FA could appeal Jarell Quansah's red card, while Jordan Henderson is out of the World Cup with a broken wrist suffered during post-match celebrations. Norway now waits in the quarter-finals, with Erling Haaland carrying the form that sent Brazil home. (The Guardian, The Guardian, The Guardian, The Guardian)

Fifa's Balogun reversal opened a wider fight

Trump confirmed he asked Infantino to review Balogun's red card before the United States faced Belgium, while Fifa lifted the one-match suspension. Uefa's anger turned a host-nation intervention into a governance conflict with implications beyond one striker and one match. (The Guardian, The Guardian, BBC)

Tottenham made Tonali its record signing

Tottenham completed a club-record deal for Sandro Tonali from Newcastle, with the initial fee at £92.5m and wages that could rise to £275,000 a week over six years. The transfer moves one of Newcastle's central midfielders to a direct Premier League rival and gives Roberto De Zerbi the statement signing he wanted. (The Guardian)

LIFESTYLE

Phone limits moved from advice to family practice

A psychotherapist who works with stressed parents described banning herself from social media and finding that her mood, attention and family relationships improved. The useful point is plain: children often feel the difference when adults stop treating the phone as the room's quiet centre of gravity. (The Guardian)

Summer food stayed close to heat and travel

Crete's family-run tavernas, bakeries and old olive culture made the island a cook's route through Greek summer food. At home, runner beans moved through Indian sabzi and thoran recipes, while prawn and feta saganaki became a quick salad for hot weather. Even the pizza-oven test came with the same lesson: summer eating rewards preparation before appetite arrives. (The Guardian, The Guardian, The Guardian, The Guardian)

A dishwasher leak became an eight-month housing problem

A John Lewis dishwasher installation left an elderly couple's home uninhabitable and forced them into hotels for months while a repair and insurance claim dragged on. The case sits in the everyday consumer category that becomes serious only after the customer has already lost the use of a home, the money for repairs and the patience to wait. (The Guardian)
