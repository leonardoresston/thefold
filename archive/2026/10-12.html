Trump declares an energy ceasefire that Ukraine has not seen and Russia has not observed

Donald Trump announced an immediate ceasefire covering long-range attacks on energy infrastructure in the war between Russia and Ukraine, saying both sides had agreed. Volodymyr Zelenskyy learnt of it through the announcement. The Ukrainian president offered to comply if Vladimir Putin did the same and asked when the arrangement would begin. Moscow gave no public confirmation. Russian drones remained in the air on Sunday, air strikes continued, and thermal power station No 5 in Kharkiv was hit. Zelenskyy said more than 200 attacks had struck Ukrainian electricity generation and transmission facilities during the week, alongside 17 hits on oil and gas sites. The distance between a presidential post and events on the ground could hardly have been wider. (Guardian, BBC)

The proposed pause arrives after a sharp rupture between Washington and Kyiv. Trump lifted sanctions on Russian diesel exports on Friday, presenting the decision as a way to bring down fuel costs before the US midterm elections. When Zelenskyy objected that new energy revenue would finance Russia's war, Trump demanded that Ukraine stop attacking Russian refineries and called for the country to choose a new leader. Kyiv's strikes on refineries, storage facilities and data centres have become one of its most effective ways to impose an economic cost inside Russia. Zelenskyy says any halt must be reciprocal and backed by consequences if Moscow breaks it. He has also offered elections during a total 60-day ceasefire, leaving the immediate question with the Kremlin: whether it will accept limits on the campaign that is damaging Ukraine's power network as winter approaches. (Guardian, BBC)

The pressure surrounding the offer matters as much as its wording. European officials believe Washington has threatened to disrupt the procurement system supplying Ukraine with weapons unless Kyiv stops its deep strikes, while US negotiators have explored economic incentives for Russia. Zelenskyy's team left talks in Florida early after being surprised by the diesel decision. He spent Sunday rebuilding support with leaders in Britain, Canada, Poland, France, Finland and the European Commission. Downing Street called for Russia to end attacks on Ukrainian infrastructure and Black Sea shipping while keeping sanctions pressure in place. Europe is being asked to sustain that position as the United States tries to set the terms through fuel policy, weapons access and public demands on Kyiv. (Guardian)

Britain's prime minister confirms Russian interference in the Brexit vote, without showing the evidence

Andy Burnham has confirmed that Russia interfered in the 2016 Brexit referendum, following a question he put to officials after entering Downing Street. The prime minister did not identify who gave him the assessment, describe the evidence or claim that the activity changed the result. His statement still marks a break with previous Conservative governments, which maintained that there was no evidence of successful Russian interference. Parliament's intelligence and security committee reached a narrower but troubling conclusion in 2020: ministers and intelligence agencies had failed to seek a proper assessment, even though Russian state media and social-media accounts had tried to influence British politics. That left the public without the kind of post-election investigation conducted in the United States. (Guardian)

The government is creating a National Centre for Information Defence to detect and disrupt hostile information campaigns, alongside a British-German partnership focused on hybrid threats. Burnham linked the disclosure to current concerns about Russian activity in European politics after discussing Alternative für Deutschland with German chancellor Friedrich Merz. A useful public reckoning will require more than confirmation from an unnamed briefing. Britain needs to distinguish an attempt to interfere from evidence that it worked, explain which institutions missed the activity and show how newer defences would respond. Ten years after the referendum, releasing that account would strengthen confidence in future votes without reopening the ballot itself. (Guardian)

Two attacks on Riyadh airport pull the Yemen war into a dangerous new phase

A projectile hit terminal 4 at Riyadh's King Khalid international airport on Sunday, killing one person, injuring 11 and starting a fire. Saudi air defences intercepted a cruise missile nearby. The strike followed a Houthi missile attack at the same airport on Saturday that killed 13 people and injured more than 300, the deadliest attack on a Gulf country since the US–Iran war began in March. Saudi authorities kept the airport closed, moved schools online until Thursday and confirmed that King Fahd airport in Dammam had also been attacked. The Houthis warned airlines and passengers away from Saudi airspace as Saudi strikes continued in Yemen. (Guardian, BBC)

The escalation is drawing outside powers closer. Around 200 US military advisers already support Saudi targeting and intelligence, and Trump is considering direct participation in the campaign. Britain has offered aerial refuelling for aircraft intercepting Houthi missiles; Saudi Arabia has sought further help from France, Egypt, Pakistan and the UK as interceptor stocks run low. The risk extends beyond airports. About a tenth of global maritime trade passes through the Bab al-Mandab strait, where renewed Houthi attacks on shipping would add another shock to transport and energy costs. Saudi Arabia intervened in Yemen in 2015 and returned to air strikes after the Houthis advanced along the Red Sea coast in September. The weekend's civilian deaths make a wider military answer more likely even though a decade of bombardment failed to dislodge the group. (Guardian, BBC)

Brazil's runoff moves from a close count to a fight over turnout and the rules of campaigning

Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva filled 12 blocks of Avenida Paulista with a March for Democracy aimed at younger voters and people who stayed home in the first round. The PT's route back from a narrow defeat runs through lower abstention and a stronger ground campaign: Flávio Bolsonaro won 47.03% of the first-round vote against Lula's 45.16%, and the first Datafolha runoff poll puts the pair at 52% and 48% among valid votes. Students, fan groups, social movements and leftwing parties organised Sunday's rally. Lula acknowledged that his campaign had lost touch with younger Brazilians and promised to give them a larger role in a new government. The crowd was large enough for the metro to close Trianon-Masp station, giving his campaign a visible lift after a subdued start to the second round. (Folha)

At the same time, a fight inside the electoral court turned WhatsApp into the campaign's latest battleground. TSE president Kassio Nunes Marques ordered the suspension of the pro-Lula channel Exército do Lula, which has more than three million followers, after Bolsonaro's campaign accused it of coordinating false claims and mass sharing. Electoral inspector Ricardo Villas Bôas Cueva reversed the ban hours later, saying the evidence did not justify shutting the entire channel and criticising an attempt to choose the judge who would hear the application. The channel may operate until the full court reviews the dispute, though material already ruled unlawful remains prohibited. The episode exposed disagreement among the judges and handed each campaign a grievance: Lula says the court is silencing voters; Bolsonaro says it is tolerating disinformation. With the candidates separated by a few points, rulings on digital mobilisation now carry immediate electoral weight. (Folha)

A boat disaster off Djibouti kills 139 people on a route that receives little attention

At least 139 people, including 15 children, died when a boat carrying 305 migrants capsized near Guhere, north of the Djiboutian fishing town of Obock. Eighty-four people remain missing and 82 survived, all of them Ethiopian nationals. The vessel was heading from Djibouti towards Yemen during the night of 8–9 October. Search and rescue work continues while local authorities provide medical care and arrange burials; the International Organization for Migration is supporting survivors with food, healthcare and counselling. The agency describes it as one of the deadliest recorded disasters on the eastern migration route between the Horn of Africa and the Arabian peninsula. (Guardian, BBC)

Hundreds of thousands of people, mostly from Ethiopia and Somalia, use the route each year to seek work in Gulf countries, especially Saudi Arabia. Conflict, climate shocks and poverty push some from home; smugglers then crowd them into boats for the Red Sea and Gulf of Aden crossing. Travellers also die during long walks in extreme heat. The direction of travel can make the route easy to overlook from Europe, yet it carries most migration out of the Horn of Africa and is among the world's busiest. Sunday's toll is a measure of both its scale and the thin margin between a journey and a mass casualty event. (Guardian)

Volkswagen asks Europe to turn industrial policy into an advantage at home

Volkswagen chief Oliver Blume has backed the EU's proposed Industrial Accelerator Act, which would steer subsidies and public purchasing towards products made with a substantial share of European materials and manufacturing. The request comes with Europe's car industry under pressure from lower-priced Chinese brands, expensive energy, weak consumer demand and the cost of developing electric vehicles quickly. Volkswagen is planning as many as 100,000 job cuts in the largest restructuring in its history. It returned to the Paris motor show for the first time in 20 years with the electric ID Tiguan, a launch intended to show that the company can still produce a mass-market answer during that contraction. (Guardian)

The proposal would reward companies that develop technology, build vehicles and employ people inside the bloc while preserving access to the market for overseas competitors. It follows an EU agreement with China designed to halve Chinese hybrid sales in Europe. That combination marks a more active industrial policy: Brussels is using procurement, subsidies and negotiated limits to protect capacity that it considers economically and strategically important. The challenge is to give European factories room to invest without sheltering them from the competition that has made Chinese electric cars cheaper. Consumers will judge the policy through price and quality; governments will also count jobs, supply chains and the ability to make essential technology within Europe. (Guardian)

Technology

Apple is preparing to put a screen, a speaker and Siri at the centre of the home

Apple's Welcome Home event on Tuesday is expected to introduce HomeView, a smart display built around an A18 chip and a heavily revised Siri. The device resembles the old iMac G4: a screen raised above a speaker base, with a separate version designed for wall mounting. The freestanding model is expected in space grey, silver, starlight and pink, while the wall model comes in the two darker neutral finishes. Voice will be the main control, and the display is meant to be read from across a room. Apple apps will run directly, with iPhone widgets providing a route to some third-party services; there is no conventional App Store. Announcement and delivery may be separated by a month, with shipments expected around mid-November. (9to5Mac, Verge)

The surrounding products show the size of the bet. A refreshed HomePod mini is due on 30 October, a new Apple TV 4K has been tested with built-in microphones and a Siri Remote that contains its own speaker, and LG is preparing compatible thermostats, locks and doorbells. A homeOS platform may bind them together. Apple has long supplied the software layer for smart homes through HomeKit while leaving the most visible hardware to other companies. HomeView would give it a permanent interface in the room and a controlled place to demonstrate the new Siri. That also raises the standard for reliability: lights, locks, cameras and household routines expose failures more quickly than a phone assistant answering an occasional question. (9to5Mac, 9to5Mac, 9to5Mac)

Grand Theft Auto V became a place people live in, and that changes the task facing its sequel

Grand Theft Auto V has sold more than 230m copies, generated about $10bn for Take-Two and stayed active across three console generations since its 2013 release. Its longevity came from two distinct achievements. The story campaign made Los Santos a vast single-player playground built around three protagonists and a quick character-switching system. GTA Online then turned the same city into a social platform where players could stage robberies, build businesses, perform vehicle stunts, make films or simply meet friends. Regular expansions gave that community reasons to return, while player-made rituals and subcultures supplied the part no release calendar could plan. Millions still play each week, more than a decade after launch. (IGN)

That history makes Grand Theft Auto VI an unusual sequel. Rockstar must persuade players to leave a mature world filled with years of possessions, friendships and shared stories, then make the replacement feel open enough to produce another culture of its own. The previous game succeeded partly because it escaped the boundaries of a crime simulator. Its friendlier driving, broad countryside and exaggerated missions gave players tools for invention, and its online economy kept funding new work. A larger map or sharper graphics can set the stage for the next game. Its real test will arrive after launch, when players decide whether the new city can become a dependable meeting place as well as a spectacular setting. (IGN)

Culture

The shingles vaccine has become a hopeful bet against dementia before the science is settled

Bookings for shingles vaccinations have more than doubled at Boots in a year and risen 41% at Superdrug, driven partly by studies suggesting the jab may reduce dementia risk. A natural experiment in Wales found dementia rates 8.5% lower over seven years among women eligible for the older Zostavax vaccine; because about half of that group was vaccinated, researchers estimated the effect among recipients at close to 20%. An Oxford study of more than 200,000 US health records found 17% fewer dementia diagnoses after the newer Shingrix vaccine than after Zostavax, equivalent to an extra 164 days without the condition over six years. Possible explanations include preventing viral inflammation in the brain and helping the immune system clear damaged cells. (Guardian)

Those are promising associations, and they remain short of clinical proof. A large English hospital-record study found no protective effect from Zostavax, while researchers still do not know whether any benefit comes from suppressing the shingles virus, from the immune-boosting ingredient in Shingrix or from differences between people who seek vaccination and those who do not. Trials in Finland and Denmark should provide firmer answers, though results are years away. Shingrix is approved to prevent a painful disease caused by reactivation of the chickenpox virus. It is available free on the NHS to eligible groups; many people under 65 pay about £450 for two doses. Alzheimer’s Research UK does not recommend the jab specifically for dementia prevention and continues to emphasise exercise, diet and lifelong mental activity. The sensible conclusion is hopeful but narrow: protect against shingles when eligible, and let the dementia claim pass through proper trials. (Guardian)

Ian Rankin leaves Edinburgh to investigate the guarded world of London's super-rich

The Heights, Ian Rankin's new novel, moves away from the Edinburgh streets of John Rebus and into a Knightsbridge apartment building made for the extremely wealthy. Its cinema, spa, marble entrance and contemporary art form the sealed setting for the murder of a concierge. The investigator is DS Gillian Gish, a new lead whose care for her mother and life in Tottenham place her across London's divided neighbourhoods. Rankin drew on Columbo for a detective who unsettles people accustomed to privacy, deference and control. The relocation gives him access to a louder performance of wealth than Edinburgh would allow and keeps his longstanding interest in crime as a way of reading power, class and public life. (Guardian)

At 66, Rankin is carrying forward the working habits behind 26 Rebus novels: an old, repeatedly repaired laptop, a small desk bought on Charing Cross Road in 1987 and long stretches of focused writing at home. Success has widened the life around the work. He and his wife Miranda direct 30–40% of his annual net income to a charitable trust supporting smaller organisations, often those helping people with special needs. Their younger son, Kit, has Angelman syndrome and lives nearby in a care facility. Rebus himself was last seen in prison, though Rankin points to the promise of good news from his lawyer and considers his release the story's happy ending. That leaves two detectives available for whatever he writes next, one rooted in the city that made him and another built to cross the social distances of London. (Guardian)

Sports

Manchester City answer Anfield's anger with three goals and a perfect record

Manchester City maintained their perfect Premier League start with a 3-0 win at Liverpool, a match played under the shadow of the independent commission that found City guilty on virtually all of the 115 financial-rule charges against them. City have appealed, and Anfield supplied a relentless chorus of taunts and banners about relegation. The game stayed tight until Ryan Gravenberch received a second yellow card in the 62nd minute. Erling Haaland put City ahead, substitute Jérémy Doku curled in the second and Rayan Cherki completed a flowing move with a controlled finish after Josko Gvardiol's backheel. The visitors absorbed the hostility, then used the extra man with the cold efficiency that has carried them through the opening weeks. (Guardian, BBC)

Liverpool's first defeat under Andoni Iraola came with three of their strongest performers missing after the international break: Dominik Szoboszlai, Cody Gakpo and Alexander Isak. Their attack lacked the weight to trouble City before the dismissal and the match opened too far afterwards. Doku's arrival six minutes before the red card proved decisive, stretching the left side and contributing to the move for Cherki's goal as well as scoring his own. The result is clean; its place in the season is unsettled. City's appeal leaves the eventual sporting consequences unknown, which means every point is being won inside a competition whose final table may be revised elsewhere. For now, the team has seven wins from seven and the most difficult away result of its campaign. (Guardian, BBC)
