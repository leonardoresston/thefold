LEAD STORY

Hormuz returned to the centre of the US-Iran conflict

The US insisted the Strait of Hormuz remained open after Iran declared it closed and exchanged strikes with American forces and US allies in the region. The escalation put the Gulf's main oil route back at the centre of a conflict already spreading through shipping, energy and food markets. (BBC)

The wider pressure is already showing up in aid and agriculture. The World Food Programme is feeding 1.5 million fewer people this year, while millions more in Somalia and Afghanistan are struggling to meet basic food needs; fertiliser exports through the strait leave harvests exposed to every new round of escalation. (The Guardian)

NEWS

Police tried to slow speculation in the Widdecombe inquiry

Senior police figures warned against public speculation over Ann Widdecombe's killing after Devon and Cornwall police said there was no evidence of terrorism or political motivation. Officers kept the motive open while asking politicians and the public to avoid adding distress for the family or pressure on the investigation. (The Guardian)

A donation cap would hit Reform UK hardest

Reform UK would have kept only 15% of last year's donations if a proposed £100,000 political funding cap had been in force. Analysis of Electoral Commission data put the party's take at £4.1m instead of £26.7m, sharpening the argument over wealthy backers before the Commons funding debate. (The Guardian)

The Hillsborough law moved back to the Commons

Keir Starmer is expected to use his final week in office to push the Hillsborough law through its remaining Commons stages. The bill is designed to help families seek justice after major disasters and create offences for officials who mislead the public or obstruct accountability; the 1989 Hillsborough crush killed 97 fans. (The Guardian, BBC)

Two men died trying to rescue children at Seaton Carew

Two men died after entering the sea at Seaton Carew beach in Hartlepool to help children in difficulty. Cleveland police said the children were safe after hospital checks, while the men were pronounced dead after being brought from the water. (The Guardian, BBC)

Spain's wildfires trapped a British couple in Almería

A British couple were found badly burned and semi-conscious in a ravine during the Almería wildfires. The fires have killed at least 13 people, burned more than 6,000 hectares and left at least 23 people missing, turning a holiday hiking area into a search-and-rescue zone. (The Guardian)

Brazil's election year returned to São Bernardo

Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva plans to open his re-election campaign in São Bernardo do Campo, the industrial city where his union and political life began. The choice places the campaign launch inside the founding geography of the Workers' party and the labour movement that made him president. (Folha Poder)

The Brazilian right kept circling Flávio Bolsonaro

Jair Bolsonaro's letter naming Flávio Bolsonaro as his presidential candidate and political voice became another attempt to settle a family split that had grown around Michelle Bolsonaro. PL president Valdemar Costa Neto defended the letter by saying the elder Bolsonaro still held the votes needed to elect his son. (Folha Poder, Folha Poder)

Dino's emendas order reached Eduardo Cunha

Justice Flávio Dino ordered the blocking of R$6.1m from former speaker Eduardo Cunha in an investigation into congressional amendments. In the electoral courts, PT, PL and other parties also asked the TSE to exclude presidential, Senate and state-government races from calculations on minimum campaign-fund shares for women and Black candidates. (Folha Poder, Folha Poder)

A super El Niño warning reached food prices

Economists warned that a possible super El Niño could deliver a global food-price shock lasting into 2028. The weather risk lands as the Iran war has already helped push world food prices to a three-year high, leaving supply chains exposed to climate and conflict at the same time. (The Guardian)

Britain's largest community solar farm was switched off

The Derril Water project in north Devon, billed as Britain's biggest community solar farm, was ordered to shut for its first summer over fears of local grid overload. Members now face an estimated £2m in lost revenue as heat pushed power systems across Europe into the spotlight. (The Guardian)

Brazil's delivery-app market showed the cost of flexibility

Brazil's app-delivery workforce grew sixfold between 2015 and 2021, while average income fell 27%. The figures put platform work back in the economic debate over regulation, exhaustion and the gap between promised autonomy and fragile rights. (Canal Meio)

TECHNOLOGY

The AI datacentre fight moved from forecast to local politics

Resistance to AI datacentres is becoming a practical dispute over electricity, water and land before the buildout reaches full speed. The industry wants large new facilities for model training and cloud services; local campaigns are already treating the projects as infrastructure decisions with neighbourhood costs. (The Verge)

Apple's abandoned car left an AI-chip inheritance

Apple's self-driving car programme ended without a vehicle, but its on-device AI work fed into the company's chip ambitions. The car processor was never finished; the engineering effort helped shape powerful AI silicon now central to Apple's hardware story. (The Verge)

Consumer AI kept pressing into social platforms and phones

Meta AI reached Instagram story fonts and other account tools, while the iPhone's position in the generative-AI race remained under close comparison with rivals. On stage in Madrid, Lorde turned AI glasses into a pop-culture objection, showing how quickly ambient cameras and assistants have become a taste question as well as a privacy one. (MacMagazine, MacMagazine, The Verge)

Fake news pages became investment-scam bait

Fraudsters are circulating cloned news articles on social platforms to steer readers towards investment sites. The false pages imitate publishers and celebrities, then redirect attention to crypto, stocks and trading platforms designed to look like a missed opportunity. (The Guardian)

CULTURE

Lowry received a 140-painting correction

A new LS Lowry exhibition aims to push back against the old caricature of the Manchester painter as naive or uncultured. LS Lowry: the Theatre of Life brings together 140 paintings focused on working-class life in the industrial north-west across the early and mid 20th century. (The Guardian)

The Odyssey moved from school text to summer blockbuster

Christopher Nolan's film has put Homer's Odyssey back into the centre of popular culture, with trailers promising Cyclops spectacle, the land of the dead and large-scale mythic action. The 2,600-year-old poem is again functioning as a shared template for cinema, television and fantasy worlds. (The Guardian)

Bugsy Malone turned 50 with its chaos intact

Alan Parker's child-cast gangster musical reached its 50th anniversary with the production stories still sounding as unruly as the film. The musical spoof used cream-firing splurge guns, teen performers and a full gangster-film vocabulary to make one of British cinema's strangest family classics. (The Guardian)

Television kept testing tech power as entertainment

The Tech Billionaire Takeover turned crypto money, political influence and billionaire governance fantasies into a documentary hour, while The Westies brought another real-life crime crew to prestige television. Culture is still drawing drama from the same place as politics: private power with public consequences. (The Guardian, The Guardian)

SPORTS

Bellingham carried England into the World Cup semi-finals

Jude Bellingham's extra-time winner sent England past Norway and into the World Cup semi-finals. Thomas Tuchel questioned the performance afterwards, and Bellingham pushed back, leaving England with a place in the last four and a tension to cool before Argentina. (The Guardian, The Guardian)

Argentina set up the old World Cup grudge match

Julián Álvarez's extra-time strike beat 10-man Switzerland and carried Argentina into a semi-final against England. The first competitive meeting between the countries since 2002 now goes to Atlanta, with Lionel Messi's champions facing Tuchel's side for a place in the final. (The Guardian, The Guardian)

Fifa opened the door to a 64-team World Cup

Gianni Infantino said a 64-team men's World Cup would be examined after the 2026 tournament, calling the 48-team version a huge success and arguing for a tournament built for the whole world. The idea would add another 16 teams before the 2030 event if it survives committee scrutiny. (The Guardian, BBC)

Ken Bates's Chelsea era received its final accounting

Ken Bates, the former Chelsea owner and chairman who bought the club for £1 in 1982, died aged 94. His two decades at Stamford Bridge helped return Chelsea to the top rank of English football, and ended with the Roman Abramovich sale that changed the Premier League's money age. (The Guardian)

LIFESTYLE

Channel crossings braced for the summer border test

France and the UK agreed to increase staffing at border controls before new fingerprint and facial-recognition checks collide with the holiday rush. MPs warned of long tailbacks unless the EU's entry-exit system is fixed or checks are suspended at peak pressure points. (The Guardian)

A heat bill put workplace temperatures into politics

Green MP Hannah Spencer plans to introduce legislation that would create an independent body to recommend maximum safe workplace temperatures. The bill arrives as repeated heatwaves turn offices, shops, warehouses and outdoor shifts into a daily health-and-safety argument. (The Guardian)

Denmark's Cold Hawaii widened beyond surfers

The north-west Jutland coast, long known to surfers as Cold Hawaii, is drawing art and travel attention around a 50km shoreline of surf spots, galleries and North Sea weather. The destination is being sold as a cooler European summer route at a moment when heat is changing holiday choices. (The Guardian)

London's novelty-restaurant economy met a hard review

Kawan, the Chinatown restaurant from Nigel Ng, opened with a large online audience behind it and a menu built for attention. The early verdict turned the launch into a warning about converting platform fame into repeat custom at London prices. (The Guardian)
