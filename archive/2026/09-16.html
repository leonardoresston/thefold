The warning system that never switched on

The public inquiry into Lucy Letby has found a complete failure to protect babies at the Countess of Chester hospital, built from missed connections, ignored clinical concerns and a leadership culture that guarded its own account of events. Three newborns died in two weeks in June 2015, as many deaths as the neonatal unit would normally see in a year. By August, after a fourth unexpected death, the total was already the highest since 2008. Senior staff reviewed that case without connecting it to the earlier cluster. An abnormal insulin result for another baby was then disregarded. Lady Justice Thirlwall concluded that safeguarding action at that point could have protected ten later babies, including three who died. (BBC, Guardian)

The sequence continued after doctors began linking Letby to the rise in deaths and serious collapses. Senior nurses dismissed their concerns, managers delayed contacting police, and consultants were drawn into a grievance process after Letby was eventually moved from the unit in July 2016. Parents remained unaware for years that their children might have been deliberately harmed. Executives presented the board with a softened version of the growing crisis, while the Care Quality Commission inspected the hospital in February 2016 without receiving crucial information. The regulator was also criticised for failing to look beyond what it was given. Police were called in April 2017, nearly two years after the first cluster. Three former executives are now under investigation on suspicion of gross negligence manslaughter, alongside a corporate manslaughter inquiry. (BBC, Guardian)

The report makes 17 recommendations. Neonatal cots and incubators should have cameras that parents can access, insulin storage should be protected by biometric controls or CCTV, and every NHS organisation should follow a formal policy when deliberate harm is suspected. Ministers are also preparing rules that would prevent executives found to have failed in their duties from moving into other NHS jobs. A single tracker is intended to show whether maternity and neonatal inquiry recommendations have actually been completed. Similar proposals have accumulated across three decades of health-service scandals; the inquiry’s hardest institutional finding is that guidance has little value when the people receiving a warning do nothing with it. (BBC, Guardian)

Letby, 36, is serving 15 whole-life terms after convictions for murdering seven babies and attempting to murder seven others. She is seeking to overturn those convictions, and the Criminal Cases Review Commission is considering whether new expert evidence should send the case back to the court of appeal. Her barrister argues that the inquiry and the government moved too quickly while that challenge remains unresolved. Thirlwall’s remit examined the hospital’s response; a separate process is considering the safety of the verdicts. Whatever the review concludes about the convictions, the duty exposed here remains immediate: a hospital does not need certainty of a crime before it protects patients, preserves evidence and asks an independent authority to examine a credible pattern of harm. (Guardian)

Brazil’s highest court breaks into open conflict

Brazil’s Supreme Court has suspended its extraordinary session on Alexandre de Moraes with the bench split, its internal struggle broadcast live to millions and a presidential election less than three weeks away. The justices had gathered to decide whether to open an investigation into Moraes over messages in the Banco Master case. Flávio Dino requested more time, bringing the proceedings to a halt after hours of argument. Kassio Nunes Marques and Dias Toffoli had already recused themselves because material taken from former banker Daniel Vorcaro’s phone mentioned people close to them. The hearing then veered from evidence and procedure into direct accusations among the judges, including exchanges between Moraes, André Mendonça and Gilmar Mendes. (Folha, Guardian)

The underlying case began with the collapse of Banco Master and Vorcaro’s arrest last November as he prepared to board a private jet. The investigation has since reached political and judicial circles. A police dossier contains WhatsApp exchanges attributed to Vorcaro and Moraes, as well as a $25m contract between the bank and the law firm of Moraes’s wife. Mendonça is accused by rivals of accelerating scrutiny of Moraes while moving more slowly on Vorcaro’s connections to the Bolsonaro family and the financing of a film about former president Jair Bolsonaro. Moraes calls the dossier a politically driven attack and has sought scrutiny of Mendonça’s own conduct. Each side is therefore arguing about the evidence while also disputing who may legitimately examine it. (Guardian, Folha)

The election has turned that institutional crisis into campaign material. Polls point to a close contest between President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva and Flávio Bolsonaro, with a likely second round on 25 October. Moraes was appointed by Michel Temer, but his role in prosecuting Jair Bolsonaro over the attempted coup after the 2022 election has made him a symbol of the court for both camps. Flávio Bolsonaro used Tuesday’s broadcast to link the scandal to Lula while leaving his own alleged connections to Vorcaro out of his message. The court now needs a process that can command trust after the spectacle: clear recusal rules, equal access to the material being judged and a decision that can be explained without reference to electoral loyalties. Suspending the session bought time. It also extended the period in which Brazil’s judges, candidates and voters are all fighting over the same evidence. (Guardian, Folha)

Yemen’s war closes in on people and shipping

Renewed fighting in Yemen has displaced an estimated 100,000 people and brought a second day of warnings over Houthi attacks on Saudi cities. The Iran-aligned group claimed a large strike with ballistic missiles and drones against King Khalid airbase in southern Saudi Arabia; air-raid sirens sounded across six governorates, including Jeddah. Within Yemen, anti-Houthi forces remain divided among the recognised government, Saudi-backed formations and groups previously supported by the United Arab Emirates. The UN Security Council has called for restraint as the next major confrontation gathers around Marib. For families forced from home, that diplomatic language arrives alongside a familiar danger: another phase of war beginning before the country has recovered from the last one. (Guardian)

The conflict is also tightening two of the world’s most important sea passages. Houthi advances threaten the Bab al-Mandab entrance to the Red Sea while commercial traffic through the Strait of Hormuz remains severely restricted by the wider US-Iran war. Only four ships crossed Hormuz on Monday, compared with a prewar average of about 130, and an attack has closed Saudi Aramco’s East-West pipeline to the Red Sea port of Yanbu. Brent crude moved above $108 a barrel after reports that loadings at Yanbu had stopped. Qatar is trying to revive talks between Iran and Gulf states on a protected shipping route, with China drawn into efforts to resolve the Hormuz blockade. Every delay leaves humanitarian access, energy exports and commercial shipping dependent on armed groups and governments that are currently exchanging fire. (Guardian)

A shared anxiety about Britain’s racial divide

A large study has found broad agreement that racial inequality remains a serious British problem, alongside sharply different ideas about who benefits from the present system. Sixty-two per cent of minority ethnic respondents and 57% of white respondents considered racial inequality serious. Nearly half of minority ethnic people said they had experienced prejudice based on race or nationality during the previous year, most often in public, on social media or at work; the rate was higher among 18- to 24-year-olds. The research combined online surveys of 2,009 white adults and 3,014 minority ethnic adults with 12 focus groups. Age, income and politics often produced deeper differences than ethnicity itself, and respondents across groups identified the gap between rich and poor as the country’s most important divide. (Guardian)

The disagreement becomes clearer around preferential treatment. Thirty-seven per cent of white respondents believed minority groups were treated better than white British people, while 33% disagreed; among minority ethnic respondents, the figures were 23% and 48%. Black participants were generally more positive about progress than people of Bangladeshi or Pakistani heritage, whose anxiety was linked in part to the 2024 riots. Women were more pessimistic than men. British Future, which conducted the Nuffield Foundation-funded study, wants the government to challenge zero-sum ideas, support local initiatives and press Ofcom over online racism. The findings offer a practical starting point: most people recognise a problem, but a growing share interpret remedies as benefits taken from somebody else. Any durable policy will have to address unequal outcomes while showing plainly how fairness is measured. (Guardian)

Britain reopens the case for a global defence bank

The UK is considering joining a Canada-led defence, security and resilience bank only weeks after the Treasury rejected the proposal. Canada has invited Britain and eight other countries, including Ukraine and Turkey, to become founding members. The bank aims to raise about €100bn and use sovereign backing to offer low-cost loans for government projects and defence contractors, with guarantees for smaller suppliers that struggle to obtain finance. Chancellor John Healey has already discussed it with Canadian finance minister François-Philippe Champagne and is due to meet him again this week. The decision has become more urgent as Britain tries to increase military capacity without promising a date for defence spending to reach 3% of national income. (Guardian)

Britain has already committed £600m to a separate Multilateral Defence Mechanism with the Netherlands, Finland and Poland, designed to cut procurement costs and support stockpiling. The previous Treasury position favoured merging the two schemes and questioned whether a new institution aimed partly at lower-rated countries and small companies would solve Britain’s procurement failures. Joining the Canadian plan could require £1bn to £2bn over three years. That is substantial public capital, though the attraction is leverage: a common bank can pool risk, place larger orders and finance suppliers beyond the reach of national budgets. The government says the two lenders should complement each other. It now needs to define the difference between them, the liability Britain would accept and the industrial capacity that each pound of backing is expected to buy. (Guardian)

Technology

Apple bundles its way into new markets

Apple is adding Apple TV and Apple Arcade to the basic 50GB iCloud+ subscription at no extra charge in more than 100 countries. The offer will roll out during September and takes Apple TV into 59 markets where the streaming service was previously unavailable, raising its global reach to 170 countries. Some subscribers will also receive Apple Music Select, a smaller tier built around ad-free radio stations, with a discounted route into the full music service. The US is excluded, and the country list is concentrated in smaller and more price-sensitive markets. Apple is effectively using cloud storage, already a routine monthly purchase, as the distribution channel for entertainment services that would be harder to sell individually. (9to5Mac, MacMagazine)

Microsoft, meanwhile, has called its first major Windows event in more than two years for 7 October in San Francisco. Satya Nadella will appear with Windows and Surface chief Pavan Davuluri and Nvidia chief Jensen Huang for a discussion of local AI on the PC. Nvidia’s presence points towards RTX Spark machines and possible details for the Surface Laptop Ultra, while improvements to Windows quality are also expected. The previous major event, in May 2024, introduced Arm-based Copilot Plus PCs and renewed Microsoft’s Qualcomm partnership. This one arrives after manufacturers have spent two years selling AI hardware ahead of a settled reason for most people to replace a working laptop. Local models can offer faster responses and keep more data on the device; the October presentation has to turn those advantages into useful tasks that feel worth the new silicon. (Verge)

Fable survives a decade of upheaval

Fable will return on 23 February 2027, ending a ten-year absence for one of Xbox’s most distinctly British series. Microsoft closed original developer Lionhead in 2016 after cancelling the multiplayer project Fable Legends. Playground Games, known for Forza Horizon, began assembling a new team in 2017 and carried the reboot through the pandemic, shifting industry fashions and repeated rounds of cuts across Xbox. More than 6,000 jobs disappeared across Microsoft’s gaming business during the game’s development, alongside studio closures and abandoned projects. Playground’s continuing success with Forza, and Xbox’s belief that Fable belongs to the platform’s core identity, gave the production protection that many other long projects did not receive. (Guardian, IGN)

The result keeps the old series’ mix of consequential choices, comic fantasy and recognisably modern British speech. Richard Ayoade, Matt King and Natasia Demetriou are among the cast, with improvisation and swearing allowed to pull the dialogue away from familiar medieval solemnity. Combat spans melee weapons, bows and magic, while the story leaves room to wander through Albion before tightening around a final decision with a personal cost. Playground had transferable experience in building open worlds, though creating a narrative role-playing team from scratch required hundreds of hires and international relocations. That long gestation makes the release more than a nostalgic revival. It is a test of whether Xbox can give a large single-player game enough time to form an identity, then persuade players that the years and institutional patience produced something that a quicker production could not. (Guardian, IGN)

Matthew Rhys makes Emmy history twice over

Matthew Rhys became the first performer in 78 years of the Emmys to win two lead acting prizes for different programmes on the same night. The Welsh actor took comedy for Apple TV’s Widow’s Bay and limited series for Netflix’s The Beast in Me; together with his 2018 drama win for The Americans, the results make him the first man to hold all three lead-category awards. Widow’s Bay dominated the evening with 14 wins from 19 nominations, including comedy series, supporting prizes for Kate O’Flynn and Stephen Root, writing for creator Katie Dippold and direction for Hiro Murai. Apple TV added nine wins at the main ceremony after taking 20 at the Creative Arts awards. (Guardian, 9to5Mac)

Jean Smart collected her fifth lead-comedy award for the fifth and final season of Hacks, becoming the first woman to win for every season of a programme. Her eighth acting Emmy put her level with Alison Janney, Julia Louis-Dreyfus and Cloris Leachman; Janney also reached eight with a supporting win for The Diplomat. The Pitt won drama series and gave Noah Wyle a second consecutive lead-drama prize, while Rhea Seehorn earned her first Emmy for Pluribus. DTF St Louis led the limited-series field with seven awards, and the final season of The Late Show won the variety prize after its disputed cancellation. The ceremony’s strongest pattern came from careers accumulating over time: repeat performances, long associations and, in Rhys’s case, two very different roles meeting in one remarkable evening. (Guardian)

Sports

Arsenal’s youngest generation takes the cup

Max Dowman scored twice and created another goal as Arsenal beat Ipswich 4–2 at Portman Road and moved into the fourth round of the Carabao Cup. The 16-year-old won the ball near halfway for his opener, carried it past Cédric Kipré and finished into the corner. Noni Madueke added the second after Dowman began the press, and the teenager calmly placed his next chance across goal soon after half-time. Mikel Merino converted Dowman’s touch for Arsenal’s fourth before Anis Mehmeti and Chuba Akpom replied for Ipswich. Dowman is only the second 16-year-old to score twice in a match for a Premier League club, after Wayne Rooney did it for Everton in the same competition in 2002. Arsenal have now won their first seven games of the season. (Guardian, BBC)

Seventeen-year-old centre-back Marli Salmon was nearly as persuasive, clearing off the line and carrying the ball through pressure on his second senior start. Arsenal finished with four teenagers on the pitch, including 19-year-old Myles Lewis-Skelly and substitute Theo Julienne, 18. Dowman already holds club and league age records from last season, when he became the youngest player in the Champions League, Arsenal’s youngest starter and the Premier League’s youngest starter, scorer and title winner. Mikel Arteta has emphasised careful preparation around a player who completed his GCSEs this summer. The restraint matters because Arsenal can see the temptation as clearly as everyone else: a decisive cup performance makes a 16-year-old look ready for every stage at once. (Guardian, BBC)

Liverpool also advanced with a 3–1 win over Tottenham at Anfield. Alexis Mac Allister finished a move begun by Rio Ngumoha, Cody Gakpo scored from Mac Allister’s pass and Dominik Szoboszlai settled the tie with a 25-yard strike in stoppage time. Spurs had controlled stretches of the first half but Lucas Bergvall and Mohammed Kudus missed clear chances. Conor Gallagher’s header briefly cut the deficit after Giorgi Mamardashvili misjudged a corner, before the goalkeeper recovered with a fine save from Omar Marmoush. Liverpool made ten changes and drew energy from Ngumoha, Lewis Koumas, James McConnell and Trey Nyoni. The score joined Arsenal’s result in giving the evening a theme: heavily rotated sides still depended on young players to provide the speed and nerve that made the difference. (Guardian)
