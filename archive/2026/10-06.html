Brazil's right takes the lead, and the runoff begins on new ground

Flávio Bolsonaro enters Brazil's presidential runoff as the favourite after finishing ahead of Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva in a first round that moved the country's politics sharply to the right. Bolsonaro won 56.1 million votes, or 47.03%, against 53.8 million and 45.16% for the incumbent. The deciding ballot comes on 25 October. The gap is only 2.2 million votes in an electorate of continental scale, yet history weighs on it: since democracy returned in 1985, no second-place candidate has overturned the first-round order. Lula now has three weeks to do something Brazil has never seen in the modern era. (Guardian, Meio, Folha)

The presidential numbers tell only part of the story. Jair Bolsonaro's Liberal party will hold 28 of the Senate's 81 seats, and the broader hard right has 43, enough for a majority. The PL also expanded from 98 to 121 deputies in the lower house. Michelle and Carlos Bolsonaro won Senate seats; Jair Renan Bolsonaro and the former president's brother Renato entered the Chamber of Deputies. Tarcísio de Freitas kept the governorship of São Paulo and Sérgio Moro won in Paraná. Jair Bolsonaro remains under house arrest while serving a 27-year sentence for plotting a coup after the 2022 election, but his family and movement have secured the institutional strength to pursue an amnesty and pressure the supreme court. The next presidency will begin with a Congress transformed around that possibility. (Guardian, Folha)

Lula's first task is to understand the voters who stayed home. A record 33 million people abstained, while the president lost 3.4 million votes compared with his 2022 first-round total. His campaign must persuade disaffected supporters that a second ballot matters and find a route into Minas Gerais and Rio Grande do Sul, two states central to Bolsonaro's advantage. Votes cast for three other rightwing candidates add up to more than 5.5 million and are more naturally available to Bolsonaro; Romeu Zema has already declared his support, as have former president Michel Temer and influential figures across the centre-right. Lula can point to a close national margin. The map beneath it is more difficult. (Guardian, Folha)

Flávio Bolsonaro has held elected office for more than two decades, though his candidacy has been sold as renewal. At 45, he offers a generational handover without asking the movement to loosen its connection to his father. Lula, now 80, is relying on his record in government and warning that the Bolsonaro family would entrench itself in power. The campaign will also draw international attention. Donald Trump celebrated the first-round result, and Benjamin Netanyahu signalled support for Bolsonaro, while Brazil's position on climate, trade and democratic institutions gives the outcome consequences far beyond the family drama. For the next 19 days, the question is whether Lula can turn abstention and fear of a restored Bolsonaro project into a coalition broad enough to defy the country's electoral precedent. (Guardian, Meio)

A Green conference vote opens a European rupture

The Green party of England and Wales is facing a serious break with its European allies after members adopted a binding policy declaring Zionism a form of racism. The motion, passed at the Brighton conference on Sunday, supports a single democratic Palestinian state across historic Palestine and says Zionism should be treated in the same way as other forms of racism. Four of the party's five MPs voted against it. The European Green party, an alliance of 47 parties, backs a two-state solution and has demanded clarity from the British leadership about Israeli and Jewish self-determination and the protection of Jewish members. Germany's Green co-leader called the new policy incompatible with the positions of both his party and the European group. (Guardian)

The vote has also exposed a problem created by the Greens' rapid growth. A little more than 1,000 members backed the motion, amounting to about 0.4% of total membership. Policy is made directly at two annual conferences, a model intended to keep power with activists. It can now produce binding positions for a national party through participation by a very small and self-selecting group. Former co-leader Ellie Chowns wants the process modernised; other senior figures argue that direct democracy is fundamental to the party's identity. One longstanding member has resigned and accused the party of failing to confront antisemitism. Zack Polanski says antisemitism and criticism of the Israeli state must remain distinct, while defending pressure on the government over Gaza. His immediate challenge is organisational as well as moral: the party needs a credible way to reconcile member control, parliamentary responsibility and alliances it may depend on as its electoral reach expands. (Guardian)

Spain turns a housing defeat into a general election

Spain will vote in a snap general election on 29 November after Pedro Sánchez's emergency housing laws were defeated in Congress. The measures included a ban on evictions until 2030 and tighter rules for temporary and room-by-room tenancies. The conservative People's party, the far-right Vox party and the Catalan party Junts combined to reject them on Friday. Housing anger has intensified since an 87-year-old Madrid resident was evicted from the flat where she had lived for 70 years after an investment fund demanded a 230% rent rise. Tens of thousands marched at the weekend, and a tent camp has grown in Puerta del Sol. Sánchez is betting that voters will connect the defeated laws to the daily cost and insecurity of renting. (Guardian)

The prime minister is also trying to reset a government that has been unable to pass a budget since 2023 and depends on a difficult coalition with Sumar and Catalan independence parties. Corruption cases involving his brother and wife, both of whom have denied wrongdoing, have damaged his position. The handling of a mass crossing into Ceuta during the summer added another source of pressure. The People's party leads the polls but may need Vox to govern, giving Sánchez a clear argument about the direction of the country even as his own record faces scrutiny. An election due by next summer has become a seven-week campaign shaped by rent, migration and the limits of minority rule. (Guardian)

Yemen's government retakes ground beside a vital shipping lane

Saudi-backed forces in Yemen have recaptured Dhubab and two other towns near the Bab al-Mandab strait as the internationally recognised government begins an offensive across Houthi-held territory. The Saudi-led coalition says 100 fighter jets are supporting the campaign and providing air protection over the narrow route between the Red Sea and the Gulf of Aden. The advance follows a rapid Houthi push along the coast and towards Taiz, where the group cut a main supply road to Aden and tightened its encirclement of the city. More than 70 fighters on both sides were reported killed around Taiz and neighbouring Lahj in a single day. Each movement of a few miles now changes access to a city, a port or one of global trade's most sensitive chokepoints. (Guardian)

The war is again crossing Yemen's borders. The Houthis say they attacked Saudi Aramco sites in Riyadh and Khurais with missiles and drones after dozens of coalition strikes, while Saudi oil exports are already constrained by Iran's blockade of the Strait of Hormuz. Washington has defended Riyadh's right to respond but has not disclosed the extent of coordination. The government has declared an ambition to recover the whole country, a goal it failed to achieve through years of war after the Houthis seized Sanaa in 2014. The early territorial gains may protect Bab al-Mandab. They also deepen an escalation in which Yemeni civilians, energy facilities and merchant shipping share the same widening field of risk. (Guardian, BBC)

Economy

The average five-year mortgage reaches 6%

The average five-year fixed mortgage in Britain has reached 6.00% for the first time since September 2023. The average two-year fix is close behind at 5.98%, its highest level since December that year. The Bank of England has kept its base rate unchanged since last December, but fixed mortgages are priced from market expectations as well as today's official rate. Turbulence in global bond markets has lifted swap rates and made lenders' funding more expensive, prompting banks and building societies to reprice. The speed of the move is visible in the choices left to borrowers: only nine fixed deals below 5% remain, down from 1,494 at the start of September. (Guardian)

Households reaching the end of an existing fix face the most immediate shock. A £250,000 mortgage fixed for five years at 6% costs about £158 more each month than the same loan at 4.94%, the market average at the start of February. Buyers may have to reduce budgets or leave the market, while owners considering a move have another reason to stay put. Nationwide has already found that annual house-price growth halved in September. The figures also narrow the government's room on housing: building more homes will not restore affordability if financing them moves out of reach. For borrowers, the practical priority is to begin comparing options well before a current deal expires and to judge fees alongside headline rates. For policymakers, the return to 6% is a reminder that confidence in inflation and public finances arrives in monthly household payments. (Guardian)

Sainsbury's and Morrisons explored a supermarket merger

Sainsbury's and Morrisons held exploratory talks this year over a deal that would have created Britain's second-largest supermarket group with 23.6% of the grocery market. The discussions are no longer active, but they show how quickly the industry's structure could change. Tesco holds 27.8%; Sainsbury's has 15.2%, while Morrisons has fallen behind Lidl since its 2021 purchase by the American private equity firm Clayton, Dubilier & Rice. That takeover placed more than £7 billion of debt on Morrisons' balance sheet. Its owner remains open to a combination with another large grocer, and Asda could also be drawn into future discussions. (Guardian)

Any agreement would face a long competition review. Regulators blocked Sainsbury's £7 billion attempt to merge with Asda in 2019 because fewer rivals could mean higher prices, and the same concern is more politically sensitive after years of food inflation. Store disposals might answer local competition problems, though the national effect would remain large. Sainsbury's has recently sold Argos for £120 million, a decade after paying more than £1 billion, to concentrate on food. Lidl, meanwhile, has lifted annual British revenue by 10% to more than £13 billion and pre-tax profit by 30%. Traditional chains are looking for scale while discounters keep gaining it through growth. A dormant conversation between two grocers therefore says something active about the market: consolidation pressure is building even before a deal reaches the table. (Guardian)

AI writing gets an invisible mark in Europe

OpenAI is adding an invisible, machine-readable watermark to text produced by eligible ChatGPT and Codex users in the European Union. The system, called textGrain, will roll out across all plans over the coming weeks as providers adapt to the EU AI Act. API customers worldwide can opt in for selected models, while approved researchers and specialist organisations may apply for access to a detector. The detector will answer a limited question—whether it finds an OpenAI watermark—without identifying a user or revealing prompts and conversations. Google DeepMind and Anthropic have developed related approaches. The common purpose is provenance: giving platforms, institutions and investigators another signal when they need to understand how a piece of text was made. (Verge, 9to5Mac)

The limits matter as much as the mark. OpenAI says detection is fallible and can produce misses or false positives, which is why the tool will not be public at launch. A watermark cannot show whether the text is accurate, who owns it, how much a person contributed or whether an unmarked passage was written by a human. It may also be weakened when text is heavily edited, translated or passed through another model. Performance tests indicate that adding the signal does not materially reduce output quality, but OpenAI is keeping the first general rollout regional while it learns from use. Schools, publishers and regulators should treat the result as one piece of evidence that requires context, never a verdict. Europe is receiving a new trace in the text; the harder work is deciding what that trace can fairly prove. (Verge)

Utah lets an AI begin prescribing acne treatment

Adults in Utah with mild or moderate acne can now scan their faces in an app and receive a prescription selected by an AI system. Nolla Health's pilot costs $4.99 a month and can prescribe eight skin treatments. Two doctors will approve every recommendation for the first 100 patients. During the next phase, covering as many as 500 patients, doctors will review prescriptions after they have been issued; after that, at least 10% will be checked each month, together with every escalation or reported side effect. The company says uncertain cases will be directed to a physician. Utah had already allowed AI systems to renew some medicines, and this programme takes the further step of issuing an initial prescription. (Verge)

Acne is a deliberately narrow place to test autonomous prescribing: it is common, visible in images and often treated through standard pathways. That makes the pilot easier to evaluate, while the staged reduction in supervision creates a clear measure of how much confidence regulators and clinicians are willing to place in the system. Important questions remain around skin-tone performance, image privacy, contraindications, pregnancy risk, follow-up and the point at which a changing condition needs a physical examination. Low-cost routine care could reach people who struggle to see a dermatologist and leave specialists more time for complex cases. The benefit depends on a safety system that notices when an ordinary-looking problem stops being ordinary, then hands the decision to a person quickly. Utah is now testing that boundary with real prescriptions and real patients. (Verge)

Culture

Two games stories show what disappears when platforms move on

NBA 2K25 will be removed from digital stores on 30 October, just over two years after its release, and its servers will close on 1 January 2027. Owners will retain offline modes including Play Now, MyNBA, The W and MyWNBA. MyCareer, however, is a single-player story tied to online progression and virtual currency, so it will become unplayable along with multiplayer and MyTeam. Players who built a character, advanced through seasons and spent money inside the mode are approaching an expiry date imposed by infrastructure. Their copies remain otherwise functional. Annual sports games have always encouraged migration to the latest edition. Server-dependent design turns that commercial rhythm into permanent loss. (IGN)

At Ninja Theory, the loss is happening before unfinished work reaches players. Staff at the Cambridge studio behind Hellblade have begun announcing departures after Microsoft said it intended to close the company when two rescue deals failed. Programmers, audio specialists and performance-capture staff describe projects they will not finish and production systems built over years. Microsoft unveiled Senua, a third Hellblade game, in June, and the trailer is widely understood to have been intended to attract a buyer. The closure sits inside a larger Xbox reorganisation that has also brought layoffs at Halo Studios and moved several development teams under Activision or Bethesda. One story removes a game people paid for; the other disperses the people and knowledge needed to make the next one. Together they sharpen an old preservation question: ownership in digital culture can be temporary, and the archive is often controlled by the company with the next release to sell. (IGN, IGN)

Jeffrey Archer dies on the eve of his final novel

Jeffrey Archer has died aged 86, one day before the publication of what he had described as his final novel. His publisher said he died suddenly and peacefully at home between writing sessions. Archer sold about 300 million books in 119 countries and 49 languages; Kane and Abel alone sold more than 30 million copies. The writing career began after bankruptcy forced him to leave Parliament in 1974. His first novel, Not a Penny More, Not a Penny Less, restored his finances and opened five decades of novels, short stories, plays and children's books. On Sunday he was still signing books and speaking to a sold-out audience in Leicester Square. (Guardian, BBC)

His public life repeatedly rose and collapsed. Archer entered the Commons at 29, returned to Conservative prominence as deputy chair under Margaret Thatcher and became the party's candidate for London mayor. That campaign ended when evidence showed he had lied during a successful 1987 libel case over allegations involving a sex worker. He was convicted of perjury and perverting the course of justice in 2001, sentenced to four years and served half. He later returned to writing and remained in the House of Lords until retiring in 2024, without taking an active role. The scale of his readership and the gravity of his conviction resist a tidy closing judgment. His last book, Adam and Eve, arrives today with the author absent and with that contradiction intact: a disciplined popular storyteller whose appetite for reinvention could never erase the court record that ended his politics. (Guardian)
