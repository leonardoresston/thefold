Iran offers a six-day route to reopen Hormuz

Iran has told Donald Trump that it can reopen the strait of Hormuz within six days and begin negotiations over its nuclear programme the following day. The offer would lift a chokehold on one of the world’s most important shipping routes, but it comes with demanding conditions: Washington must remove sanctions on Iranian oil exports, release some frozen Iranian assets and bring the wars involving Iran and its allies to an end, including Israel’s campaign in Lebanon. Tehran says it has agreed a temporary shipping route with Oman and is ready to register the plan with the International Maritime Organization. China has been consulted and supports it. Saudi Arabia, whose approval had been expected last week, pulled back after renewed Houthi attacks linked the shipping question to Yemen’s unresolved war. (Guardian)

The proposal accelerates a timetable agreed in Islamabad in June. Nuclear talks under that memorandum were due to begin after 60 days; Iran now offers to start them almost as soon as vessels move freely again. Foreign minister Abbas Araghchi is staying in New York through the weekend while the White House considers the terms. There are practical issues still to settle, from navigational fees to the control of military traffic. The larger obstacle is political. Israel would have to accept an end to its Lebanon campaign, and may resist a passage overseen by Iran. Tehran also has to persuade Gulf governments that its appeal for calm can restrain the Houthis, whose decisions Iranian officials insist they do not control. (Guardian)

There is a powerful economic incentive to make the route work. Brent crude was trading near $107 a barrel on Thursday, feeding transport and manufacturing costs far beyond the Gulf. Trump enters November’s midterm elections wanting lower energy prices; Iran wants legitimate oil sales and access to its money. Each side therefore has something immediate to offer the other. The bargain also crosses several conflicts at once, which makes every concession vulnerable to an event elsewhere. A Houthi strike in Saudi Arabia, an Israeli operation in Lebanon or a confrontation involving American military shipping could unsettle the process before the first nuclear meeting begins. (Guardian)

Britain has returned to the diplomatic channel after a year without ministerial contact. Foreign secretary Ed Miliband met Araghchi in New York, pressed for freedom of navigation and a negotiated settlement, and raised Iranian threats to Jewish communities in Britain and the imprisonment of a British couple in Tehran. The meeting followed Britain’s decision in July to proscribe the Islamic Revolutionary Guards Corps as a terrorist organisation, so the relationship remains deeply strained. A workable agreement now needs a tightly defined shipping arrangement, verifiable steps on sanctions and a sequence for talks that can survive the disputes around it. Hormuz could reopen quickly on paper. Keeping it open will require the parties to separate a useful first move from every grievance they eventually hope to settle. (Guardian)

News & Economy

Trump and Xi extend the truce while the harder bargains wait

Xi Jinping’s Washington visit has produced ceremony, warmer language and few completed agreements so far. Donald Trump welcomed the Chinese president with military honours and a state dinner, while Xi offered pandas and a new student-exchange programme. The immediate economic result is modest but useful: a year-long trade truce that kept tariffs below their previous peaks has been extended by two months, with financial analysts expecting a longer extension before Xi leaves today. Neither government wants to revive last year’s tariff escalation while American farmers await Chinese purchases and US manufacturers remain dependent on rare-earth minerals that Beijing had promised to supply. Stability itself has value after a volatile year, though it postpones the decisions that would make it durable. (BBC)

The unresolved list reaches well beyond trade. Washington is weighing weapons sales to Taiwan; Beijing wants clarity over American action in the war with Iran; both governments differ over Ukraine and over how artificial intelligence should be governed. Trump has placed security, technology and what he calls superintelligence on the formal agenda. American officials approach AI through frontier capability, safety and control of advanced chips, while China wants rapid deployment across the real economy and fewer constraints on development. Those aims still allow practical agreements on incident reporting, research contacts and the use of autonomous systems in military settings, but the summit has not yet supplied them. The pageantry lowers the temperature and gives each leader room to keep talking. Today’s final meetings will show whether that room contains a bargain or simply another extension of the pause. (BBC)

Europe prepares for a Russian test below the threshold of war

Danish defence intelligence says Russia could attempt a limited attack against a Nato member within months. Its assessment describes a low but growing risk of long-range strikes on infrastructure supporting Ukraine or a small incursion by troops without insignia. An outright invasion remains highly unlikely, though the service no longer rules it out. The likelier campaign would combine sabotage, cyberattacks and ambiguous military action intended to consume European resources and expose disagreement over Article 5, the alliance’s promise that an attack on one member is an attack on all. Moscow may judge that a deliberately narrow operation could leave governments arguing about attribution and proportionality while the damage is already done. (Guardian)

The warning arrived after a fire disabled a Starlink station in central Poland, interrupting internet access for institutions that included the Ukrainian military. Poland has called the blaze deliberate sabotage, while stressing that responsibility has not yet been established. It follows suspected attacks on railways and drone factories and an attempted drone strike at Leipzig/Halle airport that Germany attributed to Russia. The pattern leaves Nato with a problem of readiness as much as weaponry. Networks need redundancy, investigators need a faster way to share evidence, and political leaders need an agreed ladder of responses before the next incident. Deterrence becomes weaker when an adversary can predict a long debate over whether a hybrid attack qualifies for a collective answer. (Guardian)

A two-year NHS wait turns diagnosis into a matter of means

Four NHS integrated care boards in England have imposed minimum waits for ADHD and autism assessments as demand outruns their budgets. West Yorkshire now requires people to wait two years before an NHS-funded assessment with an independent provider. North East and North Cumbria has a minimum close to 78 weeks; Somerset’s is 52 weeks, Devon’s 14, and Kent and Medway has also introduced a floor. Across England, 15 boards serving 19 million people are rationing access through waiting periods, annual caps or clinical-priority tests. The queue already contains about 960,000 people seeking an ADHD assessment and 295,000 waiting for an autism assessment. (Guardian)

The financial pressure is plain: West Yorkshire’s bill for assessments rose from £7m in 2023-24 to £21m last year. Yet a fixed delay blocks people from using their legal right to choose an available private provider at NHS expense, even though private clinics already perform half of NHS-funded ADHD assessments and a third of autism assessments. Families able to spend several thousand pounds can leave the queue; everyone else may lose years of support at school, work or home. A government review led by Peter Fonagy, now due early next month, will examine the rapid rise in recorded neurodiversity and concerns about the consistency of private assessments. Quality control and capacity have to advance together. Minimum waits balance a local ledger by transferring the cost to families and crisis services. (Guardian)

Brazil enters its final campaign week with the runoff tied

Brazil’s presidential race reaches its last full week with Lula on 40% and Flávio Bolsonaro on 36% in Datafolha’s first-round measure. A runoff between them is statistically tied at 47% to 45%. The survey of 2,002 voters in 125 municipalities has a margin of error of two percentage points, and it shows no path to a first-round victory: Augusto Cury has 5%, Ronaldo Caiado 4% and Renan Santos 3%, while blank and null votes account for another 5%. Eight in ten voters say their choice is settled. Among voters who identify with neither the Bolsonaro nor PT camps, however, 35% still allow that they may change their minds. (Folha, Meio)

The coalitions are sharply different. Lula leads 46% to 32% among the poorest half of voters and 55% to 25% in the north-east, but his campaign must turn that advantage into attendance among groups with higher rates of abstention. Flávio is ahead among evangelical voters, households earning two to five minimum wages and in the south. The south-east, containing 42% of the sample, is a technical tie. Foreign policy has entered the domestic contest after Lula accused the Trump administration of intervening on behalf of his rival and American lawmakers raised their own concerns about interference. The decisive work remains closer to home: turnout, living costs, security and the credibility of each side’s promises to voters whose loyalty is still movable. (Folha, Guardian)

Britain could sit outside Europe’s new industrial preference

France is pressing for the European Union’s planned “Made in Europe” rules to begin with the 27 member states, a definition that could shut British companies out of subsidised contracts for electric cars, renewable technology and other strategic goods. The Industrial Accelerator Act would direct public money towards European production as the bloc responds to heavily subsidised Chinese competitors. Britain wants to be treated as a trusted partner, but French ministers argue that access to the internal market’s protection belongs to countries that participate in and finance it. The dispute makes the practical cost of leaving the single market visible in a new place: the procurement decisions that will shape Europe’s low-carbon factories. (Guardian)

Germany favours a broader “Made with Europe” approach that could include Britain, Japan, Canada and other reciprocal partners, and Nordic governments broadly agree. Ireland hopes to broker a common position in December, while British officials expect a delayed EU-UK reset summit in November. The timetable leaves Andy Burnham’s government with a delicate choice. Demanding a British exemption before the EU agrees its own definition could harden opposition; waiting risks allowing supply chains and tenders to form around an EU-only rule. A useful British offer would connect market access to measurable investment, shared standards and reciprocal procurement. European governments want resilient supply chains. British factories can help provide them if the political agreement catches up with the industrial geography. (Guardian)

Technology

Britain’s largest AI computer is waiting for electricity

A datacentre promoted as Britain’s largest sovereign AI facility will miss its planned 2027 opening and may wait until the early or middle 2030s for enough power. Nscale, the British startup building the project in Loughton, Essex, wants as much as 90MW of capacity, roughly the consumption of 315,000 homes. UK Power Networks has reportedly told the company that the grid cannot provide it on the original timetable. Nscale has cleared the former scaffolding yard and is investigating on-site generation and a faster connection, but the delay exposes the gap between announcing computing ambition and supplying the physical system beneath it. (Guardian)

Loughton is part of a much larger queue. Ofgem counted 315 datacentres seeking National Grid connections in June, together asking for 73GW. Britain’s entire peak electricity demand is about 45GW. Some applications will never become operating sites, yet their scale shows why local connections cannot be planned one project at a time. AI policy now depends on transmission lines, substations, planning consent and credible choices about which projects receive scarce capacity first. The government can accelerate useful connections and require developers to prove that demand is real, finance is ready and energy use can flex when the grid is tight. Otherwise a strategy sold through advanced chips will be governed by the slowest transformer in the chain. (Guardian)

Meta calls Muse a cloud computer; its files reveal how much that means

Developers have persuaded Meta’s new Muse assistant to package and share much of the filesystem inside its persistent Linux virtual machine. The material included Ubuntu files, application templates and internal Markdown and JSON documents describing how the product handles requests, memory and connections to services such as Gmail. Meta says users have access only to their own virtual machine and gain no privileged route into its infrastructure or anyone else’s data. That distinction matters, but the exported files still expose implementation details. They indicate that Muse stores memory in plain Markdown, runs a nightly review of conversations and relies on hard-coded machinery for some actions, including subscription cancellation and the control of runaway agent spawning. (Verge)

The incident is Muse’s second security argument in a week. Researcher Patrick Wardle previously found a way for attackers to hijack the agent, redirect transcription processing and access a user’s account; Meta issued a hotfix. The filesystem behaviour sits in a greyer area because company executives describe Muse as a free computer in the cloud and say broad user control is intentional. The product itself initially refused requests for a full copy as unsafe, then produced selected directories after further prompting. An agent able to operate services needs clear boundaries that agree across its interface, documentation and underlying machine. Users should know which files belong to them, which secrets are removed before export and how connected accounts are isolated. Capability is easier to trust when the permission model can be explained before a curious prompt discovers it. (Verge)

Culture, Sports & Gaming

U2’s new album carries Dolly Parton’s final recording

U2 will release Carnaval de Luz on 13 November, their first album of new material in nearly a decade. Its closing track, Torn, pairs Bono with Dolly Parton in the last performance she recorded before her death in August. The song follows broken family ties through a country arrangement, with Daniel Lanois on pedal steel. The 12-track album also reunites U2 with Lanois and Brian Eno, the production partnership behind The Unforgettable Fire, The Joshua Tree and Achtung Baby. Eno co-writes and plays synthesisers on three tracks; Martin Garrix and Ryan Tedder bring a newer pop vocabulary elsewhere. (Guardian)

The announcement lands almost exactly 50 years after the teenage Larry Mullen Jr placed a notice at his Dublin school seeking musicians for a band. All four members were present at the first meeting, and that continuity gives the record an unusual frame: a group marking half a century by returning to formative collaborators while sharing a final vocal from another defining artist. U2 have already released two EPs this year, Days of Ash and Easter Lily. The new album turns towards brighter, louder music after the political anxiety of those songs. Its first single, Street of Dreams, arrived in July, and the rock track Silencio now accompanies the full announcement. (Guardian)

Brighton gave Arsenal’s rivals a demanding blueprint

Arsenal entered the international break with their first Premier League defeat since April and their heaviest league loss in more than three years. Brighton’s 3-0 win came from an aggressive press that followed Martin Ødegaard into deeper positions, matched Arsenal four against four during the build-up and forced David Raya to play long. Brighton then turned recoveries into quick attacks before the champions could settle around the edge of the box. The method suited a side leading the league in high turnovers, with the stamina to keep contesting first headers and second balls. Rivals will study it closely because it interrupted the patterns behind Arsenal’s nine straight league wins across the end of last season and the start of this one. (Guardian)

Replication will be difficult. Brighton supplied three of the match’s four hardest-running players, and two of their goals came from low-probability shots outside the area. Arsenal actually produced the higher expected-goals total, 1.63 to 1.31, during a fourth away game in ten days. William Saliba was injured, while Ødegaard, Declan Rice and Bukayo Saka had missed a conventional pre-season after the World Cup. Manchester City now lead the table, but Arsenal have two more points than they held at the same stage of last season’s title-winning campaign. Mikel Arteta has time to restore the sharpness of his build-up. The useful warning is specific: when fatigue slows the first pass, a physically exceptional opponent can keep Arsenal away from the territory where their control usually begins. (Guardian)

Dressmaker finds a large audience by serving a precise one

An independent game about designing and sewing gowns has entered Steam’s ten best-selling titles within days of release. Dressmaker gives players a boutique, customers and a detailed process for assembling clothes in a fictional town, with enough mischief to let a tailor sabotage the occasional client. At Thursday’s count, 96% of its 2,177 user reviews were positive. It had passed large releases including Diablo IV and briefly Marvel Rivals, helped by clips of intricate stitching and finished dresses spreading through social media. (IGN)

Cape Town publisher Free Lives and developer Cozy Lives built the game around a craft and an audience that blockbuster budgets often overlook. Its modest hardware requirements let it run on years of ordinary laptops, widening access without flattening the design into something for everyone. That combination is part of the achievement. Digital distribution can reward a specific idea when players recognise that its mechanics respect a subject they already care about. Dressmaker’s early success will rise or settle as the launch attention passes, but it has already made a useful commercial case for small teams: clarity about whom a game is for can be an advantage large enough to show up on the same chart as the industry’s biggest franchises. (IGN)
