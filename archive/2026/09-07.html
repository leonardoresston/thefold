England’s SEND reforms meet a rush for legal protection

Schools and councils are preparing for the share of pupils with education, health and care plans to double from one in 20 last year to almost one in 10 by 2029. Applications have surged since ministers set out plans in February to reshape support for children with special educational needs and disabilities. Some secondary schools expect twice as many new year 7 pupils to arrive this month with an EHCP or an assessment under way. Hampshire forecasts a 58% rise between 2026 and 2030; Manchester expects a 40% increase by 2029. The national total, already above 530,000 school-age children, could pass the previous estimate of 840,000 before the end of the decade. (Guardian)

The pace reflects both unmet need and a collapse of trust. An EHCP is a legal agreement that sets out the support a council must provide, often unlocking extra funding or a place in a special school. Families who have spent years fighting for assessments see it as the strongest protection available. The government wants mainstream schools to offer earlier diagnosis, specialist help and individual support plans, while reserving EHCPs from 2030 for children with the most profound needs. Existing plans will continue after the change, creating a powerful reason to secure one now. The intended reform has therefore increased pressure on the system it is meant to relieve. (Guardian, Guardian)

Ministers are looking to Thomas Lord Audley school in Colchester for a route through the problem. The mainstream secondary starts working with families a year before admission and builds a specific plan around each pupil. It is now oversubscribed, suggesting that confidence can grow when support is visible before a crisis. Schools minister Georgia Gould also points to early speech and language work in Rochdale that reduced specialist referrals by 85%. Parts of the national programme, including experts placed directly in schools and nurseries, are due to begin this year. The wider system of common standards and tiers of support is planned for 2030. (Guardian)

Time and money are now tightly connected. High-needs spending has left councils with deficits large enough to require a statutory accounting override, and local government leaders expect debt to rebuild within a year even after the Treasury clears most of the existing shortfall. Every additional plan carries a binding cost, while the new mainstream provision needs sustained funding, teacher training and educational psychologists of its own. Families are making a rational choice in an unreliable system. The government’s test is to make ordinary school support dependable enough that parents can stop treating a legal document as their child’s only safe ground. (Guardian)

News

Ukraine prepares for another winter of war

Volodymyr Zelenskyy expects the war to continue into winter after several hours of talks with US envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner in Kyiv produced no major breakthrough. The pair arrived by train after meeting Vladimir Putin in Moscow on Saturday. Ukraine, Europe and the United States will send officials to a further round of discussions, and Zelenskyy again proposed a three-way meeting with Russia and America. The diplomatic choreography was carefully managed: Moscow and Kyiv agreed to pause attacks on one another’s capitals for three days while the envoys travelled, and Zelenskyy received them at St Sophia Cathedral beside the security-service headquarters hit by a Russian drone on Friday. (BBC)

The central disagreements remain in place. Russia still occupies about a fifth of Ukraine and continues to describe territorial demands and limits on Kyiv’s security choices as root causes that any settlement must address. Zelenskyy wants guarantees that leave Russia unable to renew the invasion. Overnight attacks continued outside the two capitals: Russia sent 108 long-range drones and small cruise missiles, while Ukraine struck an oil refinery, an airfield and air-defence systems. Kyiv’s immediate concern is the coming heating season. Russia has repeatedly targeted energy infrastructure in winter, and Ukraine depends on American Patriot interceptors whose supply has been reduced by the US war with Iran. Diplomatic language has softened the journey between the two capitals; the battlefield is still setting the timetable. (BBC, BBC)

Iran and America make the Strait of Hormuz the pressure point

Iran has promised a faster and heavier response after the United States struck Iranian oil tankers on Saturday. Washington says those attacks answered Iranian fire on American warships; Tehran says it then targeted an unmanned US vessel trying to enter the Strait of Hormuz, a claim the US military denies. A 60-day ceasefire expired in August, and renewed combat is now entering its second week. American forces have hit targets in Iran, while Iranian attacks have reached US interests in the United Arab Emirates, Bahrain, Kuwait and Jordan. Six months into the wider conflict, negotiations have yet to produce a durable pause. (BBC)

Control of the strait gives Iran leverage over the energy market and the domestic politics of Donald Trump’s midterm campaign. The waterway has been heavily restricted since February, although US escorts and a southern route have restored an estimated nine million barrels of oil a day, perhaps two-thirds of prewar flows when pipelines are included. American diesel averaged $5.85 a gallon on Friday, up from $3.71 a year earlier. Trump says the strait is open and the fighting will end soon. Shipping companies, insurers and drivers are dealing with a narrower reality in which every exchange of fire can raise the cost of moving fuel. (BBC)

A runway overrun leaves five dead in Miami

Five people were killed and five injured when a Boeing 767 cargo aircraft operated for Amazon Air overran a runway at Miami International Airport on Sunday afternoon. The 21 Air flight had arrived from San Juan, Puerto Rico, when it crossed the end of the diagonal runway, hit several vehicles and caught fire. Three of the injured were in critical condition, two others were taken to hospital, and the pilot and copilot were initially trapped in the cockpit. More than 60 fire-rescue units responded while crews contained a fuel leak. (Guardian, BBC)

The airport closed all runways and taxiways, grounding traffic at one of America’s busiest hubs and delaying about 250 flights by late afternoon. The Federal Aviation Administration and National Transportation Safety Board have begun investigations, with the first NTSB briefing expected on Monday. Their work will need to establish the aircraft’s condition, the landing sequence and how vehicles came to be in its path beyond the tarmac. Amazon said it would cooperate. The wreckage has already turned a cargo operation into a public disaster whose victims include people on the ground. (Guardian)

Brazil’s Supreme Court must judge a crisis within its own walls

Justice André Mendonça has formally released for judgment a Federal Police report connecting fellow justice Alexandre de Moraes with jailed Banco Master founder Daniel Vorcaro. Court president Edson Fachin will decide when the full bench considers the request for an investigation. Messages in the report include Vorcaro asking Moraes, shortly before the banker’s arrest in 2025, whether he should leave Brazil; the available material does not show a reply. Police also found that Moraes edited a R$131m contract between Master and the law firm of his wife, Viviane Barci de Moraes, before it was signed. Prosecutor general Paulo Gonet argues that the report is invalid because Mendonça directed police beyond his authority. (Folha)

Moraes has responded by seeking an investigation of Mendonça for abuse of authority, misconduct and an impeachable offence. That move used the court’s seven-year-old fake-news inquiry, which Fachin has since separated from the complaint. Cármen Lúcia may hold the decisive vote between two evenly matched groups of justices. She is understood to see serious problems in both the gathering of material against Moraes and his use of an exceptional inquiry against a colleague. The court now has to test the evidence, the powers used to obtain it and the counterattack with the same rigour. Any appearance that alliances determine the result would deepen the institutional damage a month before Brazil’s presidential election. (Folha, Folha)

A graphic history asks readers to handle the machinery of genocide

Dana Mashoian Walrath’s The Book of Genocides, published on Tuesday, uses ten case studies to trace five centuries of mass killing and forced displacement. The Armenian-American artist, writer and anthropologist travelled to every place represented, meeting survivors and descendants from Cambodia and Rwanda to Bosnia and Rohingya refugee camps in Bangladesh. Her own grandparents survived the destruction of the Ottoman empire’s Armenian population. The book brings that family history together with the Holocaust, the enslavement of Africans, the killing of Indigenous peoples in the Americas and a chapter on Palestinian displacement and Gaza. (Guardian)

The form makes the reader physically involved. Portrait pages can be cut into thirds and recombined, moving human faces into images of beasts, vermin and parasites. Walrath avoids explicit drawings of violence and concentrates on the language, bureaucracy and material interests that make it possible, especially the seizure of land and the gradual removal of a group’s humanity. The argument is deliberately contentious where current conflicts are concerned, and the book expects the reader to examine how each case fits the legal definition established by the 1948 Genocide Convention. Its most useful demand is close attention to the steps that precede killing, when ordinary institutions still have the power to refuse them. (Guardian)

Economy

Jaguar Land Rover prepares thousands of job cuts

Jaguar Land Rover has opened a voluntary redundancy programme that could remove as many as 4,000 jobs, close to 12% of its British workforce. The first offers will focus on salaried staff, managers and research roles, although compulsory redundancies remain possible. Business secretary Jonathan Reynolds will meet chief executive PB Balaji and union leaders on Tuesday. He has ruled out public money to preserve the present workforce, while leaving room for government investment tied to the company’s long-term plans. Unite wants retraining and voluntary departures used to prevent compulsory cuts. (Guardian, BBC)

JLR is still carrying the effects of last year’s cyberattack, which stopped production for more than a month, reduced output by 27% and caused an estimated £1.9bn of damage. It now plans £1.7bn of savings over two years. Tariffs in the United States, a crucial market for Range Rover and Defender, have settled at 10% after briefly reaching 27.5%. Chinese competition is also gaining ground in Britain, and electric-vehicle sales targets are running ahead of consumer demand. The company makes more than 400,000 vehicles a year and anchors a much larger Midlands supply chain. Tuesday’s discussion therefore reaches well beyond the headcount at JLR: skilled work, smaller manufacturers and the government’s promise to rebuild British industry all sit around the same table. (Guardian, BBC)

Europe confronts the hollowing out of its factories

European manufacturers could shed another 300,000 jobs before the end of 2026 as cheaper Chinese components move deeper into metals, chemicals and other supply chains. The estimate comes from Eurometal, which will carry ten symbolic coffins around the European Commission in Brussels today. China’s trade surplus with the EU has reached about €1bn a day, and the bloc’s annual goods imbalance stands near €360bn. Tariffs already cover Chinese electric vehicles and some foreign steel, yet European companies still face higher energy bills, carbon costs and import duties on raw materials while finished components arrive without equivalent charges. (Guardian)

The warning follows Volkswagen’s confirmation of 100,000 cuts and a European Commission projection that high energy costs and global competition could put more than a million jobs at risk. Brussels and Beijing are midway through a three-month truce intended to avert a wider trade war. The difficulty is that tariffs aimed at a finished car or a slab of steel can miss the quieter transfer of production knowledge embedded in thousands of components. Once investment, supplier relationships and trained workers disappear, a later recovery becomes far more expensive than the original protection. (Guardian)

China is strengthening the other side of that industrial contest with a $54bn capital injection into banks and insurers. Three state lenders will receive a combined 290bn yuan, while China Life Insurance is due 35bn yuan and China Taiping 7bn. The money is intended to replenish reserves, support stock-market investment and keep credit flowing into an economy struggling with weak loan demand and low interest rates. It may also help larger state insurers absorb smaller firms with deteriorating solvency. Europe is trying to decide which parts of its productive capacity require defence just as Beijing gives its financial system more room to sustain domestic companies through a period of slow growth. (Guardian)

Technology

A private rocket gives continental Europe its own route to orbit

Germany’s Isar Aerospace has placed its two-stage Spectrum rocket into low Earth orbit from Norway, completing the first fully commercial orbital launch from continental Europe. The same vehicle design survived only 30 seconds on its first attempt in March before falling into the sea. Sunday’s success gives European governments and companies an alternative to the state-supported launchers operated by Arianespace and Avio. It also answers part of a strategic problem created by Russia’s invasion of Ukraine, which ended access to Soyuz rockets, and by doubts over the long-term reliability of American support. (Verge)

Launch capacity remains a bottleneck even as satellites become smaller and cheaper. A working European commercial vehicle can shorten queues, keep sensitive institutional missions closer to home and give the European Space Agency practical weight behind its policy of buying European technology. One successful flight does not establish a dependable service; customers will watch launch cadence, price and failure rates across the next missions. Spectrum has nevertheless crossed the hardest early threshold. Europe can now build its argument for sovereign access to space around hardware that has reached orbit. (Verge)

Fairphone makes repairability feel ordinary

The Fairphone 6 Plus brings the Dutch company’s repairable Android phone into the American mainstream at $649. Its back, battery and other parts can be replaced with the screwdriver supplied in the box, and components are interchangeable with last year’s Fairphone 6. The new model adds a Snapdragon 7S Gen 4 processor, 12GB of memory, a five-year warranty and support for as many as six operating-system upgrades. It runs a clean version of Android and works officially on AT&T and T-Mobile. Earlier Fairphones asked buyers to accept dated processors, weak cameras and a high import price in return for ethical materials and longer life; this one performs much like any competent midrange handset. (Verge)

There are visible compromises. Its IP55 rating offers limited protection from dust and water, wireless charging is absent, the screen is merely adequate in bright daylight and heavy games can expose the processor. Easy access to the battery changes the calculation around average endurance, however: a worn cell can be replaced at home instead of pushing the whole device towards a drawer or recycling bin. Modular rear panels add a grip, wallet or strap, and a physical switch activates stripped-back focus modes. Fairphone’s advance is a phone whose environmental case lives in everyday ownership. Repair becomes a normal feature, backed by enough software support to make using it worthwhile. (Verge)

Nintendo gives Zelda an anniversary stage of its own

Nintendo will hold two Direct presentations on consecutive days this week. A 30-minute programme for the 40th anniversary of The Legend of Zelda begins at 3pm in London on Tuesday, followed by a general Nintendo Direct at the same time on Wednesday and a Treehouse stream. Nintendo has confirmed that the second event will concentrate on Switch 2 games arriving this winter. The Zelda broadcast is expected to provide a fuller look at the Ocarina of Time remake already scheduled for 2026, while any film footage, release date or anniversary hardware remains speculation until the stream begins. Giving the series a separate half-hour leaves Wednesday free to set out the wider console calendar without making every announcement compete with Hyrule. (IGN)

Sports

Arsenal answer Chelsea’s fast start

Arsenal came from behind to beat Chelsea 2–1 at the Emirates and join Manchester City as the only Premier League teams with three wins from three. Morgan Rogers gave Chelsea an early lead and became the first player to score against the champions this season. Kai Havertz levelled against his former club, then allowed Martin Ødegaard’s shot to run through with a deft dummy for the winner. The Arsenal captain has three goals in four matches after two seasons disrupted by injury, and his understanding with Havertz gave the home side control after a difficult opening. (Guardian)

Chelsea’s first defeat under Xabi Alonso showed the cost of their open approach. They have conceded seven times in three league games and gone 19 league matches without a clean sheet. Moisés Caicedo missed the derby after returning too early from injury last week, leaving the midfield short of its strongest shield. Mikel Arteta sees room for Arsenal to exceed last season’s title-winning level, although a muscle problem for Cristhian Mosquera may test the defence. The clearest progress was in the response: conceding first did not loosen Arsenal’s structure, and two players seeking an uninterrupted season turned the match together. (Guardian, BBC)
