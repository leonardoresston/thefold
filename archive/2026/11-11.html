News

UK police arrest senior US soldier over suspected Russian spying as US indicts Iranian cell for RAF Fairford plot

British counter-terrorism officers have arrested a 41-year-old senior United States Army soldier in Gloucestershire on suspicion of spying for Russia, following a joint operation with MI5, UK Defence Counter Intelligence, US Army Counterintelligence Command and the FBI. Stationed at Imjin Barracks in Innsworth—headquarters of Nato’s Allied Rapid Reaction Corps—the serviceman was detained near Gloucester on Tuesday and taken to a London police station under sections one and three of the National Security Act 2023, which cover disclosing protected information and assisting a foreign intelligence service and allow detention for up to 14 days without charge. Officers searched two Gloucestershire properties as FBI director Kash Patel noted the bureau has made 167 espionage arrests over the past 21 months, up 53%. (Guardian, BBC)

Hours later in New York, federal prosecutors unsealed an eight-count indictment charging three operatives linked to Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps and Iraqi militia Kata’ib Hezbollah with orchestrating September’s foiled bomb plot at RAF Fairford in Gloucestershire, the European base for American B-1 bombers during the war in Iran. Prosecutors allege that dual Iranian-Iraqi national Mohammad Baqer Saad Dawood al-Saadi, already held in a New York jail awaiting trial over 18 European attacks under the front group Harakat Ashab al-Yamin al-Islamiya—including seven in the UK and April’s stabbing of two Jewish men in Golders Green—used coded family phone calls to direct IRGC operative Mohsen Mohajeri and Iraqi citizen Mohammad Ali al-Hassani. The cell allegedly surveilled Fairford and sought vans, fuel barrels, chemical accelerants and shrapnel before a farmer spotted three suspicious vans near Whelford on 27 September, prompting UK arrests and the withdrawal of US bombers last month. (Guardian, BBC)

Two further security and military cases surfaced in London on Wednesday. Former parliamentary researcher Chris Cash, acquitted in the Westminster China spy case after the prosecution collapsed, shared more than 6,000 messages showing how his friend in China, Chris Berry, relayed inquiries from a senior Chinese official during the 2022 Tory leadership race about whether a sanctioned MP might enter the cabinet; Cash admitted his Westminster gossip was naive, yet insisted he received no Chinese money and was made a fall guy. Meanwhile, the inquiry into UK special forces in Afghanistan released photographs from two 2011 SAS night raids in Helmand that killed 17 people, including boys aged 14 and 15. Lead counsel Oliver Glasgow KC said the images—showing unarmed people lying on pillows and under blankets with head wounds and low wall blood spatter—contradicted SAS accounts and pointed to possible executions while asleep, a view backed by military police investigator Lt Col John Harvey. (BBC, BBC)

David Walliams accused of preying on young women and teenagers after High Court rejects anonymity bid

Comedian, children’s author and former Britain’s Got Talent judge David Walliams has been accused by multiple women of exploiting his celebrity status and workplace authority to groom teenage fans and pursue young women for sex. Accounts gathered from more than 30 first-hand sources in an investigation by the Guardian and ITV News include two women, now in their 30s, who met the 55-year-old star when they were 15 and allege he groomed them in ways that affected them for nearly two decades. Other women allege inappropriate conduct connected to Britain’s Got Talent and a sexual relationship with a junior employee at his former publisher, HarperCollins. Walliams denies all wrongdoing, saying he has never broken the law or acted as a sexual predator. (Guardian, BBC)

The findings were published after Mr Justice Nicklin dismissed an unprecedented High Court bid by Walliams—anonymised in court as PPT—for a lifelong injunction blocking publication. Rather than claiming breach of privacy, his barrister, Sara Mansoori KC, argued under Articles 2 and 3 of the European Convention on Human Rights that publication threatened his life after he made suicide attempts in May and July and was sectioned in a psychiatric hospital the week before the hearing. Nicklin ruled that hospital clinicians, rather than a civil court weighing press freedom under Article 10, bore the state’s duty to protect him, and Walliams confirmed on Monday he would not appeal. On Wednesday, presenter Jonathan Ross said he was horrified by both the Walliams allegations and the ten-and-a-half-year rape sentence handed to Russell Brand on Monday, while Culture Secretary Lisa Nandy condemned industry power imbalances, the Metropolitan Police confirmed several more people had come forward with potential criminal complaints against Brand, and Education Secretary Lucy Powell criticised the cross-examination of Brand’s victims. (Guardian, BBC, Guardian)

US judge allows Trump’s $10bn BBC defamation suit to proceed as House Republicans block Hegseth impeachment

A federal judge in Florida has ruled that Donald Trump’s $10bn (£7.5bn) defamation lawsuit against the BBC can go ahead, partially denying the broadcaster’s motion to dismiss over its Panorama documentary Trump: A Second Chance. The programme spliced together two remarks from Trump’s 6 January 2021 speech in Washington delivered nearly 55 minutes apart into a 12-second clip—an edit for which the BBC apologised after acknowledging it gave the mistaken impression of a direct call for violence. While US District Judge Jeffrey T Kuntz, a Trump appointee, dismissed a parallel claim under Florida’s unfair trade practices law because the broadcast was not commercial speech, he ruled that bridging a 55-minute gap went beyond routine condensation and sufficed at the pleading stage to allege actual malice. The BBC vowed to contest the case, which requires Trump to prove damages from a programme not aired in Florida and sit for a sworn deposition by 11 December. (Guardian, BBC)

In Washington, House Republicans closed ranks a week after losing both chambers in the midterms to table outgoing Kentucky Republican Thomas Massie’s resolution to impeach Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth over civilian deaths in Iran, unauthorised operations in Venezuela and Yemen, and retaliation against a Democratic senator. Republicans voted 211–204, with Massie the sole GOP dissenter, to refer the articles to the judiciary committee, though incoming Democratic Speaker Hakeem Jeffries called Hegseth the architect of a reckless war of choice and signalled Democrats will revisit his removal in January. Across the Potomac, nine Kennedy family members—excluding Health Secretary Robert F Kennedy Jr—joined lawsuits by Virginia and Washington DC to halt Trump’s planned 250ft triumphal arch near Arlington National Cemetery, arguing it would cast a shadow over John F Kennedy’s grave. (Guardian, BBC)

Labour MPs challenge Burnham’s 10-year industrial plan as Louise Casey warns England’s social care has no one in charge

Labour MPs from industrial and former red wall seats are meeting Cabinet Office minister Sally Jameson on Wednesday to warn that Prime Minister Andy Burnham’s 10-year plan for government lacks the teeth needed to halt deindustrialisation. Led by Andy MacNae and Jonathan Hinder, the Reindustrialisation Research Group argues that heavy industry is being crushed by high energy prices and that early briefings on the blueprint—which covers public ownership of utilities, lower household bills and a national care service, but has been delayed until January—fall short of Chancellor John Healey’s promise of a new industrial age. Their warning follows a London School of Economics lecture on Tuesday by Cabinet Secretary Antonia Romeo, attended by Burnham and Deputy Prime Minister Louise Haigh, in which Whitehall’s top official called the civil service too tame, reactive and risk-averse and pledged performance bonuses, tougher management of poor delivery and less sideways job-hopping ahead of a spring review. (Guardian, Guardian)

Public services and rural estates face sharp warnings elsewhere on Wednesday. Addressing the annual children and adult services conference, Baroness Louise Casey, chair of the adult social care commission, warns that successive governments have been asleep at the wheel, leaving millions reliant on an under-regulated postcode lottery dominated by private providers where nobody is in charge. Burnham has accelerated Casey’s review to 2027 and plans to fund a Scottish-style National Care Service by scrapping the state pension triple lock in the next parliament. Meanwhile, the Tenant Farmers Association warned that up to 40 holdings across Devon, Cornwall, Herefordshire and the Midlands face sell-offs as Prince William’s Duchy of Cornwall disposes of up to 20% of its 51,800-hectare estate over ten years to raise £500m for community and environmental projects, leaving Bradninch and Duloe tenants fearing they must buy their farms or leave; and the Office for Students warned that unsupervised assessments at 93 UK universities let students pass coursework in minutes using undetectable artificial intelligence. (Guardian, BBC, BBC)

Israeli opposition moves to seize Knesset agenda as UN chief presses Cop31 hosts on fossil fuel phase-out

Israel’s six opposition parties moved immediately after Tuesday’s swearing-in of the new Knesset to wrest control of the parliamentary agenda from caretaker prime minister Benjamin Netanyahu, while former military chief Gadi Eisenkot begins a six-week mandate to form a coalition. Opposition lawmakers tabled bills to establish a state inquiry into the 7 October 2023 attacks, repeal Netanyahu’s judicial overhaul and impose prime ministerial term limits that would bar Netanyahu—who has ruled for 19 of the past 30 years—from returning to office, while scheduling a vote next Tuesday to replace speaker Amir Ohana. Netanyahu used the ceremony to label lawmakers from two Palestinian-led opposition parties terror supporters and repeat baseless claims of ballot fraud in Arab communities, prompting walkouts by Democrats leader Yair Golan and former police chief Yoav Segalovich. (Guardian)

At the Cop31 summit in Antalya, outgoing UN Secretary-General António Guterres joined former US climate envoy John Kerry and Brazilian negotiators on Tuesday to urge co-hosts Turkey and Australia to turn Brazil’s post-Cop30 fossil fuel transition roadmap into concrete financing before 90 world leaders, including Burnham, Anthony Albanese and Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, speak on Wednesday and Thursday, as World Meteorological Organization data confirmed 2026 will cap the four hottest years on record at 1.47C above pre-industrial levels. Elsewhere, Ukraine’s I Want to Live project announced the capture in Zaporizhzhia of its first Indonesian prisoner of war—a 49-year-old from Java—after Russian recruiters lured 283 Indonesians in 2026 via fake job ads; and in Hamburg, Attorney General Ellie Reeves told the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea that Argentina’s bid to halt March 2027 drilling at the Sea Lion oilfield was a disguised sovereignty claim that would cost Falkland Islanders billions of pounds. (Guardian, Meio, Guardian, Guardian)

Brazil’s Federal Police rift deepens over Banco Master removals as electoral court fines Meta R$7.5m

The removal of two senior Federal Police commissioners who investigated ties between Banco Master controller Daniel Vorcaro and three Supreme Court justices has triggered an institutional rupture inside Brazil’s federal police. Director-general Andrei Rodrigues resigned from the National Association of Federal Police Commissioners (ADPF) on Tuesday after the body condemned Monday’s dismissal of anti-corruption and Supreme Court inquiries chiefs Daniel Mostardeiro Cola and Wedson Cajé Lopes as retaliation, while Transparency International Brazil warned the removals carried extreme gravity. Meanwhile, a confidential spin-off from the Supreme Court’s newly archived seven-year fake news inquiry—examining leaked Federal Revenue tax records of justices and their relatives—was reassigned to Justice André Mendonça, Senate President Davi Alcolumbre praised Chief Justice Edson Fachin’s closure of the main probe as he seeks re-election, and Justice Luiz Fux suspended lower-court lawsuits nationwide challenging the provisional measure banning online sports betting and virtual casinos. (Folha, Folha, Folha, Meio)

In electoral law, the Superior Electoral Court (TSE) voted unanimously on Tuesday night to fine Meta R$7.5m (£1m) for failing to ensure equal treatment between President Lula and Senator Flávio Bolsonaro on Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp during the presidential campaign. In the Chamber of Deputies, Centrão party leaders told Speaker Hugo Motta that their caucuses had rebelled against a deal to introduce mixed-district voting from 2034—originally intended to curb Liberal Party vote-pullers such as Nikolas Ferreira and Lucas Pavanato, who each won over three million votes this year—forcing negotiators to consider higher electoral thresholds instead. Separately, a Brasília federal court ordered the Caixa Econômica Federal to release R$2.42m from the 2018 auction of the Guarujá triplex to the former OAS group, now Grupo Metha, ruling that no criminal claim remained over the flat once attributed to Lula under Operation Car Wash. (Folha, Folha, Folha)

Economy

Anthropic trims Wall Street IPO target to $1.4tn as Niesr warns Bank of England rate caution risks sticky inflation

Artificial intelligence developer Anthropic is seeking a valuation of up to $1.4tn (£1.1tn) in next week’s Wall Street flotation, scaling back earlier expectations of a $2tn debut after US midterm victories for anti-datacentre candidates sharpened investor doubts over returns on AI infrastructure. According to a Tuesday filing, the San Francisco creator of Claude aims to raise up to $64.4bn by selling 825 million shares at $70 to $78, alongside a $26bn private placement with Alphabet and Nvidia and a $9.7bn greenshoe option that could lift total proceeds to $100bn—surpassing the $75bn raised when Elon Musk’s SpaceX listed at $1.78tn. Chief executive Dario Amodei and six co-founders are each selling $260m in stock while keeping stakes above $18bn. Meanwhile, Nvidia—down $500bn in value since Friday—hired New York law firm Wachtell, Lipton, Rosen & Katz, led by partner Joon Kim, to investigate chief executive Jensen Huang’s affair with Megaspeed executive and suspected Chinese operative Alice Huang. (Guardian, BBC)

In Britain, the National Institute of Economic and Social Research (Niesr) warned on Wednesday that the Bank of England’s quarter-point rate rise from 3.75% to 4% last week risks leaving inflation above its 2% target until at least 2030. With inflation at 3.5% and forecast by Niesr director David Aikman to top 4% in early 2027 as pay deals are set, the thinktank argued further hikes are needed even after Brent crude dipped below $90 a barrel on Tuesday. Higher borrowing costs, stamp duty of up to 19% for overseas buyers and the end of non-dom status are also squeezing London’s luxury property sector: researcher Molior counted 1,400 unsold new flats in Zone 1 at the end of September, triple the 2016 total, while housing starts fell 75% to 1,600 and developers London Richmond and Ardmore Construction entered administration. Elsewhere, Aldi is cutting up to 150 UK tech roles after 103 finance redundancies and outsourcing work to India via Genpact as UK sales growth slowed to 0.4%, while the Access to Medicine Foundation warned that pharma firms’ focus on GLP-1 weight-loss drugs—which just received US FDA warnings over sudden vision loss—risks starving poorer nations of insulin. (Guardian, Guardian, Guardian, Guardian)

Apple probes fatal gas leak at Indian iPhone plant, closes 28-year support forums and reveals PROLED MacBook Pro internals

Apple has sent investigators to a Tata Electronics component plant in Hosur, Tamil Nadu, after three contract workers died from inhaling gas while inspecting an external nano-filtration tank at an effluent treatment facility operated by Nalco Water. While production at the site—part of an Indian supply chain assembling roughly a quarter of global iPhones—was paused for Deepavali, the fatal accident hit the same complex where pollution regulators and health officials investigated farmland water contamination complaints earlier this year. Separately on Tuesday night, Apple placed its Apple Support Community forums into read-only mode ahead of their permanent closure on 20 November, retiring a user troubleshooting platform launched as Apple Tech Exchange in 1998. (9to5Mac, MacMagazine, 9to5Mac)

Across the ecosystem, an iFixit teardown of the touchscreen MacBook Pro with PROLED XDR display showed how Apple cut the 14-inch laptop’s weight from 1.55kg to 1.38kg, used larger blue subpixels in its dual-layer tandem OLED panel to extend display life, and revived the glowing lid logo with four LEDs, a diffuser and a two-way mirror coating—while letting swapped logic boards pass Touch ID calibration. Leaks also revealed a Claris-branded Mac app called Harbor that pools unified memory across multiple Macs over Thunderbolt 5 to run local open-weight AI models, while Apple TV adds toggleable American and British Sign Language overlays on Friday starting with Being Heumann and F1: The Movie, and Edovia’s Screens 5.10 brought macOS High Performance screen sharing and audio streaming to iPhone and iPad. Beyond Apple, smart-ring maker Oura agreed to buy medical AI startup Counsel Health for $625m, Adobe unveiled Firefly timeline video generation and Topaz upscaling at Adobe MAX, and Google DeepMind used agentic AI to formally verify the cryptographic security of the Signal protocol used by Signal, WhatsApp and Google Messages. (9to5Mac, 9to5Mac, MacStories, MacStories, The Verge, 9to5Mac)

GTA 6 breaks PlayStation and Xbox pre-order records as 30fps cap divides players and Sony cancels Fairgames

Sony and Microsoft confirmed on Tuesday that Grand Theft Auto VI is the most pre-ordered game in the history of both the PlayStation Store and Microsoft Store, with Sensor Tower estimating advance sales at 9.69 million units—beating Cyberpunk 2077—as console pre-loading opened ahead of the 19 November launch. Both companies urged players to pre-load early to avoid server bottlenecks, while Logitech introduced a Logi Game Day holiday letting staff take launch day off. Alongside a trailer showing SWAT raids and Vice City clubs, Rockstar confirmed GTA 6 will target 30 frames per second across all consoles, including PS5 Pro—which gets ray-traced shadows and PSSR upscaling rather than 60fps—and stream to Xbox One via Xbox Cloud Gaming. Meanwhile, Sony cancelled Haven Studios’ live-service heist shooter Fairgames after weak playtests, reassigning the Montreal team without layoffs and announcing the PlayStation Plus PC app will close on 10 December because under 0.4% of Premium members use it. (IGN, IGN, IGN, The Verge, The Verge)

Booker winner M John Harrison warns of societal confusion as Old Vic unveils 2027 season and Stacy Keach dies at 85

Speaking the morning after becoming the Booker Prize’s oldest laureate at 81—and its first science-fiction winner—for his 13th novel The End of Everything, M John Harrison warned that societies are dream-walking into a desperate situation marked by epistemological collapse. Begun after the 2008 crash and expanded after Harrison survived a heart attack at a 2015 east London reading, the Kent-set novel follows characters navigating a fracturing Britain alongside luminous beings called the iGhettis—a pun blending capitalism, Getty wealth, Apple tech and ego—while withholding neat explanations. Across the Thames at the Old Vic, incoming artistic director Rupert Goold unveiled his inaugural 2027 season, opening on 26 January with Damian Lewis as Henry V in his first major stage role in nearly a decade, followed by Supergirl star Milly Alcock in Rebecca Frecknall’s adaptation of Miss Julie, Stephen Sondheim’s Assassins, and Arinzé Kene in Kohlhaas, backed by £20 front-row preview tickets. (Guardian, Guardian)

The acting world also mourned American star Stacy Keach, who died at home in Magdalenka, Poland, on Tuesday aged 85 after heart failure. Trained at Berkeley, Yale and Lamda as a Fulbright scholar, Keach played Hamlet, Richard III and King Lear onstage, won a Golden Globe as Ernest Hemingway, worked twice with John Huston in Fat City and The Life and Times of Judge Roy Bean, and appeared in The Ninth Configuration, American History X, Nebraska and Jay Kelly, while finding television fame as Mickey Spillane’s detective Mike Hammer—a run interrupted in 1984 when he served six months in Reading prison after being arrested at Heathrow with cocaine in a shaving cream can. (Guardian)

Manchester United chiefs convene in Monaco over Carrick as Pochettino eyes return and Kane nears England cap record

Manchester United chief executive Omar Berrada and director of football Jason Wilcox fly to Monaco on Wednesday for a summit with minority owner Sir Jim Ratcliffe expected to seal the sacking of head coach Michael Carrick, with United 18th in the Premier League after one win in ten games. US men’s national team coach Mauricio Pochettino is keen on moving to Old Trafford—an interest described as mutual—though prising the Argentine from his contract through 2030 ahead of Concacaf Nations League games against Haiti would cost US Soccer’s $10m (£7.5m) release clause plus compensation for staff led by Jesús Pérez. Meanwhile, bottom club Crystal Palace could avoid a hefty payout to departing manager Pierre Sage—whom they decided to sack after Sunday’s 3-1 loss to Liverpool, with Thomas Frank favourite to replace him—after Lens sacked Yannick Cahuzac on Tuesday following a 9-1 Champions League defeat by Como and moved to rehire Sage; and Football Leaks founder Rui Pinto has opened talks via lawyer William Bourdon with the Serious Fraud Office over a cooperation deal following Manchester City’s financial charges ruling, as he fights the removal of his Portuguese witness protection. (BBC, Guardian, Guardian, Guardian)

In international football, England captain Harry Kane prepares to win his 126th cap against Croatia at Wembley on Thursday—passing Peter Shilton as England’s most-capped men’s player two weeks after winning the Ballon d’Or—crediting his improved sprint metrics at 33 to giving up post-match Haribo sweets, drinking bone broth and having monthly electrical acupuncture with specialist Alejandro Elorriaga Claraco. In Glasgow, Scotland midfielder Scott McTominay revealed ahead of Friday’s match against North Macedonia that his absence before June’s World Cup opener against Haiti in Boston was caused by an irregular heartbeat: the 29-year-old Napoli player was sedated in hospital 48 hours before kick-off to have his heart electrically shocked back into rhythm before surgery in September. (Guardian, Guardian)
