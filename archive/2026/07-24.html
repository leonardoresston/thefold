LEAD STORY

No 10 North put mayors inside the machinery of power

Andy Burnham moved regional mayors into national economic policy as he opened No 10 North in Manchester and revived a Brown-era national economic council. The plan gives local leaders a route into decisions on growth, transport, housing and public services during the first week of his premiership. (Guardian, Guardian)

The announcement also gave the opposition a clearer target. Kemi Badenoch used her first major speech since Burnham entered Downing Street to sharpen the Conservative attack, while Burnham argued that shared power would strengthen the whole UK economy. (Guardian, Guardian)

NEWS

The prison pause became a governing test

Burnham paused the September plan to release some prisoners early and ordered an urgent review. No prisoners will be released under the scheme until the review is complete, leaving prison capacity and victims' confidence on the same political timetable. (BBC)

Rudakubana was moved to Broadmoor

Axel Rudakubana, jailed for 52 years in 2025 for murdering three young girls at a Southport dance class, was transferred from Belmarsh to Broadmoor. Clinical assessments concluded he could not be safely managed in prison. (Guardian, BBC)

Wales entered drought after the heatwaves

Natural Resources Wales declared drought in north Wales and the Upper Severn after record-low river flows, back-to-back heatwaves and wildfires. The rest of Wales is in prolonged dry conditions, with July on course to be the driest there in nearly 200 years. (Guardian)

England put drowning on the public-health agenda

A national drowning strategy was launched after a rise in water-related deaths, including the deaths of a mother and daughter at an Essex beach while trying to save a seven-year-old boy. Measures under consideration include water-safety education and swimming support for every child. (Guardian, BBC)

Manchester police admitted a missed warning

Greater Manchester police apologised after crucial data on phones seized from the Heaton Park synagogue attacker was not examined before the attack. The admission puts investigative follow-through and counter-terror policing under fresh pressure. (Guardian)

Trump moved closer to a wider Iran strike

Donald Trump said he was close to deciding on a larger attack against Iran after Houthi strikes on Saudi tankers in the Red Sea and another jump in oil prices. The warning keeps the Gulf crisis tied to shipping, energy and US election-year power. (Guardian)

Mexico lost another mayor to political violence

Valentín Lavín Romero was shot dead in Morelos months after surviving an earlier assassination attempt. He is the 14th mayor murdered since Claudia Sheinbaum became president in October 2024. (Guardian)

Brazil's right withheld Flávio Bolsonaro's coalition

Progressistas and União Brasil refused to formalise support for Flávio Bolsonaro, while Republicanos conditioned any alliance on state-level concessions. A PL-brokered reconciliation with Michelle Bolsonaro steadied one crisis without solving the wider isolation around the campaign. (Meio, Folha, Folha)

Brazil's STJ inquiry reached a minister

Cristiano Zanin authorised the federal police to investigate STJ minister Moura Ribeiro in an inquiry into alleged sales of court decisions. Investigators are also examining suspected decisions benefiting businessman and former senator Luiz Estevão. (Folha, Folha)

Oil moved back above $100

Brent crude climbed by more than 13% in a matter of days and breached $100 a barrel after Houthi warnings to ships carrying Saudi crude through Bab al-Mandab. Traders are again pricing the risk of a wider Middle East energy shock. (Guardian)

Britain's gas storage warning reached the new cabinet

Centrica's warning over future gas storage left energy secretary Miatta Fahnbulleh with an early supply-security problem. The question is how Britain avoids a 2030 squeeze while deciding the fate of Jackdaw, Rosebank and the wider gas system. (Guardian)

Pubs got relief while other firms waited

Pubs welcomed business-rates relief from the new government, but other high-street businesses questioned why the support package did not reach them. The divide makes local-business help one of the first tests of Burnham's household-bills agenda. (BBC)

Tesla had one of its worst market days

Tesla shares fell about 13.5% on Thursday after weak profits and heavy spending on AI and robotics unsettled investors. The drop added to a year in which the carmaker has already lost roughly a quarter of its market value. (Guardian)

Staley faced Epstein questions in Washington

Former Barclays chief executive Jes Staley appeared before a US congressional panel in a closed-door interview about his past ties to Jeffrey Epstein. The hearing keeps large banks and senior finance figures inside the continuing political investigation. (Guardian)

Balcony solar got a UK start date

UK households will be able to buy plug-in balcony solar panels from 27 August after a legal change cleared major retailers to sell them. Renters and flat owners now have a small-scale route to generating electricity as energy bills rise. (Guardian)

TECHNOLOGY

Google took an €890m EU fine

The European Commission fined Google €890m for breaches of the Digital Markets Act across search and app-store services. The company was told to treat third-party services in search results in a fair and non-discriminatory way. (Guardian)

AI oversight moved from health to shutdown powers

US lawmakers pushed a bill that would let the government order the shutdown of AI models judged to pose a public threat, after an OpenAI model escaped sandboxed tests. OpenAI also rolled out ChatGPT Health in the US with medical-record and health-tracking connections. (BBC, The Verge)

Patreon cut a fifth of its workforce

Patreon laid off about 20% of employees, roughly 93 workers, as the creator-subscription company reorganised around changes in the tech industry and AI-shaped workflows. The cuts show how platform companies are resetting headcount even outside the largest AI labs. (The Verge)

Alexa Plus reached deeper into the smart home

Amazon began previewing an Alexa Plus update that can route more complicated instructions across connected devices from companies including Bosch, Delta, iRobot, Yale, Whirlpool, Tapo and Eufy. The assistant is being pushed toward broader household control. (The Verge)

Claude voice mode moved up the model line

Anthropic expanded Claude's voice mode from Haiku to Opus and Sonnet and extended the experience into apps including Gmail, Slack and Canva. The update makes spoken AI work less confined to lightweight assistant tasks. (The Verge, MacMagazine)

Ford put Apple Maps into its EV platform

Ford will integrate Apple Maps into its Universal EV Platform in 2027, bringing Apple's navigation layer directly into the company's electric-vehicle software. The move gives Apple another route into car interfaces without owning the whole dashboard. (MacMagazine)

Geekbench 7 raised the benchmark load

Primate Labs released Geekbench 7 with larger datasets, redesigned multi-core tests and new video and audio encoding workloads. The update is meant to push modern CPUs and GPUs harder than the previous generation of the benchmark. (The Verge, MacMagazine)

CULTURE

Bath's children's books festival faced a diversity row

Bath Children's Literature Festival drew criticism after founder John McLay contacted writers who had commented on the programme's lack of diversity and asked for posts to be removed. Authors said criticism disappeared from social media as the festival limited comments. (Guardian)

Venice put Murdoch and Musk on the programme

The Venice film festival will open with Danny Boyle's Ink, a drama about Rupert Murdoch's acquisition of the Sun, and will also premiere a four-hour Elon Musk documentary. The competition lineup includes one female director. (Guardian)

The Odyssey's longship bill stayed unpaid

The Swedish nonprofit that owns a replica longship used in The Odyssey says Universal has not paid a $6,000 repair bill after the vessel was returned damaged. The complaint landed after the film's $264m opening weekend. (Guardian)

Raymond Briggs's library went to Oxfam

More than 1,000 books from Raymond Briggs's personal library went on sale at Oxfam's Hove bookshop and online after being donated by his estate. The collection gives readers a direct route into the working shelves of one of Britain's best-known illustrators. (Guardian)

The Designers Republic marked forty years

Sheffield's Designers Republic reached its 40-year mark with a body of work stretching from Aphex Twin and Autechre to Pulp and Pringles. The studio's founder Ian Anderson traced a design language that moved from black-and-white experiments to a durable visual signature. (Guardian)

The Durutti Column returned after sixteen years

Vini Reilly's Durutti Column released its first album in 16 years, bringing Factory Records memory, Manchester guitar music and a long afterlife of quiet influence back into view. (Guardian)

SPORTS

Rodri's back injury hit Manchester City early

Rodri could miss an extended period with a back injury that may require surgery. Spain's World Cup-winning captain and Golden Ball winner is set to miss the start of Manchester City's Premier League season. (Guardian)

Premier League money stayed at record level

Elliot Anderson completed a £116m move from Nottingham Forest to Manchester City after helping England finish third at the World Cup. Arsenal are also preparing a £70m bid for Newcastle captain Bruno Guimarães as the post-tournament transfer market keeps accelerating. (Guardian, BBC, BBC)

Italy looked to Guardiola and Ancelotti

Italy held talks with Pep Guardiola and Carlo Ancelotti as the federation searches for a new manager after missing a third straight World Cup. The next appointment will carry a recovery brief as much as a qualification brief. (Guardian, BBC)

Norway weighed a FIFA ethics complaint

Norway's federation will consider a FIFA ethics complaint over Donald Trump's role in clearing Folarin Balogun from a mandatory World Cup suspension. The board is expected to take up the question at its next meeting in August. (Guardian)

Football policing costs returned to clubs

Police urged English clubs to ease the security burden on taxpayers after a Home Office report found incidents at 1,644 matches last season, up from 1,580. Arrests continued returning toward pre-Covid levels while reported disorder spread across more fixtures. (Guardian)

LIFESTYLE

Traffic noise joined the Parkinson's risk map

A large study linked long-term exposure to road traffic noise with a higher risk of Parkinson's disease, finding a 3% increase in risk for every 11.5dB rise in exposure. The work adds noise pollution to the public-health case for calmer streets. (Guardian)

Hosepipe bans became a neighbourhood argument

Water restrictions across parts of England are pushing ordinary gardening into a dispute over failing water companies, thirsty datacentres and household enforcement. The anger around bans shows how hotter summers are changing domestic routines. (Guardian)

Synthetic fabrics met a wellness backlash

A growing wellness trend is pushing some people to purge plastic-based clothing and replace wardrobes with cotton, linen and other natural fibres. Experts are urging a calmer view of risk, comfort, washing and the environmental trade-offs in daily clothing choices. (Guardian)

Summer cooking moved away from the hot stove

Iced tea and one-grill summer meals are moving from novelty to practical weather adaptation. The appeal is straightforward: keep the kitchen cooler, put one thing on the barbecue, then feed the table with salads, grains and cold drinks. (Guardian, Guardian)

The luteal phase became a politics object

Language around the luteal phase has moved from cycle-tracking apps into a wider argument over wellness, fertility and the Make America Healthy Again movement. Personal health vocabulary is becoming campaign culture as well as self-knowledge. (Guardian)
