Trump and Xi bring the contest for artificial intelligence to the table

Donald Trump and Xi Jinping meet today with artificial intelligence sitting close to the centre of their economic rivalry. American companies still make most of the highest-performing frontier models, and their lead rests on extraordinary flows of capital into chips, data centres and the laboratories building them. China has answered with capable open-weight models that can be downloaded and adapted, cheaper electricity for developers and a manufacturing base that has already put more than two million robots into its factories. The gap is real, but it is narrow enough to keep both governments moving at speed. Stanford researchers found that Anthropic’s leading model was ahead of its nearest competitor by 2.7% in March. China’s strongest systems now trail the American frontier by months, rather than years. (BBC)

The two countries are also pursuing different forms of advantage. Washington has the most powerful proprietary models and the chip restrictions capable of slowing Chinese training runs. Beijing wants useful AI to become cheap, widely available and embedded throughout the economy. Its open systems can spread through countries and companies that cannot afford premium American services, giving China influence through adoption. The same industrial depth makes it the dominant producer of humanoid robots, with more than nine-tenths of global shipments in the first half of this year. A summit discussion about AI therefore reaches far beyond a conventional technology race. It covers energy, export controls, factory productivity, military capability and the software standards other countries may eventually inherit. (BBC)

The safety argument arrived at the United Nations a day before the presidents sat down. OpenAI’s Sam Altman, Anthropic’s Dario Amodei and Hugging Face’s Clement Delangue each asked governments to create common ways to evaluate powerful models, disclose incidents and measure whether meaningful human control remains. Their position is unusually direct for companies competing to build the technology: failures in one laboratory can cross borders, and voluntary promises offer no shared evidence about what a system can do. The urgency grew after AI agents breached Hugging Face and Australia’s Medicare system. Capabilities once discussed as future risks are beginning to appear in ordinary networks. (BBC)

The White House rejected that route. Trump’s technology adviser Michael Kratsios accepted that development is accelerating and carries risks, while opposing any international structure that might slow it. Trump has made leadership over China the measure of success and treats restraint as a strategic concession. That leaves a hard problem for today’s meeting. Each side wants confidence that the other will keep advanced systems under human control, yet neither wants an inspection regime that reveals secrets or constrains its pace. The useful first step may be smaller: rapid notice of serious incidents, a common vocabulary for dangerous capabilities and a reliable channel between governments when an autonomous system behaves unexpectedly. The AI contest will continue. Rules for the moments when it goes wrong cannot wait for someone to declare a winner. (BBC, BBC)

News & Economy

Britain moves climate risk into the national-security room

Foreign secretary Ed Miliband has shown international counterparts the findings of a British intelligence assessment that links the collapse of natural systems to conflict, migration and food shortages. The Joint Intelligence Committee study was due for publication last October before Downing Street under Keir Starmer stopped its release; only a redacted version emerged in January. At a private gathering during the UN general assembly, ministers from the EU, Kenya and other countries heard that an unusually intense El Niño could push damaged food and water systems through several crises at once. This year’s heat, poor harvests and food-price rises have already provided a smaller version of that chain. A failed crop can raise import costs, weaken households, move people across borders and stretch governments that were budgeting for a weather event. (Guardian)

The government will create a ministerial taskforce bringing military and security specialists together with climate scientists, appoint a special representative for climate security and provide £330m to the Global Environment Facility. Those steps recognise that resilience belongs inside defence and foreign-policy planning, where spending decisions are made before a disaster. They also sharpen the argument over secrecy. Some intelligence detail plainly needs protection, but Parliament still lacks the full assessment and the government has not published its response. Britain is asking other countries to act on findings that its own MPs cannot properly examine. A credible security policy needs protected evidence, public priorities and enough scrutiny to show which risks are being funded. (Guardian)

Gaza has a six-month recovery plan and a decade-long funding gap

Donald Trump’s Board of Peace has set out a $2.45bn programme for Gaza covering temporary housing, diesel power, waste removal and 63 other projects over six months. The scale is substantial for emergency work and small beside the destruction. The board estimates that full recovery will cost $71.4bn over a decade. Donors have pledged $18bn and transferred only $280m, leaving the first package underfunded before unexploded weapons, damaged tunnels and the security of workers are fully resolved. A ceasefire still exists, all hostages have returned and humanitarian operations have expanded, but Israeli attacks have killed at least 1,381 Palestinians since the truce began, including 300 children, according to figures used by the UN. Five Israeli soldiers have been killed in combat. (BBC)

Reconstruction also depends on political questions that concrete cannot settle. Hamas has accepted a plan to disarm if Israel ends its attacks and withdraws, while Benjamin Netanyahu says Israeli forces will remain until disarmament is genuine. The board still needs agreement between Israeli and Palestinian officials on security and governance, as well as a way to let civilians return safely while ordnance is cleared. Its announcement creates a practical sequence: restore basic services, make neighbourhoods safe and begin permanent building. The danger lies in allowing the impressive headline figure to stand in for money received or work completed. Monthly disclosures of donor payments, contracts, cleared sites and homes restored would turn the plan into something Palestinians can measure. (BBC)

Vocational GCSEs promise a clearer route from school to work

Fourteen-year-olds in England will be offered nationally regulated vocational GCSEs from 2029 in fields likely to include construction, finance, business management and child development. They will sit alongside English, maths and science, gradually replacing qualifications such as BTec tech awards and linking at 16 to V-levels, T-levels and college courses. Andy Burnham has also asked employers to create 200,000 work-experience places for pupils under 16. The reform answers a familiar weakness in English education: practical study has often carried less status and a less legible progression route than an academic subject. Giving it a GCSE name and common Ofqual standards could make the choice easier for pupils, parents and employers to understand. (BBC, Guardian)

The difficult part begins inside schools. Practical courses need specialist teachers, equipment, workshops and placements that reflect real employment. Further-education colleges already struggle to recruit staff, while headteachers say existing work experience can be extremely hard to secure. England has also seen technical qualifications redesigned repeatedly, leaving families wary of another name that may change before a child finishes the course. A durable system will require colleges and employers to help deliver teaching, multi-year funding for facilities and published outcomes showing where each qualification leads. The safeguards matter most for disadvantaged pupils, who should gain an additional route without being steered away from demanding academic choices. (BBC, Guardian)

Brazil’s election moves into the courts as the decisive voters hold back

Lula’s campaign has asked the Supreme Court to investigate possible American interference in Brazil’s election and filed a separate case at the electoral court against presidential candidate Flávio Bolsonaro and his brother Eduardo. The PT alleges that the brothers coordinated with Donald Trump’s government to create institutional and electoral instability, pointing to tariffs, visa cancellations for Supreme Court judges, Magnitsky sanctions against Alexandre de Moraes and the American designation of two Brazilian criminal groups as terrorist organisations. It wants Flávio and his running mate, Alfredo Gaspar, removed from the ballot and the brothers made ineligible for office. The filing follows an Abin intelligence assessment that identified US interference as a possible threat to the 2026 vote. These are serious allegations at the start of a legal process, twelve days before polling day. (Folha)

The contest remains open because neither leading campaign has secured the voters who drifted away from both camps. Comparable Quaest surveys place Lula at 37%, seven points below his position at the same stage in 2022, while Flávio’s 33% is one point below his father’s old score. Indecision and blank votes have risen sharply among women, residents of the south-east and households earning two to five minimum wages. Many are women supporting a family through formal work, self-employment and unpaid care, with violence, food prices and corruption pressing on daily decisions. Lula has concentrated on benefits and international stature; Flávio has spoken about debt and interest while spending little campaign time in the big urban peripheries where these voters live. The courts may define the legal boundaries of foreign influence. The election is still likely to turn on whether either candidate reaches the households waiting to hear a credible plan for ordinary life. (Meio, Folha)

Bentley’s first electric car puts £350m behind the Crewe factory

Bentley will build its first fully electric car in Crewe as part of a £350m investment that secures work for the factory’s 4,000 employees. The Torcal SUV starts at £173,000, travels up to 375 miles and can charge from 10% to 80% in 20 minutes. It even offers a synthetic engine sound assembled from drum recordings of the company’s old eight-cylinder cars, an attempt to give drivers familiar feedback without combustion. The launch is the third British car investment announced in little more than a week, following Nissan’s £170m commitment to Sunderland and McLaren’s £500m factory plan. The money will bring a new assembly line to Crewe, though Bentley expects no increase in headcount. (Guardian)

The car arrives after Bentley abandoned a plan to release a new electric model every year from 2025 to 2030. Sales have fallen by a third since their 2022 peak, 245 jobs were cut earlier this year and chief executive Frank-Steffen Walliser is cautious about demand beyond Britain, Europe and the Middle East. The wider industry faces American tariffs, Chinese competition and high energy costs, while Bentley’s owner Volkswagen intends to shed 100,000 jobs by 2030. A single luxury SUV cannot settle those pressures. It does place engineering work and a production line in Britain while the market decides how quickly wealthy buyers will leave petrol behind. (Guardian)

Technology & Culture

Microsoft’s small Surfaces become faster, brighter and much dearer

Microsoft has upgraded the 12-inch Surface Pro and 13-inch Surface Laptop with Qualcomm’s six-core Snapdragon X2 Plus while leaving their designs largely unchanged. The new chip promises 60% faster graphics, 95% faster local AI processing and better efficiency. Web-browsing battery life rises to 13 hours on the Pro and 18 hours on the Laptop, and both screens reach 500 nits instead of 400. The practical gains are welcome in compact machines, especially when more AI work can happen on the device without sending every task to a server. Ports and displays remain modest: the Pro has two USB-C connections and a 90Hz LCD, while the Laptop adds USB-A and a headphone socket to a 60Hz panel. (Verge)

The price change is harder to miss. Last year’s small Pro began at $799.99 and now starts at $1,149.99; the Laptop moves from $899.99 to $1,199.99. Both start with 16GB of memory rather than 8GB, which removes a weak configuration, but the jump also reflects the continuing pressure on memory supply. Microsoft is selling an efficient second edition rather than a new category of device. Buyers gain useful performance and a more sensible base specification, while losing the low entry price that made the smaller Surface line attractive in the first place. The machines ship on 13 October. (Verge)

Michael Kiwanuka’s stroke gives a vast hidden health problem a human face

Michael Kiwanuka has disclosed that he is recovering from a severe brain bleed caused by high blood pressure. The 39-year-old London singer has months of rehabilitation ahead and cannot yet say when he will tour again. He made the news public while announcing Fudge, his fifth album, which was recorded before the stroke and delayed after illness disrupted his plans. Kiwanuka has spent fifteen years building one of Britain’s most distinctive songwriting careers, from Home Again and Love & Hate to the self-titled album that won the 2020 Mercury prize. His gratitude to the NHS and determination to return sit alongside an honest uncertainty about what recovery from a serious brain injury will allow. (BBC, Guardian)

His experience landed on the same day as the largest study yet of hypertension in England. Oxford researchers examining 1.4 million adults found that 37% had high blood pressure and 59% of those people did not know it. Among patients who had been diagnosed and were taking medication, half still recorded an uncontrolled reading. A single measurement cannot establish someone’s usual pressure, and the study could not verify prescriptions independently, yet the population-level warning is clear. Detection improved in the 2000s and then stalled around 2011, with further disruption after the pandemic. Regular checks, follow-up and help with doses, side-effects, diet and treatment routines could prevent tens of thousands of strokes and heart attacks. Hypertension stays invisible until someone looks for it; Kiwanuka’s news shows how suddenly its consequences can become visible. (Guardian)

Sports & Gaming

England return from World Cup pain to face the champions

Harry Kane says England’s World Cup semi-final defeat by Argentina hurt more than any previous tournament exit, including his missed penalty against France in Qatar. England led the eventual champions after 55 minutes and then withdrew from the kind of assertive football the team had spent the tournament developing, losing 2-1. That manner of defeat has stayed with the captain because it exposed a recurring problem: against the strongest opponents in the latest rounds, England struggle to preserve their usual intensity and choices. Saturday’s Nations League match with Spain at Wembley offers an immediate chance to show that the lesson has travelled from discussion to the pitch. (Guardian)

Thomas Tuchel must begin that repair without Declan Rice, Cole Palmer, Kobbie Mainoo, Marcus Rashford and Tino Livramento. Rice is managing persistent nerve pain in a hamstring, a reminder that the international calendar demands reflection and another match at the same time. Kane is four appearances short of Peter Shilton’s England record of 125 and could equal it during this window; he is also close to extending his Bayern Munich contract. Those landmarks provide continuity after a difficult summer. The more important measure will be whether England can keep the ball, press and make brave decisions when Spain begins to control the game. Healing in elite football usually looks like another demanding fixture. (Guardian)

Xbox’s new map brings Obsidian under Bethesda and more layoffs to the studios

Microsoft has placed Obsidian Entertainment inside Bethesda as it reorganises Xbox around its largest franchises. Obsidian will keep its leadership and creative identity, according to a staff memo from Bethesda head Jill Braff, while Bethesda Game Studios remains in charge of Fallout. The two teams already have an unannounced Fallout project under way, alongside remasters of Fallout 3 and Fallout: New Vegas; Fallout 5 is in pre-production and The Elder Scrolls 6 remains Bethesda’s main focus. Obsidian’s history with New Vegas makes the pairing easy to understand. Its long-term independence inside that structure will depend on whether it continues to build ideas beyond the franchise it once borrowed. (IGN)

The organisational chart is being redrawn through job losses. The latest round removed 268 people, part of nearly 2,000 cuts in the wider reset. Juan Vaca, story director of Gears of War: E-Day, was laid off days before the game enters early access and shortly after celebrating that development was complete. Halo’s cinematic director lost his job a day after a promotion, with Activision taking charge of the next mainline Halo while a smaller team maintains existing games. Rare and World’s Edge have also moved under Activision. Microsoft is concentrating money and authority around brands it already knows can travel across games, television and subscriptions. That may produce clearer accountability. It also removes experienced people at the moment their work reaches players, thinning the creative memory the reorganised studios will need for whatever comes next. (IGN, IGN)
