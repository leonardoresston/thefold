The civilians caught between two systems of violence

Independent experts appointed by the UN Human Rights Council have found reasonable grounds to believe that the United States committed war crimes in two missile strikes on Iran on 28 February. At least 156 people, including 120 children, were killed when Tomahawk missiles hit Shajareh Tayyebeh Primary School in Minab. A second attack scattered tungsten pellets across a sports complex and nearby homes in Lamerd, killing at least 21 civilians, seven of them children. Both sites were clearly identifiable civilian facilities, and investigators found no indication that either was being used for military purposes. Their conclusion is carefully framed in the language of an inquiry, yet the human facts beneath it are stark: two places built for learning and play became scenes of mass death on the first day of the US-Israeli war with Iran. (BBC)

The school stood next to a naval complex operated by Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, which was also attacked that day. Survivors said the school was struck twice. The experts concluded that it was the intended point of impact and ruled out collateral damage from a missile aimed at the military compound. They cited the weapons used, media accounts of a preliminary American inquiry and the fact that the US is the only combatant with Tomahawk missiles. In Lamerd, the same reasoning centred on Precision Strike Missiles, a weapon held only by the US. Washington has denied carrying out the Lamerd attack and has yet to publish its investigation into Minab. The State Department rejected the UN mission’s findings, while the defence secretary has maintained that American forces would never target civilians. (BBC)

The report also documents the violence inflicted on Iranians by their own state. Security forces responding to mass anti-government protests in December and January fired from rooftops into crowds, used metal shotgun pellets that blinded demonstrators, entered healthcare facilities and detained wounded people and medical workers. Tens of thousands of detainees faced torture, isolation and denial of legal counsel. At least 29 men have since been executed on charges connected to the unrest. The official death toll of 3,000 could not be independently verified; the US-based Human Rights Activists News Agency says it has confirmed more than 7,000 deaths, almost all of them protesters. Investigators found that the scale and pattern amounted to crimes against humanity, including murder, imprisonment and torture. (BBC)

Taken together, the findings resist the comforting simplicity of choosing one perpetrator and letting the other fade from view. Iranian civilians have endured systematic repression from the government that claims to defend them and attacks that the UN experts believe violated the laws of war. Accountability now depends on institutions willing to preserve evidence, publish military findings and pursue responsibility across borders. Neither government has accepted the report’s conclusions. The immediate obligation remains plain: civilian protection cannot become conditional on which flag appears on the weapon or the uniform. (BBC)

News

The Bank holds, with another rise moving closer

The Bank of England has kept its base rate at 3.75%, but the pause offers little reassurance to households hoping that borrowing costs have peaked. The monetary policy committee voted six to three to hold as higher energy prices from the Middle East war feed into inflation. Petrol and diesel prices rose by almost a quarter as annual inflation climbed from 2.9% in July to 3.1% in August. The Bank now expects inflation to reach 4% early next year. Its central concern is duration: energy costs have so far had a limited effect on wages and companies’ pricing decisions, though a long conflict raises the chance that those pressures become embedded. Traders now see a possible quarter-point increase as early as November and rates reaching 4.75% next year. (Guardian)

Threadneedle Street also proposed a striking change to the long unwind of its financial-crisis bond purchases. It wants to sell £146bn of government bonds back to the Treasury at roughly £20bn a year until 2034, subject to the chancellor’s approval next April. The Treasury’s Debt Management Office could then finance the buyback by issuing shorter-dated debt, avoiding a market already struggling to absorb long-term gilts. Quantitative tightening has reduced the Bank’s holdings from a peak of £895bn to about £488bn. Under the proposal, it would keep around £120bn to back notes and coins and allow much of the remainder to mature. The arrangement could reduce disorder in gilt markets, but it also places the costs of past monetary policy more visibly inside the public finances just before next month’s budget. (Guardian)

Brazil’s election becomes a margin-of-error contest

Brazil’s presidential race has tightened into a technical tie with a little over two weeks until the vote. A new Datafolha survey puts President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva on 39% in the first round and Senator Flávio Bolsonaro on 36%, a three-point gap within the poll’s two-point margin of error. Lula leads 46% to 44% in a direct second-round contest. The same survey gives both men a rejection rate of 47%, capturing an election in which strong support and strong resistance are advancing together. Datafolha interviewed 2,002 voters in 125 municipalities between Tuesday and Thursday. The campaign is moving against the backdrop of a crisis at the Supreme Federal Court, where arguments over judges’ links to former Banco Master owner Daniel Vorcaro have spilled into a public confrontation among ministers and into each candidate’s attack lines. (Folha, Meio)

The electorate remains sharply divided by income, region and religion. Lula leads among the poorest voters, people with less formal education, north-easterners and Catholics. Flávio is ahead among higher-income groups, southern voters and evangelicals; the populous south-east is effectively tied. The gap between the two leading candidates has fallen from ten points in May to three. Government ratings are also mixed: 42% describe Lula’s administration as bad or terrible, 34% as good or excellent and 23% as regular. Those figures leave each campaign searching for persuasion at the edges while relying on very different social coalitions to turn out. (Folha)

Lula has announced a 15% increase in Bolsa Família, raising the minimum monthly payment from R$600 to R$691 and the average benefit from R$675 to R$777. The timing has already produced a challenge at the electoral court from an ally of Flávio Bolsonaro, who alleges abuse of political power during the campaign. Lula’s own team sought an investigation when Jair Bolsonaro expanded social benefits during the 2022 election, so the argument carries an obvious echo. The programme’s recipients already favour Lula by 53% to 28%; among people outside beneficiary households, Flávio leads by a single point. The measure may reinforce the president’s strongest constituency, while the legal dispute ensures that the cost of living, state support and the conduct of the campaign now arrive as one political question. (Meio, Folha)

A narrow word could remove wide protections

More than 1,600 threatened species in the United States could lose a central layer of protection under a new Trump administration reading of the Endangered Species Act. A memo sent to Fish and Wildlife Service offices on 14 September redefines what it means to “take” an endangered animal. For more than three decades, the term has covered foreseeable harm caused by habitat destruction as well as deliberate hunting or capture. The new approach would generally require an intention to injure or kill. A ship that strikes a whale would escape the prohibition if its course was not set against the animal; cutting down a tree containing endangered bats would fall outside it unless killing or capturing them was the purpose. Logging, mining and construction could therefore proceed even when the likely harm to wildlife is known in advance. (Guardian)

The reinterpretation follows July’s removal of habitat modification and degradation from the regulatory definition of harm. Twenty states and Washington DC have sued over that earlier change. The administration says previous rules extended the law beyond the words enacted by Congress and burdened lawful land use. Environmental groups point to a 1995 Supreme Court decision that upheld the broader understanding of harm and are preparing another court fight. The legal argument turns on a few verbs, while its physical consequences stretch across forests, rivers and migration routes. Species protection often works before an animal is directly struck: it changes where a road runs, how a forest is cut and what risks a developer must avoid. Removing liability for foreseeable damage moves the law downstream, after the habitat and sometimes the animals have already gone. (Guardian)

Technology

Facebook’s deepfake rules fail two women in public life

Meta’s oversight board has ordered Facebook to remove two AI-generated videos targeting women in Britain and Europe, and described the company’s safeguards as fundamentally inadequate. One clip falsely depicted a Labour councillor in Scotland making inflammatory remarks about refugees. Another showed a young Muslim campaign volunteer giving health advice while performing absurd exercises and eating junk food. Manipulated posts aimed at the volunteer had accumulated tens of millions of views. Facebook initially left the Scottish video online even after the board raised it directly, finding that it broke neither content rules nor the threshold for an AI label. The board concluded that it violated the platform’s hateful-conduct policy by attributing sexual and criminal behaviour to refugees as a group, and that the second video breached rules against bullying and harassment. (Guardian)

The binding removal decisions deal with two posts; the broader recommendations address how deceptive material spreads. The board wants Meta to expand its use of “high risk” labels, reduce the algorithmic reach of labelled posts, add warning screens, penalise repeat distributors and publish clearer data about when labels are applied. It also wants the definition of manipulated imagery widened to cover private people shown saying or doing things they never said or did. These cases expose a gap between rules built around the content of a post and harm created through falsified identity. A deepfake can evade a narrow policy test while borrowing a real person’s face, reputation and vulnerability. Women who enter public discussion are especially easy targets because the false image travels long after a correction, and the cost of proving its origin falls on the person being imitated. (Guardian)

Apple asks a secret order to enter open court

Apple has challenged the British government’s refusal to confirm whether it issued a new order demanding access to encrypted data belonging to UK users. The hearing before the Investigatory Powers Tribunal concerned the secrecy around the Technical Capability Notice, leaving the legality of the demand itself for a later case. Apple argues that the government’s policy of neither confirming nor denying an order already discussed by politicians and officials prevents meaningful public debate and undermines open justice. Privacy International and Liberty are supporting the challenge. The tribunal will now decide whether the government may preserve that silence; a full hearing on the underlying notice is unlikely before next year. (9to5Mac)

The dispute began with a reported demand for access to iCloud content worldwide. Apple responded by withdrawing Advanced Data Protection from new UK users, because the feature gives several additional categories of iCloud data end-to-end encryption. Judges rejected an earlier attempt to conceal even the existence of Apple’s legal challenge. Pressure from Washington later led Britain to abandon the part covering American users, after which a second notice reportedly targeted users in the UK. The result is a policy with direct consequences for millions of customers that the public cannot inspect and the affected company is constrained from describing. Courts can assess classified material privately where genuine operational details require it. Apple’s argument asks whether the existence and basic reach of the policy must remain secret too. (9to5Mac)

The AI boom meets a public expecting job losses

People in 34 of 37 countries surveyed by Pew are more likely to believe artificial intelligence will destroy jobs over the next 20 years than create them. The study reached 42,151 people between February and May, before the summer’s sharper warnings about autonomous agents and catastrophic risk. Anxiety is particularly strong in wealthy economies: 76% of respondents in Australia and South Korea, and 71% in the United States, expect employment losses. That view sits uneasily beside the enormous capital flowing into datacentres, models and workplace agents. Companies describe productivity gains and new forms of work; much of the public hears a forecast about redundancy, weaker bargaining power and a more unequal distribution of income. (Verge)

The industry’s own language is changing. Leaders at Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, Microsoft and X have all entertained some form of slower frontier development or tighter measurement of progress. Their proposals arrive amid reports of rogue agents and newly disclosed safety incidents, while commercial competition and the geopolitical race with China still reward speed. The credibility test will be concrete: shared thresholds for dangerous capabilities, independent evaluation, disclosure of serious incidents and consequences when systems cross agreed lines. Voluntary caution can disappear as soon as a rival launches a stronger model. Public concern about jobs adds another dimension to that safety debate. A system can perform exactly as designed and still produce disruptive economic effects. Governments therefore need plans for labour, training and social protection alongside rules for model behaviour. (Verge)

The family people want is drifting out of reach

Britain’s fertility rate fell to a record 1.39 children per woman in 2025, part of a decline that now crosses rich and middle-income countries with very different cultures. Nearly half of Canadian women surveyed in their 40s said they had fewer children than they wanted. The United Arab Emirates, Chile and the Indian state of Tamil Nadu have lower birth rates than Germany. The numbers often enter public debate as forecasts about pension systems, tax bases and labour shortages. They also describe millions of private plans that were postponed until they quietly became impossible. Falling fertility contains chosen childlessness, medical infertility and smaller families that people welcome. It also contains an expanding group who wanted partners, homes, time or security and could not assemble them in the years when biology allowed. (Guardian)

The forces overlap long before a child is conceived. Expensive housing delays the first move into a stable home. Demanding jobs consume the hours in which relationships form and make career breaks feel dangerous. Dating apps create an abundance of possible matches while weakening the expectation that a promising connection should deepen. Young men and women are also moving apart politically in several countries. Then come the practical pressures of parenthood: childcare costs, fear of pregnancy, intensive standards, the loss of nearby family support and anxiety about the climate and the future. None provides a complete global explanation. Together they make partnership and parenthood feel like high-risk projects attempted with thin margins. (Guardian)

Politics has often approached low birth rates through payments, tax breaks and moral instruction aimed at women. Those tools miss the sequence that produces a family: meeting someone, trusting them, finding a place to live, sustaining two careers and believing support will remain when circumstances change. Policy cannot manufacture intimacy, and it can remove some of the penalties surrounding it. Affordable homes, predictable work, accessible fertility treatment, parental leave and childcare give people more room to act on preferences they already hold. The most useful measure of success may therefore be narrower than a national birth target: fewer people reaching middle age with a painful distance between the family they hoped for and the one material conditions permitted. (Guardian)

The cheap rooms behind a costly creative mythology

A new Tate Britain exhibition traces the point where 1990s British art, fashion, photography and music began moving together. Curated by Edward Enninful, The 90s: Fashion and Art includes Juergen Teller’s pink-haired portrait of Kate Moss, Sarah Lucas’s Pauline Bunny, Sean Ellis’s work with Alexander McQueen and photographs of ravers at the Haçienda. Many subjects and photographers were in their twenties, close enough in age and circumstances for the pictures to carry the ease of a shared scene. Magazines, labels, galleries and clubs supplied work, money and places to meet. The images now look like the beginning of established careers; at the time they were records of people improvising one. (Guardian)

The exhibition also recovers the material conditions beneath the style. Central flatshares could cost about £60 a week, disused warehouses and empty lidos could become party venues, and a music industry flush with CD revenue financed independent labels, publicists and magazines. Cheap space let artists live near one another and take poorly paid risks. An evening could unfold without phones recording every moment, giving photographers who carried cameras unusual access and status. London has since converted many of those informal places into expensive housing, while creative work has become easier to publish and harder to fund. Nostalgia can polish a decade until its exclusions and dangers disappear. These photographs offer something more useful: evidence that cultural abundance rests on rent, time, institutions and the freedom to fail before anyone knows your name. The show opens on 8 October and runs until 14 February. (Guardian)
