LEAD STORY

Public systems met a weekend stress test

Britain entered Sunday with the machinery of government under close inspection. Andy Burnham's expected arrival in Downing Street brought a promise from Lucy Powell that No 10 would move away from a factional briefing culture that Labour women described as a boys' club. Whitehall was already preparing for a scramble over jobs and control. Police leadership in England and Wales faced a separate call for an ethical reset, and the NHS put artificial intelligence at the front door of care with an app tool that will steer patients toward GPs, pharmacies or A&E. (The Guardian, BBC, BBC, The Guardian)

The pressure was wider than Westminster. A week-long heat health alert was issued for England, with temperatures forecast to reach 34C in the south-east, while tens of thousands marched through London for Pride. Ukraine hit a major oil terminal in St Petersburg, the pope used a visit to Lampedusa to urge Europe to do more for migrants, and Brazil's campaign season opened on a sharper social map as Datafolha found a rightward move on taxes, welfare and punishment. America's 250th anniversary weekend began with Donald Trump using Mount Rushmore for a partisan attack and later trying again to delay payment of the E Jean Carroll civil judgment. (The Guardian, BBC, The Guardian, BBC, BBC, Folha Poder, The Guardian, The Guardian)

NEWS

Burnham's first test is the culture around power

Labour's deputy leader Lucy Powell said Burnham would change No 10's briefing culture after female MPs raised concerns about gender balance and factional attacks. The transition is already moving through Whitehall, where MPs and officials expect a fight over posts, access and the tone of the new operation. Burnham's early promise is therefore managerial before it is ideological: Downing Street has to look more disciplined, more meritocratic and less dependent on anonymous briefings. (The Guardian, BBC)

Heat, Pride and policing put public trust on the street

Most regions of England are under heat health alerts from Sunday to Saturday, with the south-east forecast to reach 34C. London's Pride parade still brought more than 35,000 people from about 600 groups through the capital, with Sadiq Khan among those taking part. The same weekend, Lord Blunkett backed a report calling for a fundamental overhaul of police leadership in England and Wales. The public square is asking for celebration, safety and competence at the same time. (The Guardian, BBC, The Guardian, BBC, BBC)

Ukraine struck Russian oil as Europe watched migration and the far right

Ukraine hit a major oil terminal in St Petersburg, a target Volodymyr Zelensky described as infrastructure that helps fund Russia's war. In Italy, Pope Leo used a visit to Lampedusa to press Europe to do more for migrants and to honour those lost at sea. In Germany, riot police clashed with protesters trying to block an AfD conference in Erfurt. The continent's weekend was a map of pressure points: war revenue, border deaths and democratic resistance to the far right. (BBC, BBC, The Guardian)

America's birthday opened in argument

The United States began its 250th anniversary weekend with Trump using a Mount Rushmore speech to attack what he called a communist menace. The celebration also reopened questions about the US-UK relationship, as Britain faces a much larger economic gap with America while keeping deep cultural ties. Later in the day, Trump's lawyers sought more time to pay the E Jean Carroll civil judgment, now topping $5.8m with interest. The anniversary became a test of memory, power and accountability. (The Guardian, The Guardian, The Guardian)

Brazil's campaign clock turned public money into the first fight

Tarcísio de Freitas published more than 120 agreements with city halls and local entities before electoral restrictions took effect. Guilherme Boulos used the same deadline for deliveries in São Paulo, while federal communications teams rushed to archive official posts before campaign limits began. Datafolha added a deeper electoral current: 65% of Brazilians now say depending less on government improves life, more voters prefer lower taxes to free public services, and support for punishing adolescent offenders as adults has reached 70%. (Folha Poder, Folha Poder, Folha Poder, Folha Poder, Folha Poder, Folha Poder)

Green power is still waiting for the cheap-power promise

Britain has approved a wave of renewable energy projects, but grid connections, planning pressure and slow delivery still stand between new capacity and cheaper household power. The political risk is simple: voters can see turbines, solar farms and policy targets, yet bills remain the proof that matters. Water companies added another version of the same credibility problem, with Severn Trent doubling a long-term reward plan for its new chief executive to as much as £3.1m despite public anger over water-sector pay. (The Guardian, The Guardian)

Tourism and football money reached the same coastline

European beach protests over overtourism put the summer economy back in conflict with local access and housing pressure. At the World Cup, Aramco's sponsorship was visible in Houston while residents near the company's Port Arthur refinery described a daily cost paid in health and air quality. Leisure, energy and sport are often sold as separate industries; this weekend they met around land, water, exposure and who gets the upside. (The Guardian, The Guardian)

TECHNOLOGY

The NHS put AI between symptoms and services

The NHS app in England will begin using artificial intelligence to direct patients toward the appropriate service, including GP appointments, pharmacies and A&E. The first phase is expected to reach about 200,000 patients before a wider rollout, with the health service saying all users should have access by April 2028. The decision places AI inside the practical question every patient asks first: where should I go now? (The Guardian, BBC)

Infrastructure became an emergency software problem

NASA launched an emergency mission to stop the Swift Observatory from re-entering Earth's atmosphere after solar storms pushed the satellite into a lower orbit. The rescue depends on Katalyst Space Technologies intercepting a spacecraft launched in 2004. In Washington, the Department of Energy deleted thousands of energy-conservation pages during a historic heatwave. One story is orbital mechanics and one is public information, but both show infrastructure now depends on the systems around the hardware. (The Verge, The Verge)

Musk's British posts became part of the SpaceX market story

Elon Musk posted about race and immigration in the UK twice as often as he posted about SpaceX in the run-up to the company's IPO. The imbalance matters because political speech, platform ownership and investor expectation are now part of the same public record. At the consumer end, Apple users faced the quieter version of platform power: higher product prices in Brazil and more iOS 27 features tying AirPods and Music into the operating system. (The Guardian, MacMagazine, MacMagazine)

CULTURE

America's anniversary returned to image and argument

The US turned 250 with its founding text, its photographs and its relationship with Britain all under review. One essay returned to the Declaration of Independence and the consent of the governed; another traced how photographs helped the country sell and mythologise itself from the gold rush to 9/11. The cultural anniversary is working as an audit: what America says, what it shows and what allies still see from across the Atlantic. (The Guardian, The Guardian, The Guardian)

Taylor Swift's wedding became a fashion and media event

Stars gathered in New York as Taylor Swift and Travis Kelce's wedding took over Madison Square Garden and the city streets around it. The ceremony gave Dior a high-profile coup, with both bride and groom wearing designs by Jonathan Anderson, the Northern Irish creative director who has quickly become one of fashion's most watched figures. The wedding turned privacy, fandom and luxury branding into the weekend's other national spectacle. (The Guardian, The Guardian, BBC)

Culture kept asking what humans still make better

Writers and linguists examined how AI is changing language, while independent cinemas in the US found renewed strength through programming that feels local, unexpected and social. The Rolling Stones' late-career momentum added another answer from the old industrial model: a band can still turn age, politics and blues repetition into release-day electricity. Publicly funded arts made the same claim in Britain, where shared storytelling remains part of civic infrastructure. (The Guardian, The Guardian, The Guardian, The Guardian)

SPORTS

The World Cup kept finding new shapes of pressure

Argentina survived Cape Verde in a last-32 match that came close to a historic shock before the champions broke through. Colombia reached the last 16 after Jhon Arias scored against Ghana, and Morocco beat Canada to reach the last eight with an unbeaten run now stretching to 34 matches. England's next test is Mexico at the Azteca, with Reece James doubtful and Jarell Quansah fit again. The tournament is moving from spectacle to attrition: altitude, injuries and recovery are now as important as talent. (The Guardian, The Guardian, The Guardian, BBC, The Guardian, BBC)

Canada's exit left a Davies question behind

Canada's defeat to Morocco reopened debate over how Alphonso Davies's injury was managed and how much the co-hosts had built around one irreplaceable player. Jesse Marsch insisted his side had controlled long spells, while Morocco's Walid Regragui had the result and the quarter-final place. Egypt also closed off a sideline incident, with Hossam Hassan accepting a police apology after a confrontation at the team hotel in Dallas. World Cup management is now about squads, hotels, recovery rooms and the public stories that follow them. (The Guardian, The Guardian, The Guardian)

LIFESTYLE

Summer life turned practical under the heat

With another heatwave moving in, the weekend's gentler work was preparation. Meera Sodha offered Beijing-style courgette pancakes with hot honey cucumbers, while a supermarket mozzarella tasting sorted the reliable from the overhyped. Tim Dowling's electric-car column supplied the domestic version of the same season: modern convenience is useful only when the machine knows where it is going. (The Guardian, The Guardian food, The Guardian Filter, The Guardian lifestyle)

Private lives kept becoming public stories

Esther Freud spoke about grief after losing both parents within four days, and a musician's account of losing sight in one eye became a story about a partner who helped him record an album hours later. A separate relationship column followed a trio navigating desire, control and domestic structure. The useful lifestyle material was intimate without being small: illness, mourning and love all became questions of who steadies a life when ordinary plans break. (The Guardian lifestyle, The Guardian lifestyle, The Guardian lifestyle)
