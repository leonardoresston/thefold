LEAD STORY

Iran shock pushed oil and politics back into crisis mode

Oil markets jumped after Iran launched attacks on tankers near the Strait of Hormuz and Donald Trump declared the ceasefire deal with Tehran over. Brent crude moved above $80 a barrel, its sharpest rise since the ceasefire began, while US stocks fell as the Dow lost about 500 points and investors weighed the risk of higher interest rates. (The Guardian, The Guardian)

The diplomatic picture stayed unstable as Trump left the Nato summit in Ankara mixing praise for the alliance with threats toward Iran and confusion over world leaders. Volodymyr Zelenskyy was offered only a vague route to licensed Patriot missile production, while Mark Rutte tried to soften Trump's comments as an internal alliance argument. Tehran remained charged after the ayatollah's funeral, with talks still the narrow path left open. (The Guardian, The Guardian, BBC, The Guardian, BBC)

NEWS

Burnham moved toward Labour leadership with rivals clearing the field

Andy Burnham promised Labour MPs that party discipline would no longer be used to suppress debate as nominations for the Labour leadership prepared to open. Al Carns, the last plausible rival left in the frame, ruled out a run and told the party to get behind Burnham, leaving the contest close to a coronation. (The Guardian, BBC)

Farage's money questions kept pace with the Clacton race

Reform entered the Clacton byelection with continuing scrutiny over financial transactions flagged to the National Crime Agency. The party leader remains likely to win the seat again, yet the campaign now runs through questions about money, declarations and the distance between anti-establishment rhetoric and formal standards rules. (The Guardian, The Guardian)

Ecosystem collapse entered the UK security file

MPs demanded publication of a full report from UK intelligence leaders warning that ecosystem collapse overseas could carry catastrophic consequences for national security. The concern is immediate enough to reach food security, supply chains and the state's ability to prepare for shocks that begin outside Britain but land inside its economy. (The Guardian)

A Bedfordshire murder case became an international hunt

Police named Ndodana Mkhanyisi Tshuma as the man wanted over the deaths of his wife and two daughters near Bedford. He is believed to have left the UK through Heathrow for Zimbabwe before Nothabo Zandile Tshuma, Natalie and Nala were found dead, turning the case into an international search. (The Guardian, BBC)

Ruth Ellis received a conditional posthumous pardon

Ruth Ellis, the last woman hanged in the UK, received a conditional posthumous pardon more than 70 years after she was executed at Holloway Prison for murdering David Blakely. The decision reopens one of the defining cases in Britain's movement away from capital punishment. (BBC)

Brazil's election fight hardened around tariffs and the Bolsonaro family

Lula held a 45% to 40% second-round lead over Flavio Bolsonaro in the Meio/Ideia survey, with the gap larger among women. Flavio's attempt to move a US tariff fight beyond the election drew fire from Ronaldo Caiado and from Bolsonaro allies, while federal police searched Jair Bolsonaro's residence and found no new weapons. (Canal Meio, Folha Poder, Folha Poder, Folha Poder, Folha Poder)

The IMF upgraded its UK growth forecast to 1% for this year, putting Britain third in the G7 on its July projection. (The Guardian, The Guardian)

The car-loan payout fight moved into court tactics

The Financial Conduct Authority sought to remove Consumer Voice from the car finance payout battle, alleging problems around transparency and conflicts of interest. The dispute sits inside a compensation fight estimated at up to 9.1bn pounds, with the watchdog and consumer advocates now contesting who gets to argue for drivers. (The Guardian)

Energy planning split between nuclear life extensions and gas warnings

Sizewell B received a 20-year life extension that should keep Britain's most recently completed nuclear power station generating until 2055. At the same time, the Jackdaw gas field's owner warned of winter fuel shortages unless North Sea production is approved, leaving ministers with pressure from both low-carbon baseload and near-term gas supply. (The Guardian, BBC)

The heatwave became an everyday operating problem

The UK's third heatwave of the year spread farther north and west and became one of the longest since 1976. Advice moved from novelty to routine: use fans where they help, keep rooms shaded, and treat heat as a workplace risk, with unions across Europe pressing for temperature limits, heat breaks and adjusted hours. (BBC, BBC, The Guardian)

Estate agents are using AI-enhanced images to repaint walls, improve lawns and make listings look closer to their imagined potential. Blue-space therapy moved closer to mainstream care

Sea, river and lake programmes are gaining ground as support for trauma, anxiety and addiction, reviving the old idea of water as medicine for the mind. The practice now sits alongside community health work, outdoor therapy and the practical search for care that feels reachable before crisis takes over. (The Guardian)

Parkrun stayed simple enough to scale

The appeal of parkrun remains its low-friction design: a free 5k that can be walked, jogged or run without asking beginners to become athletes first. The useful preparation is modest too, with pacing, shoes and a repeatable Saturday routine doing most of the work. (The Guardian)
