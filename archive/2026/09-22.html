Britain enters the Gulf’s air campaign as the diesel shock comes home

Britain will provide air-to-air refuelling for Saudi fighter jets defending energy infrastructure from Houthi attacks, the first military deployment authorised by Andy Burnham since he entered Downing Street. An RAF Voyager tanker is expected to support the operation for several weeks, adding to the British Sky Sabre air-defence system already in the region. The immediate task is to protect Saudi facilities and the export route through the Bab al-Mandab strait, which has become more important as the Strait of Hormuz remains closed to most western traffic. The decision places the UK closer to an active regional confrontation, even though ministers are describing the role as defensive. (Guardian)

The deployment has a domestic purpose written through it. Damage to Middle Eastern refineries, disruption around Hormuz and Ukrainian strikes on Russian plants have removed about a fifth of global diesel supply. Britain’s average diesel price has risen 54% since fighting with Iran began in February, reaching 196.28p a litre and heading towards a record £2. Crude oil briefly dipped below $100 a barrel on Monday, yet the shortage sits further down the chain: the world lacks enough working refineries to turn crude into diesel, gas oil and other products. The resulting gap between crude and refined-fuel prices has exceeded $100 a barrel in Europe for the first time. (Guardian)

That squeeze travels quickly through an economy. Diesel powers lorries, farms, taxis, tradespeople’s vans and much of the machinery behind everyday services. Britain has only four refineries, down from nine at the start of the century, and imported about 54% of the diesel it consumed last year. Closures at Grangemouth and Lindsey cut domestic refining capacity by almost a quarter. Independent firms have little room to absorb the rise, and every delivery adds pressure to the eventual shop price. Inflation was already 3.1% last month. A fuel shock that raises transport, food and borrowing costs together will complicate the government’s first budget before any military operation can improve supply. (Guardian)

Burnham arrives in New York for his first meeting with Donald Trump carrying that tension. He wants Washington to de-escalate the Iran conflict while asking the US alliance to secure the routes on which British prices now depend. Trump remains unhappy that the UK withheld support for his strikes on Iran, and the two leaders also differ over climate policy and defence spending. Their shared interest is the increasingly visible political cost of energy disruption. British support for Saudi jets may help keep one export corridor open. It also makes clear how a war thousands of miles away has entered British foreign policy, mortgage calculations and the weekly food bill at the same time. (Guardian, Guardian)

News & Economy

Trump wants Ukraine to stop striking Russia’s refineries

Donald Trump is expected to press Volodymyr Zelenskyy for an energy ceasefire when the two meet at the UN on Tuesday. Ukraine launched its largest reported attack on the Moscow region over the weekend, hitting a major refinery and killing three people. Russian missile and drone attacks have repeatedly targeted Ukraine’s cities, power system and economy; Kyiv has increasingly used long-range drones against the infrastructure that funds and supplies Moscow’s war. Zelenskyy has proposed reciprocal steps under which Russia would stop attacking Ukrainian energy, critical infrastructure and food exports, followed by matching restraint from Ukraine. The shape of a workable agreement therefore exists, though verification and the definition of an energy target remain unresolved. (Guardian)

Trump’s urgency is sharpened by fuel prices before November’s American midterms. US diesel reached a record $6.50 a gallon on Monday, more than twice its level a year ago, and his Sunday call with Zelenskyy concentrated heavily on Russian diesel capacity. Sergei Lavrov is also due in Washington for talks with secretary of state Marco Rubio, while Trump will meet Gulf producers and Venezuela’s leader in New York. An energy truce could offer a narrow route towards reducing attacks without pretending that a wider peace has been settled. It will endure only if Russia faces the same limits as Ukraine and both sides can see violations clearly. (Guardian)

El Niño pushes the climate into unfamiliar territory

The Pacific Ocean temperature at the centre of El Niño has reached 3.05C above the 1991–2020 average, passing the previous modern-record high months before this event is expected to peak. Fourteen forecast models point to about 4C in November and December; anything above 2C qualifies as a very strong El Niño. Researchers believe the current event may be the largest for at least 1,000 years. Its redistribution of heat is landing on a planet already warmed by fossil-fuel emissions, raising the prospect that 2027 will set a new global temperature record by a wide margin. Indonesia has seen exceptional fires and dangerous air pollution, India’s monsoon has weakened, and crop losses and low water levels are already affecting Central America and the Panama canal. (Guardian)

A separate annual assessment has found seven of the nine planetary boundaries that support stable human societies at their most severe recorded level of breach. Climate, ecosystems, land, freshwater, nutrient cycles, synthetic pollution and ocean acidification are all outside the defined safe operating space. Only the ozone layer and atmospheric aerosol loading have improved over the past decade. Sixty scientists assembled the assessment, warning that forests and oceans may be losing some of their ability to absorb carbon and buffer disruption. The two findings describe one connected risk: a powerful natural cycle is amplifying heat while the systems that would normally soften its effects are under increasing strain. Better forecasts can save lives in the next year; emissions cuts, habitat protection and pollution controls determine how dangerous the next cycle becomes. (Guardian, Guardian)

A second air-traffic failure disrupts hundreds of flights

A connectivity failure at the Prestwick control centre caused widespread delays across Scotland, Northern Ireland and northern England, the second serious UK air-traffic problem in two weeks. More than 150 flights were cancelled on Monday; Cirium counted 140 cancelled departures and 137 arrivals, with easyJet and Ryanair among the worst affected. Nats restored full capacity and said the incident was unrelated to the software defect at Swanwick earlier this month, but airlines are challenging the system’s resilience and the regulator has begun an independent review due within six months. Passengers must still be looked after by their airlines, although the Civil Aviation Authority expects the failure to count as an extraordinary circumstance, making compensation unlikely. Two separate faults can have separate causes and still reveal the same weakness: a national network with too little tolerance for a local technical failure. (BBC, Guardian)

Britain brings its first charges over the Rwandan genocide

Vincent Brown, a 65-year-old Rwandan man living in north London and formerly known as Vincent Bajinya, has become the first person charged in Britain with offences connected to the 1994 genocide. Prosecutors allege that he directed and incited killings carried out by others. He faces one count of conduct ancillary to genocide and six counts relating to murder as a crime against humanity, and is due at Westminster magistrates court on Tuesday. The Metropolitan Police opened its investigation in 2019 after a request from Rwanda concerning atrocities in Kigali’s Rugenge district. About 800,000 Tutsis and moderate Hutus were killed over 100 days. The charges follow seven years of work and must now be tested in court; they also establish that the passage of three decades and residence in the UK do not close the route to accountability for alleged international crimes. (BBC)

Brazil’s presidential race tightens around two deeply rejected candidates

Lula leads the first round of Brazil’s presidential election with 37% against Flávio Bolsonaro’s 33%, according to the latest Quaest survey. The gap sits at the edge of the poll’s two-point margin of error; in a runoff Bolsonaro has 42% and Lula 41%, a statistical tie. Eight per cent remain undecided, while 7% plan to cast a blank or invalid ballot or stay home. The survey interviewed 2,004 voters from Thursday to Sunday. Both leading candidates carry unusually high rejection scores, 56% for Bolsonaro and 55% for Lula, and the president’s administration is disapproved of by 50%. That combination leaves campaigns chasing tactical voters while struggling to broaden genuine enthusiasm in the final weeks. (Folha, Meio)

Bolsonaro’s campaign is also answering questions about R$199,000 in parliamentary funds directed in 2023 to a Rio football project. Federal police obtained messages in which one of his aides discussed the release of the money with Robson Calixto, known as Peixe, who was convicted this year of belonging to the militia involved in councillor Marielle Franco’s murder. Investigators see indications that money sent to the organisation was diverted; Bolsonaro’s office says the allocation followed legal and technical requirements and that the recipient returned unused funds. Police say he directed about R$600,000 in total to the organisation under investigation. The allegations require a documented accounting of where each payment went. With the presidential runoff effectively level, the official explanations, police evidence and electoral consequences will now move together. (Folha)

Mortgage anxiety reaches the jobs market and the budget

British household confidence fell again in September as higher energy costs, uncertain employment and the prospect of another interest-rate rise converged. The S&P Global sentiment index slipped to 42.7 from 42.9, with more than half of respondents expecting borrowing costs to rise during the next year. The average two-year fixed mortgage reached 5.88% on Monday and the average five-year deal 5.92%. For a typical £250,000 loan over 25 years, moves since March add roughly £150 to the monthly payment. Direct debits for mortgages have already climbed from about £600 to £900 over four years. (Guardian)

Employment is becoming the second source of unease. Payroll numbers have declined for six months, vacancies are at a five-year low and confidence in job security is at its weakest in three and a half years. Only 17% of businesses surveyed by the British Chambers of Commerce plan to raise investment, the lowest share since the pandemic. Chancellor John Healey enters his first budget with calls to ease fuel duty and support employers, while higher government borrowing costs and defence spending narrow his choices. Households will judge the budget through monthly bills and the likelihood of keeping a job. A tax package that protects the Treasury’s buffer while deepening either worry could suppress the spending and investment needed for recovery. (Guardian)

A $110bn Hollywood merger clears its largest legal obstacle

Paramount has settled with California and 11 other US states that sought to block its $110bn takeover of Warner Bros Discovery, clearing the main remaining barrier to one of Hollywood’s largest consolidations. The agreement requires the combined company to release at least 30 films in each of its first two years and 32 annually thereafter, spend $300m more on US production than the companies did in 2025, and include independent films and large theatrical releases in the mix. Missing the target can trigger a $30m payment for each film and the forced sale of Miramax. Paramount must also retain a free streaming service and keep its California studio lots for five years. (Verge, Guardian)

The settlement also creates a five-member board of experienced journalists to protect editorial independence at CBS and CNN, and establishes rules for negotiations involving the combined cable channels. The Writers Guild has resolved its separate case. Regulators are trying to write public-interest outcomes into a merger whose scale they could no longer stop: more films, continued production jobs, a free service and barriers around newsrooms. Those promises will matter through enforcement. Output quotas can preserve volume while ownership still concentrates bargaining power over workers, cinemas, advertisers and audiences. The new company now has clearance; the next test is whether the penalties are strong enough to keep its commitments commercially real. (Verge)

Technology, Culture & Daily Life

Digital convenience meets the right to understand and be heard

Ireland’s privacy regulator has fined Google €403m over historical use of location data, finding that people may not have understood how their movements informed advertising and inferences about their health, beliefs or interests. The investigation covered location history, location accuracy and web-and-app activity between May 2018 and February 2020. Google says its controls have changed since 2019; the Data Protection Commission has ordered full compliance with European data law within six months. It is the watchdog’s fourth-largest penalty, behind cases involving Meta, TikTok and Instagram. The ruling arrives six years after the inquiry began, a delay that shows how easily enforcement can trail the technology it governs. (Guardian)

Citizens Advice is asking essential-service providers to confront a more immediate failure by guaranteeing access to a human. At least 14 million people in the UK face some form of digital exclusion, and only 20% of adults surveyed preferred chatbots for support. Among people who struggled to reach a person, 49% experienced stress or frustration, 44% endured delays and 14% abandoned the problem. The consequences go beyond an irritating conversation: correcting an energy meter reading can take six weeks offline, and digital-only homelessness applications can leave people exposed for longer. Banks, energy suppliers and telecoms firms can use automation for simple requests, but billing errors, debt and housing crises need a clear route to someone empowered to act. Good digital service begins with informed consent and ends with a remedy. (Guardian)

The AI buildout adds £100 to the price of an iPhone

Apple has raised the UK price of every iPhone by £100, including older models, as demand from AI datacentres consumes memory chips and pushes component costs sharply higher. Memory prices are more than 300% above last year and some parts cost five times their previous listed price. Samsung and Google have added as much as £80 to flagship phones, Microsoft has raised some Surface prices by £220, and Dell’s premium laptops have climbed by up to 25%. IDC expects global smartphone shipments to contract almost 17% this year, the steepest annual fall on record, as buyers pay more and replace devices less often. (Guardian)

The shortage exposes the physical competition beneath supposedly weightless AI services. Datacentres and consumer devices depend on many of the same memory suppliers, and producers such as SK Hynix expect tight supply beyond 2030. Manufacturers are cutting memory in some models, redesigning software for lower specifications and withdrawing cheaper phones that no longer make economic sense. Refurbished prices outside Apple’s own store have remained steadier, offering a temporary escape from the increase. The larger change may last: electronics companies rarely reverse a price rise once customers have absorbed it. The cost of the AI race is beginning to appear on ordinary product labels, even for buyers who have little interest in running an AI model. (Guardian)

London gets a prototype for a national Deaf cultural home

Amanprit Arnold’s DeafCity Hub has secured a temporary central London space for a year, giving the campaign a chance to demonstrate what Britain’s first permanent Deaf cultural centre could provide. The UK has 18 million people with hearing issues and growing Deaf representation on screen, yet no dedicated national centre for Deaf arts, work and community. Arnold, an urban planner born deaf, wants a building shaped around DeafSpace principles: wide corridors where people can sign while walking, circular seating, clear sightlines, natural light, soft colours, glass and mirrors positioned to support visual communication. British Sign Language became legally recognised only in 2022, and a GCSE is not expected until 2028. (Guardian)

The temporary site, secured with Westminster council and the Oxford Street Development Corporation, will act as an incubator while the project looks for funding and a development partner. Arnold hopes it can eventually support co-working, performances and Deaf-sector charities, then become a model for hubs elsewhere. Britain has 22 Deaf schools, down from 75 in the early 1980s, making independent cultural spaces more important as routes into a shared language and identity narrow. Architecture can remove friction long before a special accommodation is requested. DeafCity Hub’s year in borrowed space will test demand; its deeper proposal is that accessibility should begin in the plan of a room, building or city. (Guardian)

Sports & Gaming

Brighton expose Arsenal while City open a gap

Arsenal enter the international break three points behind Manchester City after a bruising defeat at Brighton. Nineteen-year-old Charalampos Kostoulas repeatedly unsettled Gabriel Magalhães and the champions’ defence, completing the performance with a strike from 25 yards, his third goal in eight days. Brighton’s trust in younger players gave them the intensity Arsenal could not contain, while City’s 5-3 win over Sunderland took them to 15 points from five matches. Thomas Tuchel now has his own problem before England’s Nations League match with Spain: Cole Palmer, Marcus Rashford, Kobbie Mainoo, Reece James and Anthony Gordon have all withdrawn from the squad. Arsenal have time to reset, but the early table has acquired a familiar shape. City have the lead, and the champions must respond after being beaten in duels they usually control. (Guardian, Guardian)

Bungie reopens Destiny’s past as Rockstar draws a line around GTA

Bungie will restore campaigns, destinations and raids removed from Destiny 2 six years ago, acknowledging that the content vault damaged trust among players who had paid for those experiences. Returning material needs updated lighting, encounters, enemies and underlying systems, so the work will take time, but all players will receive access. The change follows an unofficial preservation project and arrives after Bungie ended continuing support for Destiny 2 and conceded that Marathon failed to reach enough players. The studio is promising a longer future for the Destiny universe without announcing a third main game. Restoration gives it a concrete first step: make the existing history playable before asking the audience to invest in another chapter. (Verge, IGN)

Rockstar is setting the opposite kind of boundary ahead of Grand Theft Auto VI. Its new modding policy allows community creations while banning unauthorised commercialisation, real-money or cryptocurrency exchanges, gambling and sponsored content inside modified servers. It also prohibits imported intellectual property, extremist material and unofficial ports. Rockstar has embraced role-playing communities enough to acquire the team behind FiveM and partner with the large nopixel server, but it intends to decide who can build a business on top of its world. The rules puncture plans for unofficial GTA economies in which influencers promised players real income. GTA VI will reach consoles on 19 November without a PC version at launch, so its mod scene remains some distance away. Publishing the limits early should reduce uncertainty; applying them consistently will decide whether creators see a workable framework or permission that can be withdrawn at will. (IGN)
