Israel’s election leaves coalition talks on a new footing

Israel’s election produced no clear route to a parliamentary majority in Tuesday’s exit polls, leaving Benjamin Netanyahu’s governing coalition and Gadi Eisenkot’s opposition bloc facing difficult negotiations. The projections put the two camps level, with neither reaching the 61 seats needed to control the 120-seat Knesset. Likud could still emerge as the largest individual party. These were estimates as counting continued, and the eventual distribution of seats will determine who can credibly assemble a government. (Guardian)

The biggest projected change was a sharp increase in representation for Palestinian parties, from ten seats to around 17 or 18. That gives Arab Israeli leaders potential influence over whether the opposition can replace Netanyahu. Eisenkot and his partners would need to negotiate with Ra’am, the United Arab List, and possibly the Joint List. Ra’am’s leader, Mansour Abbas, has experience of joining a governing coalition, having participated in Naftali Bennett’s administration. Tuesday’s projections suggested his party alone might lack the seats to deliver a majority to Eisenkot’s bloc. (Guardian)

Talks with the Joint List would bring harder disagreements into the open. Eisenkot has set conditions involving Israel’s identity as a Jewish and democratic state, the principles of its declaration of independence, military conscription for Palestinian Israeli citizens and condemnation of Hamas. Hadash, the largest faction within the Joint List, rejected the premise that the opposition could dictate the terms. Those differences concern the place of Arab citizens in the state and the obligations expected of them. Resolving them would require substantial political commitments from parties whose cooperation has clear parliamentary value. (Guardian)

If the results confirm the deadlock, President Isaac Herzog will have discretion over whom to invite to try to form a government. Netanyahu’s administration would remain in a caretaker role during negotiations. An opposition minority government supported through individual agreements is another possibility, though it would face an uncertain life in parliament. The result therefore leaves several stages between election night and a change of leadership. Arab parties’ gains could make that change possible, while the difficulty of reaching an agreement gives Netanyahu time to seek another route to power. (Guardian)

Flávio Bolsonaro’s departure deepens Brazil’s political uncertainty

Flávio Bolsonaro was reported to have left Brazil for the United States on Tuesday after losing the presidential election to Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva. The senator missed a morning deadline to surrender his passport to federal police. His whereabouts and route became the subject of conflicting accounts, including reports of travel through Argentina. Allies described plans to seek asylum in the US, and the US State Department indicated that he and his family would receive visas allowing entry. The details of his movements remained unsettled in Tuesday’s coverage. (Guardian)

The passport order arose from a criminal investigation into possible involvement in a money-laundering scheme connected to betting companies. An investigation does not establish guilt. Bolsonaro had denied intending to leave Brazil in a video posted on Monday, and had yet to concede his election defeat. Lawmakers from Lula’s Workers’ party asked the supreme court to order his arrest and sought an Interpol red notice. Those requests were further steps sought by political opponents, rather than evidence that an international arrest alert had already been issued. (Guardian)

His departure adds a leadership question to the right’s electoral defeat. Jair Bolsonaro is under house arrest over an attempted coup, while Eduardo Bolsonaro is already in the US. São Paulo governor Tarcísio de Freitas is among the figures positioned to gain influence as the family’s role becomes more uncertain. Lula condemned Flávio’s apparent flight during celebrations of his 81st birthday. For the re-elected president, the immediate task is to prepare another administration while legal proceedings and the defeated candidate’s response continue to shape the political atmosphere. (Guardian)

UK–EU talks reopen the question of a closer relationship

British and European officials are expected to discuss options for the future relationship before the UK–EU summit on 20 November, with freedom of movement entering the political debate. Business secretary Jonathan Reynolds indicated that officials would consider the longer-term possibilities. Downing Street stressed that the immediate work concerns agreements already being prepared within Labour’s manifesto commitments, and a government spokesperson denied that current discussions covered freedom of movement. The difference in emphasis leaves the scope and timing of any wider negotiations unclear. (BBC)

Prime minister Andy Burnham has called for an open discussion of the relationship, while maintaining that major changes would need a general-election mandate. Labour’s 2024 programme ruled out returning to free movement, the single market or a customs union during this parliament, which must end by 2029. Free movement allows EU citizens to live, work and study across member countries and is a condition of single-market membership. Any attempt to pursue that deeper relationship would therefore involve choices about migration alongside trade and economic rules. (BBC)

For the coming summit, the practical agenda includes food and drink, electricity, emissions trading and youth mobility. Those areas offer a nearer-term test of what closer cooperation can deliver. Within Labour, MPs representing Brexit-supporting constituencies have warned that reopening the broader argument could damage trust. Burnham has also left questions over a possible referendum unresolved, with Downing Street saying no decision has been made. Businesses and citizens will need to distinguish the agreements being pursued now from options that would require another electoral mandate and further negotiation. (BBC)

Economy

The Budget tests the government’s room to act

John Healey enters Wednesday’s Budget facing a difficult combination of rising energy costs, pressure on household finances and limited scope for additional public spending. Expectations on Tuesday centred on targeted help with the cost of living, particularly for vulnerable households. Banks were preparing for a possible levy or surcharge to help finance support. The chancellor has promised longer-term action on energy costs, but the detailed measures and their funding will become clear when he presents the Budget. Advance indications remain proposals until the formal announcements. (BBC)

The government also intends to move more powers and resources to local leaders, making devolution a central part of its economic programme. That raises practical questions about how local spending will be scrutinised and how responsibilities will be shared with Whitehall. Further defence investment is expected, while the full route to spending 3% of national income on defence by 2030 is due in next year’s spending review. Several large commitments therefore stretch beyond a single fiscal statement and will depend on decisions still to come. (BBC)

There are some stronger signals in growth, productivity and employment, yet the Middle East conflict has renewed pressure on energy prices and borrowing costs. Labour’s commitment to avoid increases in income tax, national insurance and VAT further limits the choices available. Healey must explain how immediate support fits within those constraints and the prime minister’s wider promises of change. The sums, eligibility rules and implementation dates will determine how much of the Budget households actually feel over the winter. (BBC)

Rosebank exposes a divide over climate accounting

A cabinet disagreement over the Rosebank oilfield is turning on how Britain accounts for emissions and weighs the benefits of new North Sea production. Energy secretary Miatta Fahnbulleh has been leaning towards rejecting the development, while chancellor John Healey favours approval. Supporters argue that the field could operate within the UK’s domestic carbon budgets because much of its oil would be exported. Emissions from burning that oil overseas would then appear in other countries’ accounts, even though the climate consequences extend across borders. (Guardian)

The distinction matters to the approval process. Ministers must consider emissions associated with using the fuel, following the legal challenge to the earlier environmental consent. Taking those emissions into account and assigning them to a domestic carbon budget are separate parts of the assessment. Climate campaigners argue that allowing another major field would undermine the transition away from fossil fuels. Greenpeace and Friends of the Earth have urged the prime minister to consider the cumulative effect of decisions on drilling, airport expansion and support for lower-carbon heating. (Guardian)

The Treasury has a financial interest too: expected tax receipts from Rosebank have already been incorporated into its calculations. The operator estimates revenues for the government could reach £500m a year by the end of this parliament. Rejecting the project would leave ministers to address the resulting gap. The energy department says the decision will follow the regulatory process and consider environmental assessments and public representations. Approval remains unresolved, with jobs, investment, energy security and climate commitments all part of the argument. (Guardian)

Technology

Apple refreshes the iMac and entry-level MacBook Pro

Apple introduced an M6 version of its entry-level 14-inch MacBook Pro on Tuesday, retaining the existing design while changing the hardware inside. The US starting price is $1,999, with 16GB of unified memory and 1TB of storage. Buyers can configure up to 32GB of memory and 4TB of storage. The display remains Liquid Retina XDR, with an optional nano-texture finish. Pre-orders open on 28 October, and deliveries are scheduled to begin on 6 November. (MacStories)

Connectivity includes Wi-Fi 7, Bluetooth 6, three Thunderbolt 4 ports, HDMI, MagSafe, an SDXC card slot and a headphone socket. Apple claims up to 24 hours of battery life, with faster charging available through its 96W adapter rather than the 70W unit supplied. The company also claims graphics and on-device AI improvements over the M5 model. Those are manufacturer figures; the size of any benefit in everyday use will depend on the software, workload and configuration. Memory bandwidth also varies between the base model and the higher-memory versions. (MacStories)

The iMac’s update brings M6 and faster connections

The iMac moves from M4 to M6, starting at $1,499 in the US, with pre-orders opening on 28 October and deliveries from 6 November. Apple is offering two chip configurations, alongside memory starting at 16GB. The higher specification can be equipped with 32GB of memory, up to 2TB of storage and optional 2.5 Gigabit Ethernet. Wi-Fi 7 and Bluetooth 6 are included, while the number of Thunderbolt 4 ports depends on the model. (MacStories)

The familiar all-in-one format remains, with a 4.5K display, a 12-megapixel Center Stage camera and a colour-matched keyboard. The updated machine can drive two external 6K displays. Apple’s performance comparisons include much older Intel and M1 models as well as the M4 generation, so the reference machine matters when reading the headline claims. Households and workplaces replacing older desktops will be looking at a different scale of improvement from owners of a recent iMac. Independent testing will help establish where the new hardware delivers its biggest gains. (MacStories)

Creator Studio adds editing tools and AI assistants

Apple’s creative and productivity software will receive a substantial update in November, bringing MotionVFX tools into Final Cut Pro and Motion after Apple’s acquisition of the plug-in maker in March. Final Cut Pro will gain templates, graphics, titles, transitions and tools for tasks including tracking and colour grading. Closer integration with Pixelmator Pro will allow images in a video timeline to be edited without a separate export and import cycle. Caption transcription will expand to another 23 languages. (MacStories)

Pixelmator Pro will gain generative fill, while Logic Pro will receive an updated interface, expanded stem separation and a beta assistant. Keynote, Pages and Numbers will also add beta assistants using Apple and OpenAI models. On the Mac, support for the Model Context Protocol will let external AI agents connect to those productivity apps. A JSON-based interchange format for Keynote is intended to make presentations more accessible to automated workflows. Existing Creator Studio subscribers will receive the additions as they roll out, with a further Freeform and Pixelmator integration planned for December. (MacStories)

Fake responses swamped the disposable-vape consultation

More than three-quarters of the responses to the government’s 2023 tobacco and vapes consultation were identified as automated, according to research published on Tuesday. Health officials classified 90,835 of 118,756 submissions as bot-generated and excluded them from decision-making. Almost all of those automated entries opposed the proposed ban on disposable vapes. Many were duplicated and arrived rapidly from a small number of internet addresses, providing signals that helped officials detect the interference. (Guardian)

The study, by health-policy researchers at Edinburgh and Bath and published in Tobacco Control, describes an attempt to distort a formal public-health consultation at considerable scale. The ban subsequently became law. The findings do not establish who organised the campaign, and concerns about commercial influence should be kept separate from proof of responsibility. The health department says its controls worked in this case. Researchers warn that improving systems may make future campaigns harder to identify. For public bodies, the challenge is to preserve participation by real people while preventing repeated machine-generated submissions from creating a false impression of public opinion. (Guardian)

Digital landlines face a resilience test

MPs have warned that the move to internet-based landlines could leave people in remote communities unable to call for help during power cuts. Operators aim to complete the change by the end of January. Traditional analogue phones can continue to operate using power from the telephone network; digital services depend on equipment such as a home router, which needs its own electricity supply. Where mobile coverage is weak, losing that connection can remove the remaining way to raise an alarm. (BBC)

Representatives from the Western Isles, the West Country and the Lake District questioned whether the backup arrangements are sufficient for prolonged storms. Battery units supplied to vulnerable customers can keep equipment working for an hour or more, but outages can last considerably longer, and mobile masts may fail too. Security minister Dan Jarvis promised to examine the concerns and raise them with the relevant department. The government says the ageing analogue network is becoming less reliable and that vulnerable customers, including telecare users, must receive free backup batteries. The practical issue is whether each household’s arrangements last through the emergencies it is likely to encounter. (BBC)

A possible hinge fault comes into focus on the iPhone Duo

A teardown assessment has identified a possible explanation for broken inner screens on some newly released iPhone Duo handsets. iFixit’s lead teardown technician, Shahram Mokhtari, points to a place where the flexible display may press against part of the hinge. The central support structure needs to move as the phone closes, allowing the screen room to bend. If it fails to drop sufficiently, contact could concentrate stress at one point. Images of damaged devices have shown a dark spot and lines near that area. (The Verge)

Apple acknowledged a problem on Monday and is replacing affected units. It describes the number as small, but has not disclosed enough data to establish a failure rate. The proposed mechanism remains an expert assessment rather than a confirmed explanation for every damaged phone. Foldable screens and hinges contain delicate components, and individual failures do not by themselves establish how widespread a design problem is. Replacement experience and evidence from more devices will be needed to determine the scale of the issue. Apple has not announced a recall. (The Verge)

Sports

Fleetwood knock Arsenal out on penalties

Arsenal were eliminated from the Carabao Cup by League Two Fleetwood Town after a 1–1 draw and a penalty shootout at Highbury. Aaron Loupalo-Bi gave the hosts the lead, holding off Marli Salmon before finishing past Illan Meslier. Viktor Gyökeres equalised after the interval, but Arsenal could not turn their greater control into a winner. Fleetwood goalkeeper Jay Lynch saved Bruno Guimarães’ penalty, and Conor Haughey converted the decisive kick to send the hosts into the quarter-finals. (Guardian)

The evening brought interruptions and disciplinary trouble as well as an upset. A drone above the pitch caused an early delay, and Max Dowman was sent off after reacting to Harrison Neal. Martin Ødegaard’s introduction improved Arsenal’s play after a poor first half, while Lynch made important late saves from Ben White and Gyökeres to preserve the draw. For Fleetwood manager Matt Lawlor, who was not managing a year ago, the result is a landmark. For Arsenal, a rotated side’s difficult night ended with one route to a trophy closed. (Guardian)

Carrick stays for United’s trip to Chelsea

Manchester United have decided Michael Carrick will remain in charge for Saturday’s visit to Chelsea after defeats at Como and at home to Bournemouth intensified scrutiny of his position. United are 16th in the Premier League, with a Champions League match against Roma and a league game against Aston Villa to follow before the international break. The club has set no public deadline for a turnaround. (BBC)

The problems extend across the squad and its relationship with the coaching staff. Bruno Fernandes publicly questioned the tactics after the European defeat and was subsequently on the bench against Bournemouth. Senior figures believe the players are performing below their capabilities, while recruitment and the work of the football leadership have also come under examination. Keeping Carrick gives him another opportunity to change results, but the immediate schedule leaves little breathing room. Stamford Bridge will provide the first test of whether the same group can begin to recover. (BBC)
