News

Milburn youth review sparks union clash over tax cuts and minimum wage as UK-EU mobility scheme faces delay until 2030

Alan Milburn’s 278-page review into the nearly one million 16-to-24-year-olds outside education, employment, or training (NEET) has opened a rift between Downing Street, employers, and trade unions over workers’ rights and youth pay. Calling the report a “watershed moment” with Britain’s NEET rate at 13.5 per cent—the highest in Europe outside Romania—Prime Minister Andy Burnham backed its core plan to shift billions of pounds from incapacity benefits into a Youth Opportunity Service offering training, health support, and placements in return for participation. To encourage hiring, however, the former Labour health secretary urged ministers to double the salary threshold at which employers start paying National Insurance for entry-level under-25 jobs in retail and hospitality, apply a “cautionary principle” to banning zero-hours contracts, and slow rises in youth minimum wage rates. Pointing out that Britain spends 0.1 per cent of GDP on hiring subsidies against 1 per cent in Denmark, Milburn said the package could cut the NEET rate to 6.8 per cent by 2036 and help 440,000 young people into work. TUC general secretary Paul Nowak urged ministers to deliver guaranteed-hours rules without treating young staff as cheap labour, while disability advocates warned that replacing adult health benefits for under-25s creates a two-tier welfare state based on age rather than need. (Guardian, Guardian, BBC, BBC, BBC, Guardian)

Ahead of Burnham’s Brussels summit on Friday with Ursula von der Leyen and António Costa, a draft joint statement circulated to EU ambassadors shows that the new UK-EU youth mobility scheme may wait until 2030 to take full effect. While negotiators expect an agreement in principle this week alongside pacts on food standards and carbon trading, the draft commits both sides to applying the visa deal “no later than 2028” and making places available “no later than January 2030”. Disagreements over whether EU students at UK universities pay home fees or a reduced international rate have been postponed for six months to align with Brussels’s pending Made in Europe industrial bill. After Boris Johnson accused him of wasting his premiership on Brexit, Burnham told Sky News he will set out long-term options for EU ties next month alongside his ten-year economic plan without yet backing a single model, while the CBI urged leaders to prioritise easing post-Brexit work permits for cross-Channel business trips. (Guardian, BBC, BBC, Guardian)

At home, Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood tells the police chiefs’ conference in London today that she is launching a fast-track scheme to train 200 potential superintendents a year after a report condemned force leadership as “frankly inadequate”; between 2023 and 2025, zero forces were rated outstanding for leadership and eight serving or former chief constables faced misconduct probes. Mahmood will also unveil a national impartiality standard instructing officers to leave politics to politicians and avoid “misguided attempts at course correction” that fuel claims of two-tier policing. Meanwhile, British counter-terrorism police confirmed that 41-year-old US Army Lieutenant Colonel Brandon Carroll Tyner—arrested on 10 November at Imjin Barracks in Gloucestershire on suspicion of spying for Russia—has been transferred into US military custody following a joint operation with MI5 and the FBI. (Guardian, BBC, Guardian, BBC)

In education and climate policy, Ofqual chief regulator Sir Ian Bauckham warned GCSE and A-level students that using AI in coursework or bringing smart glasses into exams risks “life-altering” disqualification after device cheating hit 2,225 cases in 2025, echoing South Korea’s move to ban smart glasses and check sleeves for 500,000 candidates sitting Thursday’s eight-hour suneung entrance exam. Separately, the Wellcome Trust commissioned ten research teams to track 300,000 British teenagers ahead of the spring 2027 under-16 social media ban. On net zero, the Climate Change Committee warned that weakening the electric vehicle sales mandate would add 8.4 million tonnes of annual carbon emissions next decade and breach legally binding targets, even as Energy Secretary Miatta Fahnbulleh joined Spain and Kenya at COP31 in backing international levies on frequent flyers and cryptocurrency transactions. (Guardian, Guardian, BBC, Guardian, Guardian)

Ukraine collapses Kerch Bridge span in seven-stage strike as Xi opens Shenzhen APEC summit and Peltola wins Alaska

A double-arched span of the 12-mile Kerch Bridge linking Russia to occupied Crimea now lies on the Black Sea floor after Ukrainian military and Security Service (SBU) units executed a seven-stage operation that overwhelmed Russian defences. After weeks spent degrading radar and air-defence batteries across Crimea, Ukrainian units—including Robert Brovdi’s “Magyar’s Birds” brigade—launched hundreds of long-range attack drones on Monday night. Because mysterious 100mph speed limits were imposed ten days ago on Ukrainian drones using SpaceX’s Pentagon-overseen Starshield network, Kyiv sent a second wave of drones to act as an airborne radio and video relay, guiding at least four Ukrainian-made Flamingo cruise missiles with 1,000kg warheads into the weakened road arch early on Tuesday. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov insisted the crossing Vladimir Putin opened in 2018 would be rebuilt “even better than before”. (Guardian, BBC)

Putin and Chinese President Xi Jinping addressed world leaders in Shenzhen today as the APEC summit opened ahead of bilateral meetings with Donald Trump and a three-way US-China-Russia session. Calling for lower trade barriers and deeper artificial intelligence cooperation, Xi projected confidence from China’s hardware capital even as Beijing worries the Nvidia spy scandal will harden US tech curbs. Following intelligence claims that former Megaspeed executive Alice Huang had links to China’s Ministry of State Security during her affair with Jensen Huang, Washington this week restricted Chinese firms from renting US AI chips in third countries such as Malaysia, Thailand, and Brazil. Elsewhere in Asia, Trump’s trade pressure has pushed Tokyo close to lifting its 76-year ban on fresh US table potatoes, alarming Japanese farmers in a country that imports over 60 per cent of its food. (Guardian, BBC, Guardian)

In the United States, Democrat Mary Peltola unseated Republican Senator Dan Sullivan in Alaska on Tuesday—becoming the state’s first Democratic senator since 2008 despite ballot confusion over a second candidate named Dan Sullivan—to complete a nine-seat midterm swing that gives Democrats a 56-seat Senate majority. In federal court, Pakistani national Asif Merchant received a 27-and-a-half-year sentence in Brooklyn for an Iran-linked plot to assassinate Trump in retaliation for Qassem Soleimani’s killing; Manhattan prosecutors charged Irish activist Daithi O’Corrain with using an axe and ball bearings to destroy a $60 million (£45 million) US Air National Guard C-130 Hercules at Shannon Airport; and a judge extended Trump’s deposition deadline in his $10 billion BBC defamation lawsuit to 22 January. Meanwhile, Israel’s Knesset voted 66–52 to elect Matan Kahana—an ally of prime minister-designate Gadi Eisenkot—as speaker to begin unpicking Benjamin Netanyahu’s laws, and lawmakers from Serbia’s anti-corruption Student List danced a traditional kolo outside Belgrade’s parliament before taking their seats after defeating Aleksandar Vučić’s ruling party. (Guardian, Guardian, Guardian, BBC, Guardian, Guardian, Guardian)

Market sell-off and left-wing mutiny hit Lula’s fiscal package as Congress presses Moraes to cut Bolsonaro’s sentence

President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva returned to Brasília to face simultaneous rebellions from financial markets and his left-wing base over Planning Minister Bruno Moretti’s R$56 billion fiscal package. Doubts over spending discipline sent the US dollar up 2.15 per cent to R$5.77—making the real the worst performer among 33 currencies—and pushed the Ibovespa down 1.83 per cent as foreign outflows since the runoff hit R$17.5 billion, Morgan Stanley forecast a 7 per cent index drop by 2027, and the Focus survey lifted 2027 inflation expectations to 5 per cent. On the left, activists flooded Congressman André Janones’s pro-Lula WhatsApp channel with scissors emojis—turning the PT’s campaign symbol against Flávio Bolsonaro back onto the government and costing the group 300,000 followers—while PT deputy Lindbergh Farias accused Moretti of authoritarianism and the National Union of Students staged a Rio protest backed by PSOL without the major union confederations. As Moretti met PT deputies to defend an R$18.3 billion cap on corporate tax-credit offsets and Senator Randolfe Rodrigues tabled a Big Tech tax, Lula met PT chief Edinho Silva, Camilo Santana—tipped for Chief of Staff—and Rui Costa to plan his fourth-term cabinet, with Geraldo Alckmin ally Márcio Elias Rosa and Defence secretary-general Cinara Fredo leading contenders to succeed José Múcio at Defence. (Meio, Meio, Folha, Folha, Folha)

At the Supreme Federal Court (STF), Senate President Davi Alcolumbre and Chamber Speaker Hugo Motta met Justice Alexandre de Moraes on Tuesday to urge a plenary vote this year on the congressional “Dosimetry Law” reducing sentences for Jair Bolsonaro and others convicted over the 8 January 2023 coup plot. Moraes indicated he will release the action by the end of the week, and STF President Edson Fachin plans to schedule it promptly—a step supported by fellow justices to defuse opposition amnesty demands, enable Justice Kassio Nunes Marques to rule on Bolsonaro’s release request, and weaken Liberal Party (PL) plans to pursue Moraes’s impeachment next year over his links to former Banco Master owner Daniel Vorcaro. Meanwhile, the 121-strong PL caucus split over Motta’s re-election: while party chief Valdemar Costa Neto feared losing committee chairs that controlled R$8.08 billion in 2026 budget amendments, ideological deputies led by Sóstenes Cavalcante pushed to run Minas Gerais deputy Nikolas Ferreira—history’s most-voted lawmaker with 3.1 million votes—against Motta. (Folha, Folha, Folha)

Final Superior Electoral Court filings showed Lula’s campaign raised R$138.2 million and spent the full R$133.4 million limit—16 per cent above Flávio Bolsonaro’s R$114.6 million—putting the cost per vote at R$2.07 for Lula and R$1.91 for Flávio, with marketing agency Delta3 (R$41 million) and Meta ads (R$17.6 million) leading Lula’s outlays. Separately, the Federal Police concluded its five-year inquiry into R$48.7 million of undelivered Covid-19 ventilators bought by the Northeast Consortium, indicting 17 people including Bruno Dauster and Carlos Eduardo Gabas while clearing former Bahia governor Rui Costa and his wife Aline Peixoto. Elsewhere, Fachin and Moraes inaugurated a sculpture of the double-headed Oxê axe of Yoruba deity Xangô in the STF’s Noble Hall ahead of Black Consciousness Day, and around 20 emaciated members of the isolated Indigenous Awá people emerged from the Amazon in Maranhão seeking food from Guajajara villagers amid illegal logging incursions. (Folha, Folha, Folha, Guardian)

Nvidia doubles revenue while ruling out Huang’s return as California bans Waymo from freeways and Rubio denies cyber plot

Nvidia reported third-quarter revenue of $115.84 billion on Tuesday—doubling sales year-on-year and beating forecasts of $109.15 billion with earnings of $2.68 per share—as board chair Harvey C. Jones ruled out reinstating co-founder Jensen Huang. Hours earlier, Huang issued his first statement since resigning over relationships with direct report Kimberly Powell and former Megaspeed executive Alice Huang, telling the Wall Street Journal he would be “honored” to return if asked. Opening the earnings call alongside interim chief executive Colette Kress, Jones said the board had unanimously accepted Huang’s resignation, had no intention of bringing him back, and would take no questions ahead of Huang’s 1 December congressional testimony, sending Nvidia shares up 5 per cent after hours. (Guardian, The Verge, 9to5Mac, BBC)

In artificial intelligence, OpenAI launched GPT-6.2 Astra and GPT-6.2 Sol for ChatGPT Work and Codex, cutting token use while allowing Astra to replicate slide layouts and typography from screenshots and bringing Sol within half a point of Astra on the FrontierCode 2.0 benchmark at one-fifth the cost. At Microsoft Ignite, chief executive Satya Nadella declared himself “AGI-pilled” on the economic upheaval ahead while backing AI chief Mustafa Suleyman’s warning against Anthropic’s practice of treating models like Claude as potentially conscious beings with moral rights, and Proton released its privacy-focused Lumo Desktop agent. (9to5Mac, MacMagazine, The Verge, MacMagazine)

US Secretary of State Marco Rubio and intelligence chief Jay Clayton sought to calm public fears today after a week of tech glitches grounded US flights and disrupted JPMorgan Chase, the National Security Agency, Microsoft Outlook, and Apple’s iCloud, App Store, and Apple Pay. Insisting there was “zero evidence” of a systemic attack, Rubio attributed the failures to routine system patching, while security researcher Scott Helme revealed that hackers compromised web platform Optimizely to inject the Coruna crypto-stealing exploit into 178 major sites—including the BBC, CNN, HSBC, IKEA, and Zoom—targeting unpatched iPhones. On US roads, Alphabet’s Waymo suspended driverless freeway rides nationwide after California’s DMV banned its robotaxis from state highways as an “imminent hazard” following incursions into a Route 101 construction zone and an attempt to overtake highway patrol cars. (BBC, 9to5Mac, 9to5Mac, Guardian, BBC)

For Apple, repair site iFixit criticised the new A18-powered HomeView smart hub after heavy display glue and a chassis-bonded heat shield caused technicians to snap a speaker during teardown, even as Apple detailed the hub’s privacy-isolating Secure Exclaves, added iPhone Duo parts to its Self Service Repair Store, and launched Final Cut Pro 13 with MotionVFX effects. In court, Apple faces a French prosecution brought by campaign group HOP over parts-pairing repair barriers; Epic Games sought daily fines over 122,000 withheld App Store commission documents; and a US judge ruled the government violated the First Amendment by pressuring Apple to remove the ICE-monitoring Eyes Up app. Meanwhile, Apple excluded UK, EU, Brazilian, and South Korean users from iCloud+ Siri AI usage boosts in 2027, and supplier SK Hynix warned that AI memory shortages will keep prices high until 2030. In European business, Modella Capital hired Teneo to auction former WH Smith chain TG Jones—which employs 3,500 people after 150 store closures—and a leak of 15 million files alleged Deutsche Bank ignored compliance warnings over Russian oligarchs and risky clients. (The Verge, MacMagazine, 9to5Mac, MacStories, MacMagazine, 9to5Mac, MacMagazine, MacMagazine, 9to5Mac, Guardian, Guardian)

Bezos reveals hand in casting Pattinson as Bond while UK music hits £8.4bn and GTA VI sweeps Game Awards nods

Amazon founder Jeff Bezos revealed on Tuesday that he was “heavily involved” in picking Robert Pattinson as the seventh James Bond after paying over $1 billion last year for creative control of the franchise. Speaking at Semafor’s Silicon Valley summit, Bezos admitted he and director Denis Villeneuve were initially biased against the 40-year-old British star because they wanted an unknown actor, until filming three screen-test scenes with each of the five finalists showed Pattinson had “something special”. Meanwhile, critics split sharply over the sixth Hunger Games film, prequel Sunrise on the Reaping—starring Joseph Zada as a teenage Haymitch Abernathy alongside Ralph Fiennes and Kieran Culkin—between praise for its dark commentary and dismissal as an exhausted cash grab. (Guardian, BBC, The Verge, BBC)

In Britain, UK Music’s annual report showed the music industry contributed a record £8.4 billion to the economy in 2025—up 5.7 per cent—as stadium tours by Oasis, Robbie Williams, and Dua Lipa and albums by Olivia Dean lifted exports to £4.8 billion and employment to 220,500, despite warnings over grassroots costs and AI. Addressing similar threats on screen, actors’ union Equity agreed a three-year deal with producers’ body Pact giving 8,000 British performers consent and payment rights over AI “digital replicas”, banning blanket digital-double clauses and strike replacements. Elsewhere, the University of Birmingham paused plans to move the 75-year-old Shakespeare Institute and its 60,000-volume library from Stratford-upon-Avon to Edgbaston after protests led by Dame Judi Dench and the Royal Shakespeare Company. (Guardian, Guardian, Guardian)

On the eve of Thursday’s launch of Grand Theft Auto VI, Rockstar’s crime epic led The Game Awards nominations with eight nods, competing for Game of the Year before reviews have even landed against 007 First Light, Control Resonant, Phantom Blade Zero, Resident Evil Requiem, and The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time remake, while Nintendo topped publishers with 12 nominations. The Best Performance list also ended months of secrecy by confirming actress Manni L. Perez as the star playing co-protagonist Lucia Caminos, though Jason Duval’s actor went unnominated. As UK schools offered gift cards to prevent truancy, the Miami Heat unveiled a Vice City NBA court, and fans staged farewell drives through GTA V’s Los Santos, 2K studio Firaxis laid off a third of its staff—around 60 workers—following mixed reviews for Civilization VII, and esports casters protested after Embark Studios replaced human commentators with AI voices in a highlight reel for The Finals. (IGN, IGN, IGN, IGN, IGN, IGN, IGN)

Declan Rice agrees £300,000-a-week Arsenal deal to 2031 as Gunners mourn Peter Storey and Rooney opens up on mental health

Declan Rice has agreed a new five-year Arsenal contract until 2031 worth around £300,000 a week, matching Bukayo Saka as the joint highest-paid player at the Premier League champions. Signed from West Ham for a club-record £105 million in July 2023 on a £250,000-a-week deal, the 27-year-old midfielder was a driving force in ending Arsenal’s 22-year title wait in May despite second-half back and hamstring injuries, and returned to peak form to win his 82nd England cap against Spain on Sunday. Mikel Arteta’s side also agreed a contract extension with Dutch defender Jurriën Timber following the groin injury that kept him out of the summer’s World Cup, while 16-year-old Arsenal prodigy Max Dowman—already the Premier League’s youngest player, goalscorer, and title-medal winner—eclipsed Jude Bellingham’s record as England Under-21s’ youngest goalscorer by netting an 89th-minute winner in a 3–2 victory over France at Ashton Gate aged 16 years and 320 days. The celebrations in north London were tempered by the death in hospital aged 81 of Peter Storey, the combative defensive midfielder and full-back who made 501 Arsenal appearances between 1965 and 1977—eighth on the club’s all-time list—won 19 England caps, and scored the stoppage-time FA Cup semi-final penalty against Stoke that kept Bertie Mee’s 1970–71 Double triumph alive. (Guardian, Guardian, Guardian)

Elsewhere in English and international football, former Manchester United and England record scorer Wayne Rooney revealed on Gabby Logan’s Life At The Midpoint podcast that he contemplated suicide at the height of his Old Trafford playing career, describing how he hid away on two-day home drinking binges to shield his young son Kai from his depression while secretly consulting a therapist. At Old Trafford today, 16-year-old academy forward JJ Gabriel—winner of the club’s Jimmy Murphy Young Player of the Year award and son of former Republic of Ireland international Joe O’Cearuill—posted an Instagram farewell video ahead of an expected move to Real Madrid, prompting United to explore legal action over potential regulatory breaches after he cancelled his registration before turning 16 in October. Meanwhile, Professional Footballers’ Association chief executive Maheta Molango warned that football’s future is endangered by executives prioritising commercial revenue over player welfare even after the expanded 2026 World Cup generated $15 billion, as French Football Federation president Philippe Diallo officially launched his campaign in Paris with 55 European votes—backed initially by English FA chair Debbie Hewitt—and sought an alliance with Japan’s Kozo Tashima toward the 106 votes needed to topple Gianni Infantino in March’s FIFA election, while five environmental groups petitioned the United Nations to review FIFA’s climate status over its Saudi Aramco sponsorship and tournament expansion. (Guardian, BBC, Guardian, Guardian, Guardian, Guardian)
