LEAD STORY

Public life faced a test of who pays and who decides

Britain entered Monday with political money, artificial intelligence and public safety all demanding clearer rules. Nigel Farage faced questions over undeclared staff, security and accommodation provided by George Cottrell, a crypto entrepreneur previously convicted of fraud, while Reform UK denied registration rules had been broken. Yvette Cooper put AI on the foreign policy clock, warning that governments need global rules for systems that could amplify climate, migration and interference risks. Schools in England are also due an allergy-training overhaul under Benedict's Law, named after Benedict Blythe, who died after accidental allergen exposure at school in 2021. (The Guardian, BBC, BBC, The Guardian, BBC)

The same pressure ran through the wider day. EasyJet moved toward a £5.5bn takeover by Castlelake that would take Britain's largest low-cost carrier private. Brazil's São Paulo race hardened around Tarcísio de Freitas, who led Fernando Haddad by 46% to 30% in Datafolha's first-round measure. The World Cup produced both a governance storm, after Fifa lifted Folarin Balogun's suspension before the United States faced Belgium, and a sporting shock, after Erling Haaland's late double sent Brazil out against Norway. (The Guardian, Folha Poder, The Guardian, The Guardian)

Farage's benefits row put Reform's anti-politics pitch under strain

Robert Jenrick acknowledged that Farage accepted support from George Cottrell before becoming an MP, including staff, security and accommodation. Reform UK's defence is that the help counted as personal gifts and fell outside the declaration regime. The controversy still lands on the central weakness of insurgent politics: a party built against Westminster habits now has to explain private help, wealthy allies and the boundary between personal backing and political advantage. Yvette Cooper urged the United States, China and other governments to agree global rules for artificial intelligence, placing AI alongside climate, irregular migration and foreign interference as a near-term diplomatic threat. The warning gives the next phase of regulation a security frame. Britain is trying to shape the rules before the largest systems become too embedded in public administration, warfare, markets and elections to govern cleanly. (The Guardian)

Gene editing's next argument moved beyond lab safety

New embryo research and polling pushed human gene editing back into public ethics. Germline editing remains legally prohibited worldwide, including in the UK, while base-editing studies on human embryos are allowed under tight research limits when embryos are destroyed within 14 days. The debate is turning on what society wants from the technology as well as how scientists make it safer. (The Guardian)
