LEAD STORY

Burnham put mayors inside the tax base

Andy Burnham set out a devolution package that would give England’s regional mayors a share of income tax and more room to borrow for major projects. The plan moves transport, housing and skills investment away from annual Whitehall bids and puts local leaders closer to the revenue raised in their own regions. (Guardian, BBC)

The prime minister also kept North Sea oil in the frame after speaking to Donald Trump, saying Britain could be pragmatic about domestic resources while households are under pressure. The combined message is a first-week wager on regional power, energy security and visible delivery. (BBC, Guardian)

NEWS

Suffolk declared a major incident over coastal fire

Hundreds of people were evacuated after a wildfire spread across more than 80 hectares on the Suffolk coast near Sizewell B. The nuclear power station monitored the blaze, and the RSPB reserve at Dunwich Heath was badly hit as crews worked through dry, windy conditions. (Guardian, BBC)

Channel crossings turned deadly again

Four people died in two incidents after 752 people crossed the Channel on Wednesday, the busiest day for small boats this year. A charity said French riot police funded by the UK used teargas as people tried to board a boat in which three women were later found dead. (BBC, Guardian)

Palestine Action’s ban reached the Supreme Court

Huda Ammori, a co-founder of Palestine Action, won permission to challenge the group’s proscription at the UK Supreme Court. The case now puts the government’s use of anti-terror law against the direct-action group before the country’s highest court. (Guardian, BBC)

European heat hit power, tourism and borders

Wildfires forced evacuations in Crete, and record-low Danube levels shut Hungary’s only nuclear plant as prolonged heat and low rainfall strained south-eastern Europe. Spain sent extra troops and police to Ceuta after thousands of people entered the enclave from Morocco by swimming and wading across. (BBC, BBC, Guardian)

Brazil opened fresh election and influence cases

André Mendonça authorised a Federal Police inquiry into Fábio Luís Lula da Silva, known as Lulinha, over suspected influence peddling. The PT also went to the TSE against Flávio Bolsonaro over the amplification of anonymous AI posts attacking Lula, while Flávio’s campaign changed its communications command. (Folha, Folha, Folha)

Labour readmitted Diane Abbott

Diane Abbott was readmitted to the Labour party after a suspension, apologising for past comments and receiving a formal warning. The decision restores one of the party’s longest-serving MPs during the opening days of Burnham’s government. (BBC)

A Russian missile destroyed a US-owned drone factory in Kyiv

A ballistic missile destroyed Terminal Autonomy’s Kyiv drone factory, in what appears to be Moscow’s first strike on a US company during the war. The attack moved Ukraine’s defence-production network deeper into the diplomatic fight around western military support. (Guardian)

The Bank of England held at 3.75%

The Bank of England kept interest rates unchanged at 3.75% in a split vote, with oil near $90 a barrel and the Iran war keeping inflation risk alive. The decision delays a cut that had looked more likely before the latest energy shock. (Guardian, Guardian)

Shell’s profits jumped with oil and gas

Shell posted quarterly profit of almost $10bn, more than double the previous period, after higher oil and gas prices lifted earnings. The result renewed calls for a windfall tax while households face another inflation squeeze from energy markets. (Guardian)

Apple and Amazon beat revenue forecasts

Apple and Amazon both topped Wall Street revenue expectations while investors kept scrutinising the cost of AI spending. Apple posted quarterly revenue of $109.4bn; iPhone revenue reached $54.3bn and services revenue reached $30.7bn. (Guardian, MacStories, MacMagazine)

Rolls-Royce kept rising in London’s market debate

Rolls-Royce’s improving outlook kept alive the possibility that the engine-maker could challenge for the top of the London market. The company’s exposure to civil aerospace, defence and energy has turned its recovery into one of the City’s most watched industrial stories. (Guardian)

TECHNOLOGY

Epic’s iPhone store reached Brazil

The Epic Games Store became available on iPhone in Brazil after the country’s iOS opening allowed alternative app marketplaces. The change gives Brazilian users a route around Apple’s App Store for Epic’s mobile catalogue. (MacMagazine)

US senators pressed Apple over Chinese memory suppliers

American senators from both parties put pressure on Apple over potential partnerships with Chinese memory manufacturers. The scrutiny makes component sourcing part of the broader US technology-security fight with China. (MacMagazine)

Spotify added notes and a running mode

Spotify began adding user annotations to songs and rolled out an AI-driven running mode in its iOS app. The updates push the service further into personal context, turning listening habits into a more interactive product surface. (MacMagazine, MacMagazine)

Qualcomm saw Apple moving faster to its own modems

Qualcomm indicated that Apple has accelerated its migration toward in-house iPhone modems, with lower Apple-related revenue expected in coming quarters. The shift keeps one of the smartphone industry’s biggest supplier relationships under pressure. (MacMagazine)

Spam car-finance texts drew UK raids

Officials seized laptops, phones and documents in raids on claims firms accused of sending nuisance messages about car-finance mis-selling payouts. The crackdown puts a consumer-data edge on one of Britain’s largest compensation disputes. (Guardian)

John C. Dvorak died

John C. Dvorak, the technology columnist known for sharp predictions and provocation around the personal-computing industry, died. His long career helped turn hardware, software and platform arguments into mass-market tech commentary. (Meio)

CULTURE

Gunther von Hagens died at 81

Gunther von Hagens, the German anatomist who created plastination and turned preserved cadavers into international exhibitions, died after living with Parkinson’s disease for nearly two decades. His work made anatomy a public spectacle and a recurring ethical argument. (Guardian)

McCartney entered the Mercury race for the first time

Paul McCartney received his first Mercury prize nomination, joining a shortlist that also includes Olivia Dean, Dave, Raye, Suede, Knats and Nia Archives. The lineup puts legacy pop, rap, soul and newer jazz-adjacent acts into one of Britain’s most visible album contests. (Guardian)

The National Theatre bent Sophocles toward Bergman

A new National Theatre production starring Cate Blanchett and Nina Hoss brings Sophocles together with Ingmar Bergman, pairing ancient tragedy with modern psychological cinema. The staging turns the classical season into one of London’s more ambitious theatrical experiments. (Guardian)

Netflix faced a $105m stolen-film lawsuit

The makers of Fortitude, an unreleased Nicolas Cage war thriller, sued Netflix for $105m after a hard drive containing the film was stolen from the streamer’s Los Angeles office. The case puts unreleased studio assets and platform security into a Hollywood damages fight. (Guardian)

Children of Blood and Bone trouble surfaced before release

Tomi Adeyemi, whose books are being adapted into a film starring Viola Davis and Idris Elba, described the production process as deeply distressing and appeared to signal a dispute around the project. The conflict brings rare author-side turbulence into a major fantasy franchise before it reaches cinemas. (Guardian)

SPORTS

Uefa voted for a World Cup boycott threat

Uefa’s 55 associations agreed to boycott Fifa competitions if Gianni Infantino proceeds with plans to sell stakes in the World Cup’s commercial rights. The first threatened action would begin with the Under-20 Women’s World Cup, escalating the fight over football’s most valuable tournament. (Guardian, BBC)

Fifa’s sales pitch promised more tournaments

Fifa’s JP Morgan-backed deck for the World Cup sell-off promised financial growth through more global tournaments and new debt, while leaving the women’s game out of the central pitch. Concacaf rejected the funding plan, widening pressure on Infantino. (Guardian, BBC)

The Premier League backed a £1.5bn EFL package

Premier League clubs agreed a proposal to share an extra £1.5bn with the English Football League and create a lifeboat fund for clubs in crisis. The vote moves the long-running redistribution fight into the next stage of football governance. (Guardian)

Newcastle reset after Howe’s exit

Newcastle moved into a difficult summer reset after Eddie Howe’s departure, with spending rules and squad churn now central to the club’s next step. Alan Shearer warned of challenging times for the club under its Saudi owners. (BBC, BBC, Guardian)

LIFESTYLE

Houseflies kept invading UK homes

Neighbourhoods from Leicestershire to Carmarthenshire dealt with repeated waves of houseflies, with hot weather, waste conditions and local habitats shaping where the problem landed. For affected households, nets and zappers became part of the summer routine. (Guardian)

London bakeries kept blending family traditions

London’s third-culture bakeries continued to fold Filipino, east Asian and other family food traditions into contemporary British baking, from Soho to south-east London. The city’s pastry counter is becoming another map of migration and neighbourhood taste. (Guardian)

Holiday cooking got stripped back

Summer food advice turned practical: cook away from home with a short essentials list, then use a meat thermometer to stop guessing at roasts and barbecue cuts. The useful kitchen trend is fewer tools, clearer checks and less heat indoors. (Guardian, Guardian)

Sweden’s quiet islands offered a slower summer

The islands of Östergötland on Sweden’s Baltic coast offer sparse communities, small boats, kayaks and early light away from busier European holiday routes. The appeal is a summer measured by water, wildlife and low-key movement between islets. (Guardian)
