News

Burnham calls rejoining EU without a referendum unthinkable as Haigh weighs scrapping nature watchdogs and Somerset gigafactory loses second builder

Prime Minister Andy Burnham told the House of Commons on Monday that rejoining the European Union without another referendum would be “unthinkable”, offering his clearest commitment yet to a public vote if he eventually concludes Britain should return to the bloc. Updating MPs on Friday’s Brussels summit—which covered food and drink border checks, emissions trading, and youth mobility, and will be enacted through Thursday’s European Partnerships Bill—Burnham dismissed warnings from former Tory premiers David Cameron, Theresa May, and Rishi Sunak against reopening Brexit, calling them “stuck in the past” over a bad deal. While declining to rule out a referendum pledge at the next election, Burnham promised “no banging on about Europe” and said No 10 had “no fixed destination” as it watches upcoming elections in EU states such as France. Rival campaigns Stay Out and the European Movement-backed Get In have already launched, while Whitehall officials questioned whether Burnham’s 120-strong “Number 10 North” office in Manchester has the clout to deliver next month’s 10-year reindustrialisation plan and his New Year “rewiring the state” white paper. (Guardian, BBC, BBC)

That state-rewiring drive drew immediate criticism from green groups after de facto Deputy Prime Minister Louise Haigh told the CBI conference that Natural England, the Environment Agency, Ofwat, and the Drinking Water Inspectorate are under review for possible abolition to curb more than 20 statutory planning consultees. In an open letter, the RSPB, the Wildlife Trusts, the National Trust, and the British Trust for Ornithology warned that scrapping the only regulator with a legal duty to protect nature would leave Britain exposed to floods, fires, droughts, and extinctions despite government impact assessments finding minimal evidence that environmental rules block housebuilding. Meanwhile, Green Party leader Zack Polanski backed a Verdant thinktank report by Sacha Deshmukh endorsing UK membership of NATO, off-the-shelf procurement, and nuclear weapons maintenance cooperation with France—breaking with the party’s 2024 pledge to dismantle Trident immediately after YouGov polling showed only 17 per cent of voters trust the Greens on defence. (Guardian, Guardian)

Disability Minister Sir Stephen Timms’s final Personal Independence Payment (PIP) review, published on Tuesday afternoon, recommends linking awards for England and Wales’s four million claimants to real-life extra costs rather than diagnoses and fast-tracking the most severely disabled 10 per cent without reassessment, while proposing no major cuts despite annual PIP spending rising from £16.3 billion pre-pandemic to £32.1 billion now and a projected £41.5 billion by 2030–31. In Somerset, Tata-owned battery maker Agratas lost its second lead builder in five months on its £5.2 billion Bridgwater gigafactory for Jaguar Land Rover—backed by £380 million in state aid—after contractor TSL walked away over cost and timeline disputes following June’s sacking of Sir Robert McAlpine, clouding its January 2028 target. Simultaneously, European benchmark Dutch month-ahead gas prices jumped up to 8.6 per cent to €96.08 per megawatt-hour on Monday amid a forecast cold snap, Qatari supply slowdowns, and continental storage at 69 per cent against an 80 per cent norm, prompting Citizens Advice chief Clare Moriarty to warn that Chancellor John Healey’s £100 council-tax rebate will be swallowed by Ofgem’s expected 26 per cent (£443) January price cap rise. (BBC, Guardian, Guardian)

In housing, Hamptons forecast that British rents will rise 14.2 per cent between 2026 and 2029—nearly double the 7.2 per cent house-price forecast—after rental supply fell 26 per cent nationwide and 39 per cent in London over a decade, making buying cheaper than renting in seven of 12 major cities outside London led by Glasgow (£288 a month cheaper) as Savills projected record corporate build-to-rent investment following May’s Renters’ Rights Act. Elsewhere, Education Secretary Lucy Powell took 18 university leaders to Beijing for the first UK–China education summit in nine years to defend a £7 billion sector serving 150,000 Chinese students, two months after MI5 warned that a Chinese front body had covertly funded over 100 UK academics; cross-party MPs urged Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband to mandate public company registers in the British Virgin Islands, Cayman Islands, and Turks and Caicos; Bristol signed a three-year reparative twinning pact with Speightstown, Barbados; and the High Court heard that Turkish heiress Nebahat Isbilen paid Andrew Mountbatten-Windsor £750,000 in 2019—later repaid, with Andrew summoned for 14 December—after adviser Selman Turk allegedly claimed it was needed for passport help, alongside £210,000 routed to Sarah Ferguson. (Guardian, BBC, Guardian, Guardian, Guardian, Guardian)

NATO vows space defence over Russian nuclear satellite reports as leaked files expose 460,000 Russian war deaths and over 1,000 suicides

NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte pledged in Berlin on Monday that the alliance would defend every inch of its territory, including in space, after German broadcasters NDR and WDR and Süddeutsche Zeitung reported intelligence shared among NATO allies warning that Russia could launch a nuclear-armed anti-satellite weapon dubbed “Sputnik-S” within 60 days. German Chancellor Friedrich Merz said the reports showed the escalation Moscow is prepared to pursue in breach of the 1967 Outer Space Treaty, while European Space Agency chief Josef Aschbacher called orbital nuclear weapons “the ultimate nightmare scenario” and Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov dismissed the reports as propaganda. The alert followed a brief Russian drone incursion into Polish airspace during overnight strikes on western Ukraine, days after Russian projectiles landed 60 miles from an Anglo-French exercise in Poland. Meanwhile, Libya’s UN-backed government in Tripoli demanded an explanation from Kyiv after Ukraine’s SBU confirmed that seven mothership-launched sea drones sank the Russian Navy corvette Merkury and hit a weapons cargo vessel in Khalifa Haftar-held Benghazi. (BBC, Guardian, BBC)

Inside Russia’s military, an analysis of more than 11 million confidential Rosstat death certificates obtained by Belarusian opposition hackers Cyber Partisans and Ukrainian collective Silent Crow identified over 460,000 Russian war deaths between February 2022 and September 2026, making war the leading cause of death for Russian men under 65 last year. The files record at least 1,087 military suicides—614 in occupied Ukraine or border regions such as Belgorod and Bryansk, including 29-year-old Sergeant Alexei Bolgov near Donetsk—alongside 885 firearm deaths of “undetermined intent” that frequently mask suicides, together equating to an entire regiment amid widespread coercion, physical abuse, and open-ended contracts. (Guardian, Guardian)

Following Sunday’s collapse of the COP31 climate summit in Antalya, former US vice-president Al Gore, Potsdam Institute director Johan Rockström, and Australian negotiations president Chris Bowen urged an overhaul of the UN consensus rule after petrostates blocked a fossil-fuel phaseout and stalled a goal to lift electricity’s share of global energy use from 20 to 35 per cent by 2035 amid surging AI data-centre demand. In his opening 2026 BBC Radio 4 Reith Lecture, Cambridge professor Robert Macfarlane called trust in COP summits misplaced, backing smaller fossil-fuel phaseout alliances such as the Santa Marta process while condemning the “end-times tech bro” and Mars colonisation. (Guardian, The Verge, Guardian)

In the US, a Second Circuit appeals panel reversed the late Judge Alvin Hellerstein’s refusal to move Donald Trump’s New York hush-money conviction to federal court, pausing his state appeal and offering a route to seek dismissal of his 34 felony counts on presidential immunity grounds. Elsewhere, Israeli academics and rights groups Standing Together and Ir Amim demanded the release of 26-year-old Hebrew University student Mahdi Tounsi, who appeared on video link bruised, without his glasses, and with his hair and beard shaved after his 8 November dorm arrest and an administrative detention order signed by Defence Minister Israel Katz; French campaigner Gisèle Pelicot announced legal action against publisher Tinbad and author Sabine Prokhoris for sending journalists non-consensual nude photos taken by her convicted ex-husband to promote a book; China announced a death sentence for an official surnamed Ma over leaking 35 state secrets for $150,000; and Iran flogged a singer and four others for performing without a hijab. (Guardian, Guardian, Guardian, Guardian, Guardian)

Centrão backs Lula’s fiscal package in pursuit of ministries as PF report on Moraes omits 47 Vorcaro messages and STF vacancy tops 400 days

Centrão parties delivered 83 per cent of their votes—211 of 254 deputies—in an extraordinary Monday night session of Brazil’s Chamber of Deputies convened by Speaker Hugo Motta (Republicanos-PB) to approve the base text of Planning Minister Bruno Moretti’s fiscal adjustment package. Motivated by currency jitters after the real touched R$ 6 to the dollar on Monday and by talks over President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva’s fourth-term cabinet and nearly R$ 3 billion in blocked parliamentary amendments, União Brasil gave 44 of 50 votes as Pedro Lucas Fernandes and Moses Rodrigues bid to retain the Regional Integration and Tourism ministries, while Republicanos gave 36 of 42 votes as it seeks a second ministry and Caixa Econômica Federal. Earlier, the Finance Ministry cut its GDP growth forecast to 1.9 per cent for 2026 and 1.8 per cent for 2027—below the 2.4 per cent budget projection—and raised IPCA inflation estimates to 5.3 per cent this year and 4.5 per cent next year, while Senate President Davi Alcolumbre (União Brasil-AP) courted first-term senators ahead of February’s leadership vote against Rogério Marinho (PL-RN) and Tereza Cristina (PP-MS) by promising faster release of non-mandatory committee amendments. (Folha, Meio, Folha, Folha)

At the Supreme Federal Court (STF), disclosures showed that the report sent by Federal Police chief Andrei Rodrigues to STF President Edson Fachin on Justice Alexandre de Moraes’s ties to arrested Banco Master owner Daniel Vorcaro relied solely on keyword searches, omitting 47 screenshots of notes-app messages exchanged in late 2025 as well as two Banco Master contracts with the law firm of Moraes’s wife, Viviane Barci de Moraes: a R$ 131 million retainer and a R$ 50 million share transfer in a Legacy jet and helicopter. In the omitted messages before his Guarulhos Airport arrest over fraudulent credit sales to BRB, Vorcaro asked whether Central Bank chief Gabriel Galípolo knew and whether Prosecutor-General Paulo Gonet or Rodrigues could reverse the probe. Meanwhile, the STF vacancy left by Justice Luís Roberto Barroso’s October 2025 retirement passed 400 days—the longest since 1985, four times the 100-day average—leaving 64 cases suspended by tie votes after the Senate rejected Lula’s nominee Jorge Messias in April. (Folha, Folha)

On the right, former president Jair Bolsonaro—hospitalised from house arrest with a small-intestine blockage—was diagnosed with bilateral aspiration pneumonia at Brasília’s DF Star ICU, where doctors Brasil Caiado and Claudio Birolini kept him on antibiotics and a nasogastric tube. The stay sparked a public clash between his son Carlos Bolsonaro, who noted on X that no family member was present while Michelle Bolsonaro returned from Israel, and the former first lady, who rebuked pessimistic posts before Carlos shared a photo of his father’s feeding tube. With Flávio and Eduardo Bolsonaro in the US and Nikolas Ferreira clashing with PL chief Valdemar Costa Neto, São Paulo Governor Tarcísio de Freitas positioned himself for 2030 by retaining 11 of 18 state secretaries and concentrating budget and regulatory power under former federal aides Natália Resende and Rafael Benini, while the Federal Police opened an R$ 11.4 million tender for three rifle-resistant presidential sedans for Lula without sunroofs, citing Benazir Bhutto’s 2007 assassination. (Folha, Folha, Meio, Folha, Folha)

Technology

Nvidia reinstates Jensen Huang after Trump intervenes to spark $1 trillion rally as Australia agrees 5 per cent global revenue fines for big tech

Six days after Nvidia chair Harvey Jones declared that the board would not reinstate co-founder Jensen Huang following his 12 November resignation over relationships with two subordinates and an admitted affair with suspected Chinese intelligence intermediary Alice Huang—former head of Singapore’s Megaspeed, suspected of smuggling Nvidia chips into China—directors voted unanimously on Sunday night to restore him as CEO, president, and director, replacing interim chief Colette Kress. President Donald Trump had sent Vice-President JD Vance to reassure the board on national security and dialled into Sunday’s meeting to overrule Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent’s call for an outside hire, boasting on CNBC that “I made the deal” and posting an AI image on Truth Social of Huang’s face on Trump’s bloodied body raising a red bra. Cheering staff greeted Huang at Nvidia’s Silicon Valley headquarters on Monday as shares surged 32 per cent to add over $1 trillion in value, lifting Nvidia back above $4 trillion after an AI tech rout had dragged the Nasdaq 5 per cent below its 1 January level despite Anthropic’s stock debut, while House Democrats vowed to investigate Trump and Huang when the CEO testifies next week. (Guardian, Guardian, BBC, The Verge, The Verge, Guardian)

In Australia, Communications Minister Anika Wells agreed amendments with the Greens on Tuesday to pass Digital Duty of Care laws this week, exposing platforms and chatbots to fines of up to A$109 million or 5 per cent of global revenue, creating a private right for citizens to sue big tech for online harm, and letting users opt out of addictive algorithms and endless scrolling from late 2027. Meanwhile, over 150 child safety groups including Jonathan Haidt’s Anxious Generation Movement and 52 UK parliamentarians urged Amazon, eBay, and Business Secretary Jonathan Reynolds to halt Christmas sales of nearly 80 AI toys after tests found a ChatGPT-linked plush bunny for three-year-olds discussing sexual consent, while aviation analyst Bob Mann Jr attributed mid-November’s US FAA flight grounding to the rollout of its AI-assisted Smart System rather than an Arkansas router. (Guardian, Guardian, Guardian)

As autonomous agents advance, JPMorgan Chase banned third-party AI agents logging in with customer credentials and Bank of America, Citibank, PNC, and Wells Fargo warned users that agent use is at their own risk ahead of Apple’s expected agentic Siri in iOS 28. For developers, Anthropic launched Claude Ship in beta (`/ship`), pairing Code Review, PR Steward, and Claude Approvals agents to review, fix, and merge GitHub pull requests alongside a revamped mobile app, while OpenAI brought GPT-6.2 Sol to ChatGPT’s main tab and set a 23 December retirement for GPT-5.6 Sol and Terra. On the open web, Andreas Kling released the first macOS and Linux alpha of the independent Rust-ported Ladybird browser engine; Kagi launched its ad-free $99-a-year Kagi Mail beta; and a coalition of 55,000 EU businesses including Mozilla, Vivaldi, and Shopify petitioned Ursula von der Leyen over Apple’s rules forcing non-WebKit browsers into separate EU-only apps. (9to5Mac, 9to5Mac, MacMagazine, 9to5Mac, The Verge, The Verge, MacMagazine)

In Apple hardware and software, reviews of the £1,999 foldable iPhone Duo praised its matt 7.6-inch internal OLED screen, 5.4-inch outer display, and right-edge iOS dock while noting its squat 84.1-millimetre width requires two hands and its soft inner screen dents under a fingernail. Apple also advertised four silicon photonics engineering roles for optical data-centre interconnects amid M8 Ultra server rumours; completed its US Apple Pay rollout at Walmart and Sam’s Club alongside iOS 27’s card grid, Amex point redemptions, and Tap to Share; named its 2026 App Store Award finalists including Ulysses, Things, and Big Walk; prepared (PRODUCT)RED editions of HomePod mini 2 and AirPods Max 2 for 1 December and a 3 December reopening of its Reading store at The Oracle after closing Basingstoke; and saw Meta launch Edits for iPadOS as open-source tool HomePod Hospital enabled Linux on first-generation HomePods. (Guardian, MacMagazine, 9to5Mac, MacStories, MacMagazine, MacMagazine, MacMagazine, MacMagazine)

New £437m London Museum shines at Smithfield as Spielberg and Paul Thomas Anderson team up for a western and GTA VI breaks PlayStation records

After a 10-year, £437 million overhaul of Smithfield’s 1883 Victorian General Market, the rebranded London Museum—formerly isolated on its 1976 London Wall roundabout—has unveiled its cathedral-like home beneath the rooftop sign “All the grit and the glitters”. Designed by Stanton Williams, Asif Khan, and Julian Harrap Architects under Horace Jones’s glazed dome and brick vaults, it turns 44 former butcher units into 250 metres of street frontages while displaying Tudor jewels, Syd’s century-old coffee stall, and a preserved piece of the Whitechapel fatberg. In Illinois, artist Theaster Gates bought Antioch’s 60,000-square-foot Pickard China factory—America’s last fine porcelain maker, where 12 staff still produce White House tableware beneath tarpaulin-patched roofs. (Guardian, Guardian)

In film and television, Steven Spielberg announced he will direct an adaptation of Kevin Barry’s 1890s western novel The Heart in Winter from a script by Paul Thomas Anderson—Anderson’s first full screenplay for another director—starring Oscar winners Jessie Buckley, Cillian Murphy, and Mark Rylance for release in 2028. At the box office, Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim’s $7 million A24 Elizabeth Holmes documentary You Can See Everything passed $102.5 million globally after a UK record £2.4 million opening for a non-music documentary. Meanwhile, John Hamburg’s fourth comedy outing Focker In-Law drew two-star reviews despite adding Ariana Grande alongside Ben Stiller and Robert De Niro, and Apple TV renewed Ted Lasso for a fifth season while ordering a Timberlake docuseries and expanding James Marsden’s CIA thriller Disavowed. (Guardian, Guardian, Guardian, The Verge, MacMagazine, MacMagazine)

Following Grand Theft Auto VI’s $3.79 billion opening day, Sony confirmed it was the largest launch in PlayStation history, with 39 million unique PS5 players logging 683 million hours (17.4 hours each) and driving the biggest non-holiday spike in new PS5 console activations since 2020. Comcast recorded its highest-ever internet traffic day (24 per cent of peak traffic), while Alinea Analytics reported a 42 per cent drop in Call of Duty’s daily players from 7.6 million to 4.4 million. In London, 2022 GTA VI leaker Arion Kurtaj received a seven-year sentence but was released on licence for time served under a five-year Discord and Telegram ban. Inside Leonida, Luther Archer confirmed that his late brother D’Angelo recorded the newly uncovered funk track Kamikaze in November 2024 with Slingbaum, Yussef Dayes, and James Gadson before dying in October 2025, while YouTuber Memeio followed power lines across 12 substations to enter the Vice City cheat code “Life is passing me by”, opening a portal to a polygonal 1980s Malibu Club featuring Danny Dyer as Kent Paul. (IGN, IGN, The Verge, IGN, IGN)

Sports

FIFA confirms UK as 2035 Women’s World Cup host as leaked files expose Infantino’s 96-year sell-off plan, Raya avoids surgery and Brentford rout Everton

A special virtual FIFA Congress ratified England, Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland on Monday as co-hosts of the 48-team, 104-match 2035 Women’s World Cup—Britain’s first World Cup since 1966, using at least 19 stadiums across the four nations alongside a UK push to bring one million more girls into football and counter online manosphere misogyny—while approving the US, Mexico, Costa Rica, and Jamaica for 2031. Hours earlier, leaked internal FIFA memos from 17 and 18 July with Thrive Capital (“Project Box”) revealed that Gianni Infantino’s scrapped $20 billion FIFA Forward Enterprise plan would have transferred commercial rights for FIFA events to a for-profit subsidiary over rolling 12-year licences for up to 96 years without a tender, backed by a $250 million break fee and a 5 per cent management bonus pool worth $50 million per $1 billion added above $20 billion. (BBC, Guardian, Guardian)

In the Premier League on Monday night, Brentford climbed to sixth by routing 10-man Everton 4–0, spoiling David Moyes’s club-record 593rd match in charge. Converted left-back Keane Lewis-Potter scored twice via deflections before Everton captain James Tarkowski received his first red card in 351 Premier League games (after a record 74 bookings) following a VAR review for a studs-up challenge on Yehor Yarmoliuk, ruling him out of Sunday’s Merseyside derby before Vitaly Janelt’s long-range strike and Fábio Carvalho’s late fourth completed the win. Meanwhile, pundits debated the “Grand Theft Auto effect” after Manchester United’s Bryan Mbeumo posted a past-midnight GTA VI photo before Sunday’s 4–0 loss at Liverpool, where United conceded 28 shots ahead of Wednesday’s Champions League trip to Sporting. (Guardian, BBC, Guardian, Guardian)

Ahead of Tuesday’s Champions League fixtures, Arsenal manager Mikel Arteta confirmed that goalkeeper David Raya needs no surgery on the shoulder injured at Newcastle and could return against Manchester City on Sunday, with Kepa Arrizabalaga deputising against Niko Kovač’s Borussia Dortmund and Jobe Bellingham. Aston Villa manager Unai Emery kept Monday training at Bodymoor Heath after UEFA barred Villa from practising on Galatasaray’s sandy, emergency-repaired Rams Park surface in Istanbul, while Paris Saint-Germain lost forward Désiré Doué for the season to a right ACL tear sustained six minutes after scoring at Nice, joining winger Khvicha Kvaratskhelia on the sidelines. (Guardian, Guardian, Guardian)
