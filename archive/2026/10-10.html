Labour holds Holborn and gains a little room to breathe

Labour has kept Holborn and St Pancras, the north London seat vacated by Keir Starmer, with a result that gives Andy Burnham’s leadership its first clear electoral lift. Sagal Abdi-Wali won 45% of the vote, ahead of Green leader Zack Polanski on 33%, after a campaign that had briefly looked capable of delivering a second extraordinary Green capture from Labour. Turnout was 38.7%. The margin broadly matched late expectations, yet the ease of the final victory matters in a diverse inner-city constituency of exactly the kind the Greens have been building towards. Labour can now argue that some voters who left during the final Starmer period are prepared to reconsider the party under new management. (Guardian, BBC)

Abdi-Wali arrived in Britain as a child refugee from Somalia in the 1990s and used her victory speech to answer doubts about who belongs in public life. Reform UK’s Zia Yusuf had questioned her Britishness early in the contest; voters have now sent her to the Commons for one of the capital’s most recognisable seats. Labour deputy leader Lucy Powell presented the result as evidence that the party has listened to progressive voters and can bring them together again. That claim will need more than a single favourable night. Burnham still has to turn a change in mood into wider enthusiasm, and the low turnout suggests many people in a safe Labour area remain detached from the offer. (Guardian)

The Greens leave with their second-best byelection result and an immediate argument over Polanski’s judgment. Their 33% confirms that they can organise a serious campaign in Labour territory. It also arrives days after the party conference adopted a motion equating Zionism with racism and supporting a single Palestinian state. Polanski missed the vote while campaigning in Holborn and later said he would have opposed the motion after two amendments he favoured were defeated. The chair of Jewish Greens called for him to resign, while other members of the group challenged her view; Caroline Lucas said he should have attended and voted. Polanski intends to stay. The party has no formal mechanism for removing its leader between its two-year elections, so the dispute will be settled through authority, membership pressure and future results. (BBC, Guardian)

The broader lesson is still unsettled. Labour was facing a Green party polling above it earlier this year, when the upset in Gorton and Denton weakened Starmer and helped accelerate the pressure on his leadership. The Greens are now around 11% nationally, while Labour has opened a lead of more than 15 points over them. Holborn suggests Burnham has interrupted that movement on the left and made Labour legible again to some former supporters. It does not show how the party will fare against Reform, or whether people who stayed home will return. For one Saturday, however, Labour has a new MP, a repaired defensive wall in London and time to make its case before the next test arrives. (Guardian)

Brazil’s runoff begins inside the margin of error

Flávio Bolsonaro has opened the second round with 52% of valid votes against Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva’s 48%, according to Datafolha. The four-point lead sits at the edge of the survey’s margin of error, and the spontaneous question produces an even tighter 46% to 44%. Bolsonaro is strongest among evangelical voters, residents of the south and south-east, and households earning between two and five minimum wages. Lula leads among Catholics, north-eastern voters and the lowest-income group. Those familiar divisions explain much of the contest. The decisive movement may come from people who avoided a firm camp in the first round and now have sixteen days to decide whether either candidate can speak to their daily concerns. (Meio, Folha)

Rejection remains high on both sides. Forty-six per cent say they will not vote for Lula under any circumstances, while 43% say the same about Bolsonaro; 3% reject them both. The remaining 8% has no fixed barrier to either name. Among voters who describe themselves as neither petista nor bolsonarista, 23% currently choose a blank or invalid ballot or do not know how they will vote. That group is large enough to reverse the lead. It is also unlikely to respond to the kind of language that excites the committed base, making the next debates unusually important: 78% of voters regard the candidates’ participation as very important. (Folha, Folha)

Lula’s campaign is concentrating on pensions, Bolsa Família and the risk that social programmes would face cuts under Bolsonaro. The difficulty is that dissatisfaction has weakened the president even among some people who depend on those programmes. Bolsonaro begins with wider regional support and has inherited more voters from defeated candidates, although he must keep a coalition that includes people attracted by change without deep loyalty to his family. Each campaign is asking voters to imagine the other presidency as a danger. The race may turn when one of them gives the unaligned electorate a practical reason to vote for his own programme. (Meio, Folha)

A peace prize and US sanctions put international law under the same spotlight

South African jurist Navi Pillay has received the Nobel peace prize for a career built around the idea that law can restrain violence. The 85-year-old defended anti-apartheid activists, served on the tribunal for Rwanda, became UN high commissioner for human rights and now sits at the International Court of Justice. Her work on the Rwanda tribunal helped establish rape and sexual violence as instruments of genocide. More recently, she chaired the UN commission that concluded Israel had committed genocidal acts in Gaza with an intent to destroy Palestinians there. Israel rejected that finding and reacted furiously to the award. The Nobel committee placed Pillay’s independence and the institutions of international law at the centre of its decision. (Guardian)

Hours later, the Trump administration sanctioned the International Criminal Court as an institution. American businesses will be barred from providing services to the court, including banking and software, after a six-month wind-down period. Seventeen judges and officials had already faced individual measures. Washington says the ICC threatens US sovereignty through the possibility of prosecuting Americans and through its arrest warrants for Israeli officials, including Benjamin Netanyahu. The ICC has no current case against a US national and describes itself as a court of last resort for cases that domestic systems cannot or will not handle. The United States, Israel, Russia, China and India are among the countries outside its 125-state membership. (BBC, Guardian)

The measures could reach beyond judges and prosecutors. If payment systems, technology and suppliers become unavailable, trials and appeals may become impossible to conduct fairly. Former Philippine president Rodrigo Duterte and other defendants accused of grave crimes are already in The Hague; licences will temporarily allow detention facilities to keep operating, but a court without a functioning administration could eventually have to release people awaiting judgment. The UK, Canada, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, Denmark and the Netherlands have rejected the sanctions and reaffirmed support for the court. Pillay’s prize celebrates legal institutions as a peaceful route to accountability. The sanctions test whether allied governments are willing to provide the practical protection those institutions now require. (BBC, Guardian)

A newborn vaccine trial advances despite an ethical revolt

A trial in Guinea-Bissau will enrol 14,000 infants and delay the hepatitis B vaccine for six weeks in half of them, after ethics bodies in Denmark and Guinea-Bissau allowed the study to proceed. Mothers will be tested before birth, and babies whose mothers test positive will receive the vaccine and remain outside the trial. The other newborns will enter a randomised study of vaccination, mortality and several broader health outcomes. Four members of the Danish committee have resigned in protest. Their concern is simple and serious: the vaccine already prevents a known infection that can produce chronic liver disease and cancer, while the hoped-for wider effects under investigation are speculative. (Guardian)

Guinea-Bissau has one of the world’s highest rates of hepatitis B, with about 18% of adults carrying the virus. Infection can pass from relatives and carers as well as during birth, the maternal test can produce false negatives, and children infected during their first year face a particularly high risk of lasting harm. The World Health Organization recommends vaccination at birth. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention has set aside $1.6m for the work, matched by private foundations, and health secretary Robert F Kennedy Jr has praised the Danish researchers while trying to remove the routine birth dose from US recommendations. The trial places the scientific question inside a population whose exposure is high and whose universal newborn programme has been delayed until 2028. That combination is why critics see consent and local approval as insufficient answers to the central ethical problem. (Guardian)

A plant selfie reveals a giant waterlily already close to extinction

A photograph of a swimmer among waterlilies in Colombia’s Inírida River has led botanists to identify a species so large that its leaves can be seen in satellite images. Kew Gardens specialist Carlos Magdalena noticed the picture on Instagram three years ago and realised the plant sat beyond the known range of Victoria amazonica. He traced the location, studied images from space and joined Colombian botanists and Indigenous communities on a five-day expedition through flooded forest. With ten hours left before their flight home, a local driver guided the team overnight to a small lake. The material collected there has now been formally described as Victoria colombiana. (Guardian)

Only three other giant waterlily species are known. Their leaves can reach three metres across and carry the weight of a small child, and their lineage predates the separation of the continents. Researchers estimate that just 30 to 50 plants of the new species remain in the wild, which would make it critically endangered almost as soon as science has named it. Work is under way to secure a Red List assessment, collect seeds and perhaps establish a more accessible population within Indigenous territory before attempting cultivation in Bogotá or at Kew. A casual picture supplied the clue; years of local knowledge, permits, taxonomy and patient fieldwork turned it into a discovery with a chance of survival. (Guardian)

Economy

Europe and China try a negotiated brake on car imports

China has agreed to moderate hybrid-car exports to the European Union in a deal Brussels believes could cut shipments by more than half over four years. That would keep several million plug-in and battery-assisted hybrids out of the European market as carmakers confront intense price competition and governments worry about factory jobs. The agreement emerged from talks that began in June and forms part of a 16-point programme covering cars, rare-earth export licences and European food and drink access to China. It is an unusual attempt to manage a trade surge before tariffs and retaliation harden into a longer conflict. (Guardian)

The practical mechanism may involve minimum prices for Chinese hybrids sold in Europe, with company undertakings used to slow volumes while remaining within World Trade Organization rules. Europe’s trade deficit with China is running at about €1.18bn a day, and the political concern reaches chemicals and textiles as well as vehicles. Chinese manufacturers have gained share through scale, lower costs and a faster transition to electrified cars; European producers are trying to protect employment while making that same transition without pushing affordable models beyond consumers’ reach. Brussels calls the car arrangement a first step and will brief diplomats before next week’s leaders’ summit. Its durability will depend on measurable export restraint, credible enforcement and enough domestic investment to give Europe’s industry something useful to do with the time it has bought. (Guardian)

Technology

Microsoft gives Windows another AI centre of gravity

Microsoft is extending Windows 11 around agents that can move between local models and cloud services, with a new class of Nvidia-powered computers aimed first at developers and creators. The Surface Laptop Ultra starts at $2,599 and uses the RTX Spark platform to run models with more than 120bn parameters on the device; competing machines from Asus, Dell, HP, Lenovo and MSI will follow, with the most expensive configurations reaching $6,999. Local processing can reduce cloud fees and keep some sensitive work on the computer. It also gives Microsoft a direct answer to developers who currently reach for a MacBook Pro when they need a powerful portable workstation. (Verge)

The broader idea, called hybrid intelligence, would let Copilot and third-party agents choose where work runs and act across the operating system. GitHub Copilot demonstrated local coding, Microsoft listed a growing range of compatible models, and Windows Search received a faster interface that behaves more like a launcher. The difficult part is still the ordinary use case. Copilot Plus PCs arrived two years ago with similar promises for neural processors, then delivered a small collection of creative tools and a troubled Recall feature whose privacy concerns delayed its launch. The new machines have a clearer audience because model builders can calculate the saving from local work. Windows users will judge the agentic system by simpler measures: whether it solves a repeated task, protects private files, shows what it changed and remains easy to stop. (Verge)

Apple prepares to bring its intelligence system into the home

Apple has scheduled a “Welcome home” launch for 13 October, with a smart display expected to lead its first coordinated home-product push in years. The device, commonly called HomePad before launch, is described as a six-inch square screen on a rounded base, with wall-mounted and countertop versions. It is expected to recognise different household members and adjust its display to the person speaking or approaching, combining Siri and Apple Intelligence with interface elements borrowed from the iPad, Apple Watch and the iPhone’s StandBy mode. An in-person press event in New York will sit alongside an online presentation. (9to5Mac, MacMagazine)

A new Apple TV 4K and the first substantial HomePod mini update are also expected. Faster chips would give both devices access to Siri’s newer AI functions, while software references point to an Apple TV surround system using four full-size HomePods. Apple has also worked with LG on locks, cameras, a thermostat and a doorbell that may appear later as jointly developed HomeKit accessories. Together, the pieces suggest a home platform organised around people and rooms, with the separate boxes working as one system. The launch will show whether Apple can make that arrangement feel coherent, and whether its delayed AI work is dependable enough for a device shared by an entire household. (9to5Mac, MacMagazine)

Gears of War turns strong attention into a difficult sales question

Gears of War: E-Day reached an estimated 1.7 million players through Game Pass while selling about 230,000 copies across Xbox and PC in its first week, according to unofficial data from Alinea Analytics. Steam accounted for 168,000 sales and the Xbox ecosystem for 62,000. At the same time, about 722,000 Game Pass members paid $30 for a Premium upgrade that unlocked five days of early access. The figures suggest a large and engaged audience for the well-reviewed prequel, alongside a steep decline in the role of the traditional $70 sale on Microsoft’s own platform. Microsoft has not confirmed the estimates, and new or retained subscriptions are missing from the calculation. (IGN)

The distinction matters because Microsoft needs revenue as well as reach from its renewed emphasis on exclusive games. Estimated earnings were $27m inside the Xbox ecosystem and about $37m in total, before accounting for any subscriptions attracted or retained by the release. Internal documents disclosed during the Activision Blizzard deal had already acknowledged that putting a game into the service reduces base-game sales during its first year. Game Pass can give a blockbuster an immediate audience and lower the cost of trying it. E-Day shows how that success can leave the value of each player harder to read, especially when the most committed customers pay for early access to a game they still do not own. (IGN)

Sports

Manchester City’s guilty verdict moves into football’s long appeal

Manchester City have been found guilty of breaching Premier League financial rules across the nine seasons from 2009-10 to 2017-18, bringing an independent commission’s long inquiry to a verdict and sending the case immediately into appeal. City continue to deny wrongdoing. A serious punishment is expected if the decision survives, but the appeal may keep the outcome unresolved into the new year. That leaves the current league leaders returning to play at Anfield on Sunday while clubs and supporters try to separate the competition in front of them from a judgment that reaches back through the most successful period in City’s history. (Guardian, BBC)

The consequences cannot be recreated through a revised table alone. Michael Carrick was in the Manchester United side that lost the 2011-12 title on goal difference after Sergio Agüero’s stoppage-time winner, and later worked on the staff of another United team that finished second to City. A medal awarded years later would leave the emotion of those afternoons exactly where it was. Liverpool finished behind City in 2013-14, 2018-19 and 2021-22; their supporters will give Sunday’s meeting an anger that exceeds an ordinary match between rivals. Arsenal manager Mikel Arteta, formerly Pep Guardiola’s assistant, says his conscience is clear but will wait for the appeal before addressing the findings in detail. Former City captain Vincent Kompany continues to take pride in what the players achieved on the pitch. (Guardian, BBC)

Rui Pinto, the Football Leaks founder whose disclosures helped trigger the investigation, will meanwhile remain in Portugal’s witness-protection programme. Authorities suspended a decision that would have removed him on Saturday after finding that he had not been properly notified or given an opportunity to challenge the risk assessment. Pinto, who shared City emails and financial records with Der Spiegel in 2018, received a suspended sentence in Portugal for offences including unlawful access to data and attempted extortion; he was later acquitted on 241 other counts. His complicated record does not lessen the importance of the documents. The Premier League must now finish the process with enough speed to preserve the present competition and enough care to produce a result that can carry the weight of the seasons under examination. (BBC, Guardian)
