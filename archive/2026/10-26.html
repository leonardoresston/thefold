Serbia’s student movement wins at the ballot box

Aleksandar Vučić conceded defeat in Serbia’s parliamentary election on Sunday, opening the way for a student-led movement to take power after two years of protests. Preliminary projections gave the Student List an outright majority in the 250-seat parliament. Vučić acknowledged that his Serbian Progressive party had lost, putting the students on course to end his 12-year dominance of the country’s politics. Celebrations spread through Belgrade and other cities as supporters gathered in the streets, waving, embracing and sounding car horns. (Guardian)

The figures remained provisional. A representative sample from CeSID and Ipsos projected 138 seats for the Student List and 104 for Vučić’s party, with minority parties likely to share the remainder. By 7pm, estimated turnout had reached 67.6%, the highest since the breakup of Yugoslavia in 1992. Some voters waited for as long as two hours. That mobilisation followed a final campaign rally in Belgrade attended by an estimated 150,000 people, an indication of how far the movement had travelled beyond university campuses. (Guardian)

Its origins lie in the collapse of a concrete canopy at Novi Sad railway station in November 2024, which killed 16 people. Anger over evidence of poor construction and corruption grew into a national challenge to the government. Vučić resigned the presidency last month and gambled on a snap election. The students assembled a full slate of parliamentary candidates, including academics, athletes and farmers, and campaigned in rural areas long considered strongholds of the ruling party. (Guardian)

The immediate task is to turn that coalition into a government capable of delivering its promises of accountability and democratic renewal. The campaign took place against a ruling party with extensive influence over broadcasting and state institutions. Monitors raised concerns about surveillance, intimidation and irregularities, including suspected vote-buying on election day. Vučić’s concession substantially eased fears that he would reject defeat. Final results and the transfer of authority will now determine how quickly the new majority can begin its work. (Guardian)

Storm Boelo kills two and cuts power across Britain

Two men were killed by falling trees on Sunday as Storm Boelo brought damaging winds, electricity failures and transport disruption across Britain. A 60-year-old man died on a minor road at Mid Kelton in south-west Scotland. In London, a man in his 20s was killed when a tree fell on his car in Mill Hill at about 12.40pm. The deaths came during a weekend in which the strongest weather affected Scotland, Northern Ireland and northern England, with dangerous conditions reaching well beyond the areas under amber warnings. (Guardian)

More than 160,000 properties were left without power. Around 53,000 Scottish homes remained disconnected at 5pm, according to network operators’ updates. Gusts reached 84mph in Argyll and Bute, 83mph in Eryri and 82mph on Tiree. Engineers were working to restore supplies, but high winds and difficult access limited where repairs could safely begin. Scotland’s resilience room met on Sunday afternoon, bringing ministers, emergency agencies and forecasters together to assess outages and the response. (Guardian)

Rail services faced speed restrictions and closures, with flooding and fallen trees affecting lines in Scotland. Flights were cancelled or diverted at Leeds Bradford airport. Network Rail also shared images of loose garden objects reaching the railway, including a trampoline beneath a train. The Met Office advised people in affected areas to secure outdoor items and stay indoors because of flying debris and dangerous waves. Boelo followed Storm Austen, which had brought strong winds to south-west England on Friday, leaving network operators and transport services dealing with successive periods of severe weather. Further difficult conditions were expected early in the week. (Guardian)

Burnham opens the argument over free movement

Andy Burnham has opened a debate over restoring free movement with the EU, with senior cabinet support and warnings from Labour MPs representing Brexit-voting areas. The prime minister signalled that keeping Britain outside free movement would cease to be an absolute condition in negotiations with Europe. He also suggested that a manifesto commitment could provide a mandate for rejoining the single market without another referendum, while full EU membership would require a new public vote. (Guardian)

Housing secretary Angela Rayner backed discussing the trade-offs and putting a proposal to voters at a general election. Foreign secretary Ed Miliband is among ministers favouring a closer European relationship. The political resistance is already visible: some MPs in the north and Midlands fear that reopening immigration arguments will expose their seats to Reform UK. Home secretary Shabana Mahmood has previously stressed the difficult choices involved in closer ties. The Conservatives and Reform attacked Burnham’s intervention, while Labour’s existing manifesto still rules out returning to the single market and free movement. (Guardian)

Businesses have a different immediate concern: the limits of the current arrangement for trade. The British Chambers of Commerce wants the Treasury to examine the economic effects of alternative UK-EU relationships, arguing that the reset agreed under Keir Starmer in May 2025 is approaching its practical limits. Paperwork and barriers to trading with European customers remain a source of frustration. Free movement would also restore reciprocal opportunities for British and EU citizens to work across borders, linking the immigration argument to the wider question of economic access. (Guardian)

Next month’s EU summit gives the discussion a near-term setting, although no change has been agreed. This week’s budget adds pressure to explain the growth consequences of Britain’s choices. Lower migration can reduce forecast tax receipts and narrow the chancellor’s room to meet fiscal rules. The Office for Budget Responsibility has previously estimated that Brexit’s trade and mobility barriers will leave long-run output about 4% lower. Burnham now has to turn an invitation to debate into a clear negotiating position and an electoral offer. (Guardian)

An extra £432m for the rough-sleeping pledge

The government plans to commit another £432m to tackling rough sleeping in this week’s budget, taking the announced funding close to £900m. Burnham wants everyone sleeping rough in England to have an offer of a safe route off the streets or wider support by Christmas. The additional money is intended to sustain that effort through the rest of the parliament, extending beyond the immediate winter deadline. An initial £442m from housing department budgets is due to be allocated before March 2027. (Guardian)

A Downing Street summit on Monday will bring together charities, businesses, housing providers and local leaders. Specialist outreach teams and additional accommodation are already operating in Coventry, with provision being expanded in places including Plymouth and Camden. The National Housing Federation is working with housing associations to identify at least 2,000 potential homes and bed spaces, including empty buildings that could be brought back into use. Those spaces will be central to translating a national funding announcement into somewhere people can actually stay. (Guardian)

Burnham’s Greater Manchester experience helps explain the emphasis on coordination. His A Bed Every Night programme expanded emergency accommodation, but reductions in rough sleeping were followed by renewed increases. He has argued that people travelled to the region because support elsewhere was weaker. The government’s current promise depends on councils and services across England moving together, and on support continuing after someone first leaves the street. Shelter welcomed the funding, while the Conservatives challenged its cost and Burnham’s record. With two months to the deadline, delivery will be judged through accommodation and sustained help for individuals. (Guardian)

France’s protest bill raises fears of collective liability

France’s senate will debate legislation this week that could make demonstrators liable for up to €10,000 of damage even when they did not personally cause it. The proposed law would apply to people continuing to demonstrate near vandals or within the same group, and would expand the use of drones over protests. Trade unions, civil liberties groups and leftwing politicians warn that the financial risk could deter peaceful participants from attending demonstrations at all. (Guardian)

The government is pushing the measure during a widening confrontation over schools, living costs and public services. High-school students have spent more than a month protesting over absent teachers, deteriorating buildings and the university applications system. Unions and yellow-vest activists have joined the demonstrations. Earlier this month, largely peaceful protests were accompanied by looting, fires and damage to public buildings, prompting tighter security measures and restrictions on gatherings. Prime minister Sébastien Lecornu argues that the new law is essential to respond to that destruction. (Guardian)

The bill was originally prepared after violence surrounding Paris Saint-Germain’s Champions League victory in May. It now sits within a broader set of restrictions, including bans on gatherings outside schools and demonstrations in city centres, as well as local curfews. Opponents fear these powers will create lasting constraints on public protest. Marine Le Pen, launching her presidential campaign in Orléans on Sunday, embraced the demand that vandals pay. Lecornu is due to meet student representatives on Monday, with pressure growing for a response on school funding and repairs alongside the security measures. (Guardian)

Machado returns to Venezuela

María Corina Machado returned to Venezuela on Sunday after almost a year abroad, appearing among supporters in Barquisimeto before travelling towards Caracas. Her ally Edmundo González, who remains in exile, confirmed her arrival and said he also intended to return. Images showed crowds greeting Machado at the Sanctuary of the Divine Shepherdess church and along the road as her convoy moved east. How she entered the country was not immediately clear. (Guardian)

Her arrival brings a leading opponent of interim president Delcy Rodríguez back into domestic politics. Machado left Venezuela last December to receive the Nobel peace prize, shortly before the US operation that seized Nicolás Maduro in January. Although her movement is widely believed to have won the 2024 presidential election, Maduro refused to relinquish power. Washington subsequently established a working relationship with Rodríguez, his former vice-president, leaving Machado outside the administration that followed his removal. (Guardian)

Machado has called for fresh presidential elections by July 2027. Whether the Trump administration supports that timetable remains unclear. US secretary of state Marco Rubio credited US-backed reconciliation talks with creating the conditions for her return, pointing to the release of political prisoners and the return of opposition figures. Venezuelan officials had previously suggested she could face arrest, but there was no visible intervention by security services as she travelled on Sunday. Her freedom to organise, and the government’s response to her renewed presence, will shape the next stage of the electoral dispute. (Guardian)

Audi commits $4bn to a US SUV factory

Audi plans to spend $4bn on its first US factory, shifting some production across the Atlantic as Volkswagen absorbs the cost of American tariffs. The plant in Macon, Georgia, is intended to produce 200,000 SUVs and create 4,500 jobs. It will build the Q7, Q8 and Q9, moving some output from Bratislava in Slovakia. Audi chief executive Gernot Döllner announced the investment alongside Donald Trump at a campaign rally on Saturday night, shortly before the US midterm elections. (Guardian)

The decision reflects the strain that 15% tariffs on EU imports are placing on European manufacturers. Volkswagen has estimated that the duties cost it up to €5bn a year before allowing for lost sales. The group has also been shifting some production of cheaper models from Mexico to the US. Building near American buyers gives Audi a way to reduce its exposure to import charges, although a new factory is a long-term investment whose benefits will depend on demand and the trading environment over many years. (Guardian)

The announcement comes as Volkswagen cuts jobs and closes factories at home. It has cited competition from Chinese carmakers, tariffs and the expense of investing in electric vehicles for other markets. Audi sees continued American demand for large petrol SUVs and is developing the Q9 with that market in mind. The investment gives Trump a prominent manufacturing announcement, while adding to the difficult allocation choices facing a European car industry trying to fund new technology and maintain competitiveness across markets with increasingly different policies. (Guardian)

The iPhone Duo’s hinge comes under scrutiny

Early reports of broken iPhone Duo screens have put the folding handset’s hinge under scrutiny just days after launch. Some owners describe cracks running down the centre of the inner display, accompanied by a horizontal strip of pixels glowing white. A teardown by iFixit offers a possible mechanical explanation, while stopping short of establishing a confirmed cause. The company suspects that part of the complex hinge may sometimes fail to descend properly, concentrating pressure in a vulnerable area. (The Verge)

An accordion-style cable passes over the same point, close to the battery’s edge. That arrangement could help explain the audible crack described by some owners. The evidence so far does not establish how widespread the failures are, and the proposed explanation remains a theory. Apple had not immediately responded to a request for comment. Establishing whether the issue affects a small batch, particular conditions or a wider design will require more information than individual failure reports and a single teardown can provide. (The Verge)

The inspection also found practical improvements. iFixit gave the Duo a repairability score of five out of ten, its highest for a foldable. The outer nano-texture layer can be replaced relatively easily without changing the OLED panel underneath. That surface scatters reflections, helping disguise the crease, while unusual subpixel arrangements may also reduce its visibility. Dust entered the port, speaker grilles and hinge, but less penetrated the phone than in the competing foldables used for comparison. Repairing the inner display itself remains difficult and expensive. Those findings leave the first weekend of ownership with encouraging repair features and an unresolved durability question. (The Verge)

Elvis catalogue owners sue Anthropic

The owners of Elvis Presley’s music catalogue have sued Anthropic, alleging that it used 274 songs without permission to train its AI models. Authentic Brands Group, which controls rights connected to Presley’s music and likeness, is seeking $150,000 for each allegedly infringed track, a total of about $41m. The case was filed on Friday and detailed on Sunday. The songs named include Can’t Help Falling in Love, All Shook Up and A Little Less Conversation. (Guardian)

The complaint also alleges that Claude generated infringing versions of copyrighted material and that safeguards introduced to prevent such output were ineffective. These are allegations to be tested in court. The filing brings an artist’s estate directly into the growing litigation over how AI developers obtain and use creative work. Record labels and industry bodies have already pursued copyright claims against AI companies, while musicians have raised concerns about imitation and the use of their work without consent. (Guardian)

For catalogue owners, the dispute concerns control of a commercially valuable body of songs and the ability to set terms for its use. For developers, cases of this kind bear on the material available for training and the measures needed to prevent protected text from appearing in responses. The Presley claim combines those questions in one action, seeking damages for the alleged use of the catalogue while challenging the effectiveness of output restrictions. No finding of infringement follows from the filing itself. (Guardian)

Sports

United consider Carrick’s future after another defeat

Manchester United’s hierarchy held crisis talks over Michael Carrick’s future after a 3–0 home defeat by Bournemouth left the club 16th in the Premier League. Rayan, Marcus Tavernier and Evanilson scored for the visitors, handing United a sixth defeat in 12 matches across all competitions. They have won only once in the league this season, and their last victory in any competition came against Sabah in the Champions League on 10 September. (Guardian)

Eddie Howe is the preferred replacement of director of football Jason Wilcox if the club makes a change. Other admired candidates include Unai Emery and Julian Nagelsmann, although Aston Villa would demand substantial compensation for Emery. No dismissal had been announced on Sunday. Carrick insisted he was continuing as normal and acknowledged that results and performances had fallen short. United would need to inform the New York Stock Exchange before announcing a managerial dismissal, with the exchange closed over the weekend. (Guardian)

The selection of Bruno Fernandes became a focal point. Carrick left his captain out of the starting side after Fernandes criticised his tactics following the midweek defeat at Como, but maintained that the decision was taken for football reasons. Fernandes remained an unused substitute. Supporters applauded Carrick as he left the pitch before booing players who followed him down the tunnel. Harry Maguire, captain for the day, said the squad backed the coach and needed to improve its own performances. The club enters the week facing a decision over leadership amid a prolonged shortage of wins. (Guardian)

Liverpool end Brighton’s winning run

Liverpool earned their first home Premier League win of the season, beating Brighton 2–0 through a Pascal Gross own goal and an Alexander Isak finish. The result ended Brighton’s run of four successive league victories and clean sheets. Isak’s goal took him to six in six Premier League appearances, providing another sign of his growing effectiveness in a forward line that also includes Florian Wirtz and Bradley Barcola. (Guardian)

Wirtz helped force the breakthrough after Ronald Araújo won the ball and delivered a low cross. The move ended with Wirtz’s shot taking a decisive deflection off Gross. Barcola then capitalised on a poor clearance from Olivier Boscagli and supplied Isak, who guided the ball into the far corner. Brighton had earlier wasted a clear opportunity through Yasin Ayari, whose missed chance became more costly as Liverpool scored twice in quick succession. (Guardian)

Ayari also hit a post after an Alisson passing error, but Brighton struggled to turn their second-half possession into clear chances. Araújo, Jérémy Jacquet and Virgil van Dijk made important interventions, with support from players tracking back ahead of them. Liverpool manager Andoni Iraola praised the aggressive start and the ability to recover possession and attack quickly. The match went ahead despite Storm Boelo, with conditions inside Anfield relatively calm. For Liverpool, the combination of sharper attacking play and sustained defensive work delivered a home league result that had been missing from their season. (Guardian)
