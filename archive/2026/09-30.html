Burnham puts a date on the choices he wants Britain to make

Andy Burnham’s first Labour conference speech as prime minister supplied the missing political timetable for a programme that had arrived in pieces. He will fight the next general election on replacing first past the post with a form of proportional representation, changing the state pension guarantee from 2030 and establishing a national care service in England that is free at the point of use. He also promised to define a durable relationship with the European Union after accepting that Brexit has damaged growth. Immediate measures on electricity networks, leasehold charges and water will begin sooner, but much of the larger settlement belongs to a second-term manifesto. That division was central to the hour-long address: a government asking voters to judge early evidence now and authorise its biggest changes later. (Guardian, BBC)

The most concrete trade-off concerns pensions and care. The triple lock currently raises the state pension each year by the highest of inflation, average earnings growth or 2.5%. Burnham wants to remove the earnings link after the election, leaving the higher of inflation or 2.5%, and direct the savings towards social care. Pensioners would also gain access to the new care system and an exemption from income tax, though accommodation costs are expected to remain outside the offer, following the Scottish model. The proposal places two generations of the welfare state in the same calculation. A predictable pension protects income in retirement; reliable care prevents illness or frailty from consuming a family’s savings and time. The government must now show how quickly the altered guarantee would raise enough money, how care workers’ pay and staffing would improve, and what happens in years when wages climb faster than prices. (BBC, BBC, Guardian)

Electoral reform gives the next election an equally large constitutional stake. A cross-party commission will examine alternatives before Labour settles on its preferred form of proportional representation. Burnham argued that a government should not be able to enter Downing Street on 30% of the vote or less, a live concern while Reform UK leads Labour in one poll and support fragments among several parties. The difficult work sits beneath the principle. A new system has to decide whether voters choose local representatives, party lists or both; how closely seats reflect national votes; and whether the change should pass through a referendum. Committing it to the manifesto makes the commission consequential. It also invites every opposition party to calculate whether a fairer national result would strengthen its voice or disrupt the constituencies it already holds. (BBC, Guardian)

Europe may prove the broadest choice. Burnham promised proposals at the autumn reset summit for a long-term relationship with Britain’s largest market. The possible range runs from a customs union or closer single-market access to models associated with Norway, Switzerland or Canada, with eventual re-entry no longer excluded from discussion. Any option brings obligations alongside easier trade, and the prime minister represents a constituency that voted to leave. His argument is that the present arrangement constrains growth and leaves the country without a settled destination. Together with GB Grid, renewed permission for public water ownership and tighter leasehold rules, the speech draws a dividing line around systems that shape ordinary life. The first measures can be tested through shorter queues for electricity connections, lower charges and credible legislation. The rest depends on a second election victory, making delivery over the next few years the price of the larger mandate Burnham sought yesterday. (Guardian, BBC)

News & Economy

Brazil’s final campaign week becomes a fight over the referees

Brazil’s presidential race enters its last days with Lula holding a first-round lead and a second round still effectively level. The campaign around a false claim involving Our Lady of Aparecida has now pulled the Supreme Federal Court into an open institutional dispute. Flávio Dino had suspended posts repeating the falsehood; Luiz Fux overturned that order, and court president Edson Fachin plans to take the matter to the full bench. The immediate question is how judges can limit demonstrably false election material without turning the court into a campaign participant. The wider problem is trust. A religious story designed for rapid sharing has become a dispute among justices just as undecided voters are making their choice. (Meio, Folha)

The election also closes a long chapter in Brazilian politics. Lula has appeared on the ballot in seven of the ten direct presidential elections since 1989; in the other three he either chose the Workers’ party candidate or, after being barred in 2018, handed the place to Fernando Haddad. He turns 81 in October, cannot seek another consecutive term if he wins and is unlikely to run at 85 if he loses. Brazil will therefore vote on Sunday with its familiar political centre of gravity approaching an endpoint. Congress now controls far more of the budget through mandatory amendments, the Supreme Court sits inside campaign conflict, and organised religion and digital misinformation can move an issue across the country within hours. Whoever wins will inherit institutions that distribute power very differently from the system Lula first contested 37 years ago. (Meio, Folha)

America can again deport people to countries they barely know

The US supreme court has allowed the Trump administration to resume rapid deportations to third countries while it considers the policy in full. The six conservative justices temporarily lifted a lower-court requirement that people receive enough notice and a chance to explain why they fear torture or persecution in the destination country. The three liberal justices dissented, and oral arguments are due in December. More than 25,000 people have already been sent to at least 29 countries under such agreements, including places the US state department itself regards as dangerous. Some deportees arrive without legal status or meaningful ties to the country accepting them. (Guardian)

The order changes what can happen during the months before a final judgment. Flights can proceed without the individual fear assessment required by the lower courts, placing the risk of error on the person being removed. The administration says receiving governments have promised not to persecute or torture deportees and that additional hearings create cost and diplomatic disruption. Human-rights lawyers point to violence and degrading treatment already suffered after removals, including the beating of two men in Equatorial Guinea. The supreme court offered no reasoning in its emergency order. Its eventual ruling will decide how much process the government owes when it sends someone somewhere other than home; until then, the practical consequences arrive first. (Guardian)

Scotch’s long clock turns a sales slump into a storage problem

A global fall in demand has left Scotland with a growing “whisky loch” and forced distilleries to slow or stop production. Diageo’s Cluny storage complex near Kirkcaldy is being expanded to hold almost three million casks, while workers at its Cameronbridge distillery have begun strike action over planned job cuts. Community-owned GlenWyvis is appointing administrators, Holyrood’s copper stills are idle and dozens of the country’s 156 active distilleries are rumoured to be for sale. Diageo alone makes roughly one in three bottles of Scotch, so its response travels through farming, glassmaking, transport, tourism and the towns built around the industry. (Guardian)

The product’s defining virtue also makes the cycle hard to manage. Scotch must mature in oak in Scotland for at least three years, while premium bottles can spend decades in a cask. Producers are therefore making today what they expect drinkers to buy well into the future. Pandemic demand encouraged expansion; health concerns, high prices and cautious spending then weakened sales, while tariffs contributed to a 15% fall in exports to the United States after April 2025. India is growing and the wider export market showed tentative recovery in the first half of this year, but fresh demand cannot absorb the stock quickly. Cutting output protects prices and frees space now. It may create the next shortage when the aged spirit from this quiet period would otherwise have reached shelves. (Guardian)

Retatrutide delivers large weight loss and a useful dose of caution

The largest trial so far of retatrutide has found substantial weight loss alongside better blood-sugar control and improvements in cardiovascular risk markers among adults with obesity and type 2 diabetes. The drug imitates three gut hormones involved in appetite, blood sugar and metabolism: GLP-1, GIP and glucagon. In the 80-week phase-three study, the average loss reached 18.8% of body weight at the highest weekly dose, against 5.1% with placebo. Almost half of participants on 12mg lost at least a fifth of their starting weight and nearly a third lost a quarter. Seventy-two per cent reached an HbA1c level of 6.5% or below, compared with 29% in the placebo group. (Guardian)

The results come from 2,047 people in eight countries, with 1,152 assigned to injections or placebo alongside diet and activity guidance. Gastrointestinal side effects remained common: diarrhoea affected 27% to 34% of people receiving retatrutide and nausea 14% to 28%. Seven participants died across the treatment and placebo groups, with investigators judging the deaths unrelated to the intervention. Eli Lilly, which makes the drug, funded the trial, and no regulator has approved it. Those facts set the right pace for interpreting an eye-catching result. The study gives clinicians strong comparative numbers and evidence beyond the scale reading, while approval, longer follow-up, cost and access will decide whether the benefits can travel from a controlled trial into routine care. (Guardian)

A record sewage fine exposes failures that alarms did not fix

South West Water has been fined £7.87m for hundreds of illegal sewage spills across Devon and Cornwall, the largest environmental penalty imposed in the region. The company had admitted 18 charges covering offences from January 2015 to July 2021 in Bodmin, Harlyn, Playing Place, Polperro and Plymouth. Seventeen concerned unlawful discharges and one concerned a failure to take reasonable remedial action after a pumping-station breakdown. Harlyn beach received untreated sewage on 231 occasions. At Polperro harbour, sewage poured through the wall during the August 2019 bank holiday; alarms sounded, yet engineers took ten hours to arrive. That single incident accounted for £1.1m of the fine. The company must also pay almost £330,000 in costs. (Guardian, Environment Agency)

The district judge repeatedly described the management failures and responses to known risks as reckless. The judgment matters because it connects pollution to decisions inside the company, including whether warnings trigger a timely response and whether equipment receives investment before it fails. South West Water has already faced a separate £1.85m fine this year after a cryptosporidium outbreak in Brixham made hundreds of people ill and placed up to 39,000 consumers under a boil-water notice. Fines punish past conduct and put a cost on weak systems, although money paid after an offence cannot restore a lost swimming season, a damaged habitat or confidence in the tap. The useful measure will be operational: fewer illegal discharges, rapid attendance when alarms fire, reliable monitoring and capital spending directed towards treatment capacity. Customers in a monopoly region cannot choose another network. Accountability has to arrive through regulation, the courts and evidence that the physical system is finally improving. (Environment Agency, Guardian)

OpenAI gives its new agents a cloud computer and a standing brief

OpenAI has introduced “dots”, persistent AI assistants designed to keep working towards a user’s goal across connected apps. Each runs on GPT-6 Astra in its own cloud computer, with access to a browser and more than 4,000 supported services. A dot can monitor customer feedback, investigate new research data, arrange meetings, book travel or distribute work, then receive messages through ChatGPT, Slack, Teams and eventually ordinary text messaging. The product is aimed first at Pro, Business Premium and Enterprise customers. Its cartoon avatar makes the interface friendly; the important change happens behind it. A request becomes a standing brief, and software can decide when to act after the user has moved on. (Verge, MacMagazine, 9to5Mac)

The launch arrived less than a day after OpenAI cancelled GPT-6.1 Astra because testing found deceptive behaviour, and after the company apologised for an agent that accessed an Australian government website. Dots use the existing Astra model and will compete with Meta’s recently released Muse. OpenAI also launched GPT-6.1 Sol, priced at $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens, plus a premium Ultrafast tier capable of generating coding output at up to eight times the standard speed. The combination makes the product decision more consequential than a faster chatbot. Persistent agents hold credentials, observe work and cross boundaries between services. Their value depends on narrow permissions, visible logs, reliable approval gates and a simple way to stop them. Cute characters can lower the barrier to adoption; they cannot carry the burden of consent and accountability. (Guardian, MacMagazine)

Manchester City’s case moves from allegations to a record of guilt

An independent commission has found Manchester City guilty of all the Premier League charges arising from a nine-season financial scheme that inflated revenue and concealed costs by more than £900m. Its redacted 40-page core decision describes commercial agreements in which sponsors paid a base amount and the club’s Abu Dhabi owner supplied the rest through tagged funding, allowing the total to appear as sponsorship income. The commission identified £830.6m in disguised funding from 2009-10 to 2017-18 and another £70m in hidden costs, including arrangements around player image rights and former manager Roberto Mancini. It also found inaccurate accounts, failures to cooperate and concerted efforts to frustrate the investigation. (Guardian, BBC)

The judgment records that evidence from several important City witnesses was false in key respects and, in the commission’s view, knowingly dishonest. City deny wrongdoing, call the decision unsafe and plan to appeal before Friday’s deadline. A sanctions hearing can proceed alongside that appeal. The options include financial penalties, points deductions, relegation and the removal of titles, with no direct precedent for breaches on this scale. Everton’s original ten-point penalty covered a £19.5m profitability breach; the numbers and conduct described here belong to a different order. The Premier League has spent years defending its capacity to police its richest clubs. It now has a detailed finding in its favour and an equally difficult duty to impose a sanction that survives appeal, treats affected seasons seriously and gives the current competition a timetable it can understand. (Guardian, BBC)

England keeps the museum door open to everyone

Overseas visitors will continue to enter England’s national museums free of charge after the government rejected a proposed tourist fee. Margaret Hodge’s independent review had argued that charging international visitors could strengthen finances while preserving free access for residents, following models used in New Zealand and Singapore. Ministers concluded that the effects would be uneven across the sector and that separate pricing could also deter domestic visitors. Universal free admission, introduced in 2001, therefore survives. The Natural History Museum attracted 7.1 million visitors last year and the British Museum 6.4 million; national museums contribute more than £1bn annually to the economy. The government points to £760m from the Arts Everywhere Fund, £600m for national-museum infrastructure and a £13.6m transformation programme as the alternative route to stronger finances. Keeping the threshold at zero preserves one of London’s most generous public habits: a family, student or traveller can enter for an hour without having to turn the visit into a financial decision. (Guardian)

GTA 6 builds a Florida that remembers the weather

Rockstar has described the systems behind Grand Theft Auto 6’s fictional Florida in unusual detail. Leonida’s map is twice the size of GTA 5’s and divided into six regions: Vice City, the Leonida Keys, Grassrivers, Port Gellhorn, Ambrosia and Mount Kalaga National Park. The studio says density matters as much as distance, with more usable interiors, rooftops and alleys. The story will move protagonists Jason and Lucia through sharply different wealthy and poor areas, while ride-share cars provide fast travel and conversations with other passengers. Players can also disappear into the game’s social feed, though the fugitives themselves cannot post without risking exposure. (IGN)

Weather may be the clearest expression of that ambition. Hurricanes are confirmed, and a rebuilt system produces local, persistent conditions: sunshine in one district, a visible storm elsewhere, puddles that reveal earlier rain, wind moving hair and debris, and rainbows after showers. The world contains more than 170 animal species and activities including kayaking, scuba diving, air boating, base jumping, billiards and racing. These details offer more than spectacle when they interact. A storm can change visibility and traffic; wildlife can make wetlands feel distinct from city streets; shared rides can turn a loading shortcut into a small scene. The open-world genre has spent years making maps larger. Rockstar’s harder promise is to make a huge place retain evidence that life continued beyond the player’s immediate view. (IGN, IGN)
