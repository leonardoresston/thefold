LEAD STORY

Burnham put Trident renewal inside an industrial pitch

Andy Burnham will put Trident renewal at the centre of a defence and industry push in Barrow-in-Furness, using the next phase of Britain’s nuclear submarine programme to frame defence spending as work for communities as well as a security commitment. The trip moves his first week in office from social care into national security and reindustrialisation. (Guardian)

The social care argument still shadows the new government. Burnham’s call for cross-party reform has opened the political test that sank earlier attempts: turning broad agreement into a funding plan that survives Westminster and reaches households. (Guardian, Guardian, BBC)

NEWS

European wildfires killed firefighters and emptied villages

Three firefighters died on Crete and in mainland Greece as repeated heatwaves and strong winds made Europe’s fires harder to contain. In Spain, residents west of Madrid returned to damaged homes after evacuations, while authorities kept watch for new fronts in extreme heat. (Guardian, Guardian)

UK education and police data were stolen

The Department for Education and a police database were hit by a cyber-attack that exposed more than 740,000 pieces of data. Details taken included email addresses and phone numbers belonging to parents, staff and public-service users. (Guardian)

A teenager was convicted over a UK contract killing plot

Johannes Kongsnes Natland, a Norwegian teenager, was found guilty of travelling to England to carry out a killing ordered by an international crime gang linked to Iran. The case put a foreign-directed murder plot inside a British criminal court. (Guardian)

Rushdie’s attacker was convicted of terrorism offences

Hadi Matar was convicted in federal court over the 2022 stabbing of Salman Rushdie, adding terrorism convictions to the 25-year New York state sentence he is already serving. The jury found him guilty of all charges tied to the attack on the British-Indian author. (Guardian, BBC)

Brazil’s election courts moved on AI

Alexandre de Moraes gave Jair Bolsonaro 48 hours to explain whether he approved an AI video used at the PL convention; Bolsonaro’s defence later said he had not authorised the use of his image. The TSE also ordered big tech companies to explain how they will moderate electoral content, while allowing secrecy over some AI data. (Meio, Folha, Folha, Folha)

Reform donations drew fresh scrutiny

George Cottrell transferred more than $2m to his mother in the days before Fiona Cottrell made donations to Reform UK. The transfers, made 24 to 72 hours before the political gifts, reopened questions around money moving into Nigel Farage’s party. (Guardian)

Police closed the Polanski guillotine-image case

Scotland Yard will take no further action after Green party leader Zack Polanski shared an image of a man wearing a T-shirt showing a guillotine and Nigel Farage. Polanski described the repost as an inadvertent mistake but refused to apologise to his Reform rival. (Guardian, BBC)

The Fed held rates in a divided vote

The US Federal Reserve kept interest rates unchanged for the fifth time since December, despite renewed pressure from Donald Trump for cuts. The divided decision showed inflation worries still weighing on the central bank after energy prices rose again. (Guardian)

TfL opened a potential £1bn Ulez case

Transport for London is pursuing legal action worth up to £1bn against carmakers over diesel vehicles it says entered the clean-air zone without paying when they should have been treated as non-compliant. The case turns emissions enforcement into a major financial claim. (Guardian)

Meta stumbled as AI spending stayed in focus

Meta shares fell nearly 8% after second-quarter earnings missed forecasts, even as Mark Zuckerberg continued to push the company’s AI story. The company is preparing a larger move into personal AI agents that can act on users’ behalf. (Guardian, The Verge)

Microsoft’s cloud growth could not hide Xbox weakness

Microsoft’s cloud and AI businesses powered ahead, while Xbox content and services revenue fell 10% and hardware sales dropped 13% in the quarter. The split kept gaming under pressure inside a company otherwise lifted by enterprise AI demand. (The Verge)

TECHNOLOGY

Microsoft confirmed a Copilot super app

Microsoft is building a Copilot app that combines chat, coding and agentic features across consumer and commercial use. Satya Nadella told investors the product is due this year, making Copilot a broader front door for Microsoft’s AI work. (The Verge)

OpenAI pointed toward a device family

OpenAI president Greg Brockman described work on a family of devices for interacting with the company’s AI models, while also discussing the desktop ChatGPT app and the legal fight with Apple. The hardware push remains lightly sketched but is now part of the company’s public roadmap. (The Verge, MacMagazine)

Russia put Telegram’s founder on wanted lists

Russian officials charged Pavel Durov with aiding terrorism and put the Telegram founder on international wanted lists. The FSB accused the messaging app of being used for sabotage and murder, extending Moscow’s crackdown on online communications. (Guardian)

Qualcomm warned phone chip prices are rising

Qualcomm will raise processor prices from 1 September, adding another cost pressure for phone makers already dealing with expensive memory. The move points toward higher handset costs in the next upgrade cycle. (The Verge)

The US robot ban reached household vacuums

A US ban on advanced robotic devices swept beyond humanoids and into robot vacuums, including Roomba-style products and Chinese-made cleaners. The rule treats mapping and sensor-heavy consumer hardware as part of a wider security fight over robotics. (The Verge)

Apple pushed back on UK App Store payment changes

Apple formally opposed UK proposals that would force changes to external payments on iOS. The response kept Britain inside the global fight over whether app stores can keep developers inside their own billing systems. (MacMagazine)

CULTURE

Peppa Pig won its Wolfoo copyright case

The owners of Peppa Pig won a copyright battle against the children’s animation Wolfoo after a judge found extensive infringement. The order requires Wolfoo videos to be removed from online platforms. (Guardian)

V&A Storehouse staff backed strike action

Staff at the V&A Storehouse in east London voted overwhelmingly for strike action in a dispute over toilet and hydration breaks. Employees across the V&A’s London sites also backed wider action, raising pressure on one of the capital’s newest cultural venues. (Guardian)

Music lost Glen Hansard and Kavinsky

Glen Hansard, the Irish singer-songwriter and Once star, died aged 56 in a motorcycle crash. French electronic producer Kavinsky, whose Nightcall became tied to Drive, died aged 50, with authorities saying there were no suspicious elements. (Guardian, Guardian)

BTS stepped away from the Grammys

BTS will not submit Arirang or its songs for the 2027 Grammy awards after the introduction of a new Asian pop prize. The move places one of east Asia’s biggest acts in direct conflict with the academy’s new category structure. (Guardian)

Andrew Scott’s Ian Charleson film will open London festival

Elsinore, an Andrew Scott-led biopic of Chariots of Fire actor Ian Charleson, will open this year’s London film festival. The slot gives the Oscar-tipped drama the festival’s first-night stage. (Guardian)

SPORTS

Uefa called an emergency meeting over Fifa’s World Cup plan

Uefa summoned its 55 members for a Thursday meeting to coordinate opposition to Gianni Infantino’s plan to sell stakes in a new World Cup commercial venture. The backlash has hardened across European football as Fifa pushes a competition-rights structure valued around $20bn. (Guardian, Guardian)

Argentina faced Fifa charges after the World Cup final

Fifa opened disciplinary proceedings against Argentina over a Falklands banner displayed after the semi-final against England and disorder around the final loss to Spain. Leandro Paredes and Nahuel Molina are also under investigation over a brawl after the final. (Guardian, BBC)

Premier League clubs split over the EFL settlement

Manchester City are leading opposition to the Premier League’s proposed £1.5bn settlement with the English Football League before a Thursday meeting on the package. The dispute centres on how the funding burden would be shared across top-flight clubs. (Guardian)

LIFESTYLE

Early sugar limits were linked to better brain health

A study linked lower sugar intake before age two with better long-term brain health, while stopping short of treating the evidence as conclusive. The finding adds another measure to the argument over how much sugar young children should encounter. (BBC)

Organic eggs carried a heavier climate cost

A UK study found organic eggs can have twice the climate impact of eggs from caged chickens, with a full shift to organic free-range production linked to greenhouse emissions equivalent to 2.316m tonnes of CO2. The finding complicates a familiar supermarket choice. (Guardian)

British beer kept shrinking by strength

Drinkflation kept spreading through beer as brands held prices and sizes steady while cutting alcohol content. The change leaves shoppers paying the same for weaker drinks and gives producers a quieter way to manage costs and tax pressure. (Guardian)

Rome’s San Lorenzo stayed outside the tourist crush

San Lorenzo, Rome’s student and working-class quarter, remains a different city break from the crowded centre, built around murals, cheap food, nightlife and a long memory of resistance. The neighbourhood offers a looser route through a capital strained by visitor numbers. (Guardian)
