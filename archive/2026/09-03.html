Burnham’s promises meet the price of borrowing

Andy Burnham’s first budget test has arrived before the budget itself. A broad sell-off in government debt pushed the UK’s ten-year borrowing cost as high as 5.29% on Wednesday, its highest level since 2008, before it eased slightly. The move was global, driven by renewed inflation fears, higher oil costs and vast demand for capital from technology companies. Britain is especially exposed because expensive debt feeds quickly into the narrow margin the Treasury keeps against its fiscal rules. (Guardian)

The prime minister used his first questions in the Commons to promise decisions grounded in fiscal responsibility and said the 28 October budget would keep to the rules while easing household costs. That reassurance followed Tuesday’s expansive programme: more public control of utilities, help with energy bills and a possible rise in defence spending to 3% of GDP. Those commitments were popular with many Labour MPs. They also made investors ask how the sums would work. (Guardian)

The arithmetic is unforgiving. Analysts think higher debt costs could consume as much as half of the £24bn cushion built into the previous fiscal plans. Debt interest already takes roughly one pound in every twelve spent by the government. Chancellor John Healey must also cover a defence shortfall, Burnham’s proposed VAT cut on electricity and the likely effect of lower immigration on the growth forecast. Markets now expect three Bank of England rate rises over the next year, taking the policy rate to 4.5% and threatening another round of mortgage pressure. (Guardian)

Healey will set out his growth approach on Monday. Burnham, meanwhile, hosts Emmanuel Macron today as he begins building his own European relationships and prepares for a possible UK–EU reset summit in November. The government’s political ambition is clear; the next few weeks will show how much room it can buy for that ambition, and what it is prepared to give up when the bill comes due. (Guardian)

News

Israel seeks US backing for Palestinian expulsions from Gaza

Israel’s defence minister, Israel Katz, has put forced migration from Gaza back at the centre of government policy. He said Israel was prepared to move Palestinians by sea or air and was seeking Donald Trump’s approval, arguing that US support could persuade neighbouring countries to receive them. Egypt has pressed Washington to oppose any such plan. The removal of a population under military occupation would be widely regarded as ethnic cleansing and a potential war crime. (Guardian)

Trump proposed a US takeover of Gaza in early 2025 that depended on moving Palestinians out, then retreated from it. The White House’s later 20-point peace plan omitted deportation. Katz described the idea as frozen and still alive. The White House gave no immediate answer on whether discussions continue. His intervention comes during an Israeli election season in which Benjamin Netanyahu’s coalition is leaning into harsher measures against Palestinians. (Guardian)

Netanyahu visited an Israeli position near the yellow line inside Gaza and said troops would remain there until Hamas disarmed. A phased withdrawal has been proposed as part of the recent ceasefire, yet Israel continues to tie any retreat to the complete demilitarisation of the territory. Katz’s appeal to Washington therefore reaches beyond campaign rhetoric: it tests whether the United States will place a limit on what Israel can demand from Gaza’s civilian population. (Guardian)

The world’s best climate outcome now includes overshoot

A UN Environment Programme assessment says global heating will reach at least 1.8C above pre-industrial levels even under the most optimistic plausible path. Crossing the Paris agreement’s 1.5C threshold is now considered unavoidable and likely within the next few years. Every additional fraction of a degree increases extreme weather, glacier loss, ecosystem damage and the threat to islands and coastal cities. (Guardian)

The report sets out an “overshoot, peak and decline” path: rapid, sustained emissions cuts would have to work alongside large-scale removal of carbon dioxide from the atmosphere. Net zero remains an essential milestone, and forests or engineered removal cannot substitute for cutting fossil-fuel pollution. The authors believe temperatures could return to 1.5C later this century if the peak stays around 1.8C. Beyond that, weakened natural carbon stores make the route back far less credible. (Guardian)

The consequences grow sharply as temperatures rise. Heating of 3C could strip more than a quarter of the mass from glaciers by 2100 and add up to 13cm to sea levels from that source alone. Global food output could fall by as much as 14% by 2050 without effective adaptation. The revised outlook narrows the space for delay while preserving a meaningful distinction between a dangerous overshoot that can be reversed and a hotter world with irreversible losses. (Guardian)

Nepal’s flood disaster moves from rescue to survival

More than 1,200 people have died in Nepal and Tibet after a melting Himalayan glacier collapsed into a valley and sent flash floods down the Bhotekoshi and Trishuli rivers. More than 5,000 people are missing in Nepal and another 546 in Tibet. Betrawati had only minutes of warning: the collapse began at 8.37am and the town was engulfed by 8.50. Mud buried much of a six-storey building, carried a shop 200 metres and forced the river into the route of the old highway. (Guardian)

Rescuers are still reaching settlements that disappeared from the map. Ground teams recovered 90 bodies after finally entering Timure. In Syabru Besi, where one building remains, at least 500 people — a quarter of the population — are missing. More than 200 children from Nilakantha school are also unaccounted for, many believed to have been walking to class when the water arrived. Families displaced by the 2015 earthquake had rebuilt in informal homes along the riverbank, placing them in the flood’s path once again. (Guardian)

Washed-out highways have left a twelve-hour mud-and-stone route as the only road from Kathmandu towards Rasuwa, and relief trucks are stuck short of the worst-hit communities. Army helicopters continue to lift survivors from hillsides and isolated villages. The immediate emergency is giving way to a longer crisis of orphaned children, missing relatives and towns that have lost homes, schools, roads and livelihoods at once. (Guardian)

The hidden records of Britain’s nuclear test veterans

Former defence minister Tobias Ellwood has acknowledged that he gave Parliament inaccurate information in 2018 about medical records from Britain’s nuclear weapons tests. He had been told that the Ministry of Defence could find no evidence of blood samples taken for radiological monitoring. Those samples existed across the armed services, but veterans trying to link cancers and blood disorders to radiation exposure have been unable to obtain them. (Guardian)

About 22,000 British personnel took part in 45 large-scale tests in Australia and the South Pacific between 1952 and 1967. Pilots flew through mushroom clouds, soldiers crawled through fallout and troops watched detonations in shorts and short-sleeved shirts. Many later became seriously ill and were denied pensions for lack of medical evidence. Britain, unlike France, the United States and the former Soviet Union, has never compensated its atomic test veterans. (Guardian)

The claim that no records existed unravelled in 2022 with the discovery of Merlin, a classified database containing 28,000 documents assembled after a 2007 lawsuit by 1,000 veterans and relatives. The ministry says it has since examined tens of thousands of documents and published the findings of a records exercise. Ellwood’s admission leaves a harder question for the government: why ministers were kept from the full picture, and whether the concealed evidence denied ageing veterans a fair route to recognition and compensation. (Guardian)

Brazil’s Banco Master case spreads through the state

Brazil’s Banco Master investigation has become an open conflict inside the Supreme Court. Justice André Mendonça made public a Federal Police report that identified fellow justice Alexandre de Moraes as the recipient of messages from former banker Daniel Vorcaro. Prosecutor general Paulo Gonet then asked for the order behind the report to be annulled, arguing that Mendonça had exceeded his authority. Gonet also appears in the material he wants set aside. Court president Edson Fachin must decide whether the full bench will confront the disclosures. (Meio, Folha)

The police material describes Vorcaro seeking help as his bank approached liquidation and asking Moraes whether he should leave Brazil two days before his arrest. File metadata attributes the final edit of a contract worth about R$130m between Banco Master and the law office of Moraes’s wife to a user named “Ministro Alexandre de Moraes”. The bank also issued credit cards with R$300,000 limits to two of the justice’s children. These findings remain allegations within a contested investigation, but they demand answers about access, influence and conflicts of interest at the top of the judiciary. (Meio, Folha)

The crisis lands one month before a presidential election. Fachin has promised measures in the coming days and said Supreme Court justices must observe limits. Congress is discussing ways Moraes might leave without an impeachment trial, while the investigation also touches financing linked to a film about former president Jair Bolsonaro. Each institution is now judging evidence that implicates parts of the others, making transparency and a clear chain of lawful authority essential to preserving public confidence. (Meio, Folha)

Technology

Uber drivers challenge the algorithm that sets their pay

Drivers from Britain, the Netherlands and elsewhere in Europe have brought a collective action against Uber in Amsterdam, home of the company’s European headquarters. The claim covers about 241,000 drivers and argues that automated profiling is used to set personalised fares, allocate work and train Uber’s models in breach of data-protection law. The drivers want compensation and an injunction; the potential damages could reach billions of dollars. (Guardian)

The case turns a familiar workplace complaint into a test of algorithmic management. Drivers say identical journeys can be offered to different people at different prices and that a return trip may pay less when the system knows someone is far from home. One pair in Tottenham compared their screens and found offers of £27 and £23 for the same job. The lawsuit claims dynamic pricing has reduced UK drivers’ annual income by about £5,000 since 2023. (Guardian)

Uber rejects the allegations. It says individual acceptance history does not determine a fare and that prices reflect the journey, duration, destination, demand, promotions and testing. The challenge follows an €825m fine from the Dutch data-protection authority over automated account deactivations, which Uber is appealing. Together, the disputes ask how much explanation workers are owed when software functions as their manager and the company alone can see its reasoning. (Guardian)

Google keeps its advertising exchange

A federal judge in Virginia has rejected the US Justice Department’s attempt to force Google to sell AdX, the exchange at the centre of its online advertising machinery. Judge Leonie Brinkema accepted most of the proposed behavioural remedies but stopped short of a breakup. AdX runs instant auctions when web pages load and charges publishers a 20% fee. Its direct contribution to Google is modest; its position between publishers and advertisers gives it outsized control over the open web’s economics. (Guardian)

Brinkema ruled in 2025 that Google held illegal monopolies in publisher ad servers and exchanges, including through links that steered customers towards AdX. The remedy now leaves that structure intact under tighter conduct rules. It is the third consecutive failure by American antitrust authorities to secure a big-tech breakup, after judges declined to make Meta sell Instagram and WhatsApp or Google sell Chrome. Trials against Amazon and Apple are not expected before 2027, so the question of whether courts can meaningfully unwind digital monopolies remains open. (Guardian)

A weak identity link opened thousands of Dropbox accounts

About 5,000 Dropbox accounts were compromised through a single-sign-on flaw involving Lenovo IDs, and files were downloaded from roughly 1,500 of them. Attackers could register a Lenovo identity using a victim’s email address and then present that identity to Dropbox. The service matched the email to an existing account and created a session without asking for the Dropbox password or confirmation that the new login method should be linked. (9to5Mac)

The unauthorised access ran from 4 to 21 August. Dropbox has fixed the flaw and expired every session authenticated through a Lenovo ID. The episode shows why federation needs checks at both ends: Lenovo’s email verification could be bypassed, while Dropbox treated the external identity provider’s claim as sufficient proof for an established account. Anyone affected should review account activity, reset credentials and enable two-factor authentication, especially if the same email address is used across other services. (9to5Mac)

Culture

Cassandra Wilson leaves jazz a wider map

Cassandra Wilson, the Mississippi-born singer whose contralto joined jazz to blues, folk, country, R&B and popular song, has died at 70. She died at home in Jackson on Tuesday with family, friends and her manager present. Wilson began piano lessons at six, played clarinet in marching bands and performed in coffeehouses while at college before moving through New Orleans into New York’s M-Base collective in the early 1980s. Her voice carried the musical history of the American south without being confined by genre. (Guardian)

Four Grammy nominations brought two wins for best jazz vocal album: New Moon Daughter in 1997 and Loverly in 2009. Her final album, 2015’s Coming Forth by Day, reworked Billie Holiday’s songbook and arrived alongside Wilson’s Apollo Theater concert marking Holiday’s centenary. The National Endowment for the Arts named her a Jazz Master in 2022. Her catalogue endures as an argument for jazz as a living language, capable of absorbing many traditions while retaining a sound unmistakably rooted in place. (Guardian)

Wilson’s influence travelled through musicians as well as records. Jon Batiste, whom she took on tour early in his career, recalled the way she wove folkloric and diasporic music into experimental and popular forms. Jackson’s mayor described her as a singular musical voice who carried Mississippi wherever she performed. Those tributes capture the continuity in a career that kept stretching outward: every new setting remained connected to the blues, phrasing and deep tonal character she learned at home. (Guardian)

CD Projekt concentrates its studios around The Witcher 4

CD Projekt Red has put its remake of the first Witcher game on hold while development of The Witcher 4 and a new expansion for The Witcher 3 move ahead. The decision gives the company a clearer order of work across several large projects and delays a remake that had been assigned to the Polish studio Fool’s Theory. For players, the practical message is simple: the next mainline game has priority, and the return to Geralt’s earliest adventure will wait until that production is further along. (IGN)

The company is also expanding the small team behind Project Hadar, its first wholly original intellectual property. More than 30 people are working on it, with a plan to reach as many as 50 by the end of the year. That remains a fraction of the staff committed to The Witcher 4, and the project has not been formally unveiled. The hiring nevertheless marks the early construction of a third creative pillar beside The Witcher and Cyberpunk, built cautiously while the studio’s immediate resources stay focused on games already promised. (IGN)

Sports

The Premier League closes a £3.4bn window

Premier League clubs spent more than £3.4bn in the summer transfer window, 11.9% above last year and far beyond their European rivals. Manchester City led with roughly £458m and completed a total midfield rebuild, including the record-equalling £125m purchase of Enzo Fernández from Chelsea. Liverpool recorded the largest net spend at about £217m and paid £123m for Bradley Barcola. Even Crystal Palace, the division’s lowest spender, committed around £50m. (Guardian, BBC)

Arsenal’s window ended with Gabriel Jesus joining Barcelona and uncertainty around Gabriel Martinelli, leaving a title-winning attack led by Bukayo Saka, Martin Ødegaard, Eberechi Eze and Kai Havertz. Across the league, accounting rules and rising television income continue to turn players into tradeable assets as much as members of settled teams. The fees make for compelling deadline-day theatre; they also widen the financial distance between England and the rest of Europe, and place more of each club’s future performance into the price paid today. (Guardian)

Chelsea complete the Women’s Champions League field

Chelsea beat Real Sociedad 1–0 in San Sebastián and 6–2 on aggregate to enter the Women’s Champions League phase alongside two other English clubs. Keira Walsh settled a quiet second leg in the 54th minute, sweeping in from a corner after the home side had denied Chelsea clear chances. The commanding first-leg advantage allowed Sonia Bompastor to rotate four players before Saturday’s Women’s Super League opener against Aston Villa. (Guardian)

The performance was more functional than fluent, but qualification was secured without late anxiety. Bompastor also gave debuts from the bench to Nelly Las and Giulia Dragoni and European minutes to 17-year-old Chloe Sarwie. Real Sociedad, playing their second match under hurriedly appointed coach Gorka Álvarez, move into the Europa League. Chelsea return home with their main continental objective met and valuable competitive work completed before the domestic season begins. (Guardian)
