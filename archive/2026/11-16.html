News

Burnham pledges early-years overhaul and ‘benefits backstop’ for young people as 100-MP Rosebank rebellion brews

Andy Burnham has promised a “root-and-branch rethink” of how the state supports childhood and young adulthood ahead of Tuesday’s publication of Alan Milburn’s review into youth unemployment. With the number of 16-to-24-year-olds outside education, employment, or training (NEET) passing one million this year and costing £22 billion annually, the prime minister said politicians bore responsibility for sixteen years of austerity that closed Sure Start centres, youth clubs, and mental health services while treating technical schooling as inferior to academic routes. Seeking to prevent a repeat of last summer’s backbench welfare revolt against Keir Starmer, Burnham pledged parity between vocational and academic pathways and nearly 500,000 job and training placements over a decade, while insisting that welfare must act as a backstop rather than a starting point. Under the plan, 18-to-24-year-olds on Universal Credit for a year will be guaranteed a funded six-month job matched to their skills, while under-25s lose access to Personal Independence Payment and the Universal Credit health element in favour of a standard youth allowance, with Milburn noting that upfront investment means the overall benefits bill will take ten years to fall. (Guardian, Guardian)

Downing Street’s welfare drive coincides with a growing rebellion on the Labour benches over the giant Rosebank oilfield off Shetland, where up to 100 MPs are expected to sign a letter urging Burnham to refuse consent. Organised with the Labour environment affiliate Sera—whose members include Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband and ministers Luke Pollard, Georgia Gould, and Matthew Pennycook—the letter already carries 75 signatures, backed heavily by the 2024 intake and MPs who reluctantly accepted the gas-focused Jackdaw field. Signatories warn that approving Rosebank would destroy Britain’s climate credibility without lowering energy bills, exposing a cabinet split between Energy Secretary Miatta Fahnbulleh, who leans towards rejection, and Chancellor John Healey, who favours drilling. North Sea producer Adura pushed back on Sunday as first gas came ashore from Jackdaw, projecting that the two fields would generate £1.4 billion in tax revenue this parliament and £3.8 billion by the end of the next. (Guardian)

Defence and justice pressures are also converging across Whitehall. Defence Secretary Wes Streeting welcomed the 140 crew of the Vanguard-class submarine HMS Vengeance back to HM Naval Base Clyde at Faslane on Sunday after a record 217 days submerged on nuclear deterrent patrol since 12 April—a deployment extended when a relief boat suffered a technical fault, leaving the hull encrusted in marine growth and missing acoustic tiles. In England and Wales, section 242 of the Crime and Policing Act 2026 takes effect today, granting a statutory pardon and wiping police records for roughly 150 women investigated or convicted over ending their own pregnancies across the past 150 years, prompting cleared women such as Nicola Packer to demand a formal government apology. Meanwhile, government rape adviser Professor Katrin Hohl and Victims’ Commissioner Claire Waxman urged ministers to reform courtroom directions on rape myths after the conviction of Russell Brand, who was moved over the weekend from HMP Wandsworth to category C HMP Littlehey in Cambridgeshire as Culture Secretary Lisa Nandy warned broadcasters to stamp out abuse or face regulation. (BBC, Guardian, Guardian, BBC)

Iran strikes US base in Bahrain and Houthis seize Red Sea gateway after Saudi-backed forces retreat

Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps launched six drones at a US air defence radar at the Al-Rifaa helicopter base in Bahrain on Sunday, the second Iranian drone strike on the Gulf state in three days following recent American attacks on Qeshm Island. While Bahraini air defences intercepted the drones, the Revolutionary Guards separately claimed that two ships hit mines in the Strait of Hormuz, sinking a supertanker carrying two million barrels of crude despite US assurances that shipping lanes had been cleared. In Tehran, foreign ministry spokesperson Esmaeil Baghaei said Iran was “waiting for no one” after Donald Trump stated last week that Washington was no longer seeking a deal—even though Vice-President JD Vance, who blamed the Republican midterm defeat on the nine-month war that began with US-Israeli strikes in February, had sought direct peace talks before Trump told him to focus on investigating Democrat-run cities. (Guardian)

At the southern entrance to the Red Sea, Saudi-backed Yemeni government forces withdrew on Sunday from Dhubab Airport and positions overlooking the Bab al-Mandab Strait to defend the interim capital, Aden, leaving the vital shipping gateway under the control of the Iran-aligned Houthi movement. The retreat ends a month-long offensive to hold ground around Perim Island and Mokha after the Houthis’ September advance. Although the Houthis say they will target only Saudi vessels, their hold on the strait squeezes global oil supplies because Riyadh has relied on Red Sea terminals since the Strait of Hormuz was shut, amid fighting that the UN estimates has displaced nearly 330,000 Yemenis. (BBC)

Western envoys defy Moscow threats in battered Kyiv as Starshield limits persist and Estonia shuts Russian border

Volodymyr Zelenskyy warned on Sunday that Russian overnight strikes across eight Ukrainian regions—which injured six people and cut power to 41,000 customers in Chornomorsk near Odesa while hitting substations and railways in Dnipropetrovsk and Zaporizhzhia—were getting through partly because speed caps on SpaceX’s military Starshield terminals remained active along the front line. Calling on Washington and Elon Musk to stand on the “right side of history” after conflicting accounts between Musk and Donald Trump over the 161km/h (100mph) cap following tensions over Ukrainian strikes on Russian oil refineries, Zelenskyy said interceptor drones were still losing links before catching jet-powered Shaheds. Kyiv is preparing a Coalition of the Willing summit with Britain, France, Germany, Poland, and Italy alongside next week’s Ramstein defence talks, while Estonia will close all border crossings with Russia from 1 December after blaming Moscow’s GRU intelligence service for a weekend sabotage attack on an Estonian drone maker. (Guardian, BBC)

In Kyiv, ambassadors from the EU, the US, Britain, Germany, France, Poland, Estonia, Italy, Spain, Sweden, and Denmark have rejected a Russian warning to leave the capital or face “mortal danger”. EU ambassador Katarína Mathernová—whose delegation building had its ninth floor wrecked by drone debris in September—and Danish envoy Thomas Lund-Sørensen, based near the Kyiv orthopaedics institute hit on Friday, dismissed the threats as cognitive warfare as embassies sandbag walls and install corridor beanbags and extra showers during 24-hour water outages. In Lviv, Prince Harry made an unannounced fourth wartime visit to Ukraine on Saturday, meeting amputees and wheelchair rugby players at the Superhumans Center clinic and promising support for Ukraine to host a future Invictus Games after sending 60 athletes to Birmingham next year. (Guardian, Guardian, BBC)

Four years after three dozen Russian billionaires sat in silence across the Kremlin’s Yekaterininsky Hall on the day the invasion began, sections of Russia’s super-rich are privately discussing a political transition after Vladimir Putin. Sidelined politically since the 2003 arrest and ten-year imprisonment of Yukos oil chief Mikhail Khodorkovsky, senior tycoons speaking to Kremlin observers describe a shift in elite sentiment as business leaders weigh how a post-Putin era might restore property protections or revive 1990s-style oligarchic influence. (BBC)

Electoral court challenges test right-wing majority in Brazil’s Senate as Pope Leo XIV demands justice for Amazon defenders

Legal challenges in Brazil’s superior courts could strip three right-wing senators-elect of their seats before the new legislature convenes on 1 February, potentially expanding President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva’s Senate base and influencing the chamber’s presidency race. The Superior Electoral Court (TSE) will judge candidacy appeals against former Lava Jato prosecutor Deltan Dallagnol in Paraná and Marina Candia in Alagoas, while rivals are weighing a Supreme Federal Court (STF) challenge against Lahesio Bonfim in Maranhão; unseating them could install third-placed finishers Alexandre Curi, Renan Calheiros, and Roseana Sarney and aid Senate President Davi Alcolumbre against right-wing challengers Rogério Marinho and Tereza Cristina. Even so, projections show Jair Bolsonaro’s Liberal Party (PL), with 29 senators, will hold nine of the 27 seats—a full third—on the crucial Constitution and Justice Committee (CCJ), forcing four government-aligned parties to combine ten seats to outvote it, while 19 defeated lawmakers on the 40-seat Joint Budget Committee (CMO) are writing a lock into the 2027 Budget Guidelines Law to stop incoming legislators from inheriting their share of R$28.5 billion in individual amendments. (Folha, Folha, Folha)

Senate leaders across party lines have also revived talks on a constitutional amendment ending presidential re-election from 2030, though bloc leaders Eduardo Braga and Dr Hiran rejected extending executive and legislative mandates to five or ten years. Meanwhile, TSE data confirmed that women aged 70 and over in the Northeast supplied 287,000 of the 2.9 million extra runoff votes, driving record-low abstention as turnout among 70-to-79-year-olds rose by 868,000, including 526,000 women. Online, a 19-page pamphlet titled the Minimanual of Informational Insurgency (or Janonismo)—written by Rio psychoanalysts Iago Ribeiro and Lucas Neckel and inspired by Congressman André Janones and Carlos Marighella’s 1969 guerrilla manual—has been translated into 23 languages as a left-wing digital playbook. (Folha, Folha, Folha)

In the Peruvian Amazon, Pope Leo XIV ended his South American tour on Sunday in Pucallpa along the Ucayali River by demanding justice for 57 environmental defenders killed between 2010 and 2024, including Kichwa leader Quinto Inuma Alvarado, while condemning illegal gold mining and impunity over 370 recorded cases of sexual violence against Indigenous Awajún and Wampis schoolgirls. In The Hague, the International Court of Justice will rule on Thursday on the Gambia’s seven-year case accusing Myanmar of genocide against the Rohingya—potentially the first time a state is found guilty under the 1948 Genocide Convention, with implications for the case against Israel—as Donald Trump, Xi Jinping, and Vladimir Putin prepare for a trilateral meeting at Wednesday’s APEC summit in Shenzhen after ASEAN talks in Manila, and Mexico confirmed the death in hospital at 80 of imprisoned Guadalajara Cartel founder Miguel Ángel Félix Gallardo. (Guardian, Guardian, Guardian, BBC)

Economy

Milburn proposes phasing down employer National Insurance for under-25s as Brexit food checks stall hauliers and digital financial abuse hits young women

Alan Milburn’s review of youth economic inactivity will recommend lowering employer National Insurance contributions gradually over five years for workers aged 21 to 24—starting in retail and hospitality—and doubling the threshold at which firms begin paying NI for those staff from £5,000 to £10,000 when fiscal conditions allow. Warning that skills training is pointless without entry-level jobs, the former health secretary stopped short of recommending an immediate blanket cut for 21-to-24-year-olds, which the Resolution Foundation estimates would cost £5.1 billion a year, while Shadow Work and Pensions Secretary Helen Whately urged Labour to reverse its employer NI rise altogether. (BBC)

On trade, EU relations minister Hamish Falconer confirmed on Sunday that Britain will have a “voice in the room” without a vote under the sanitary and phytosanitary (SPS) deal Andy Burnham hopes to sign at Friday’s UK-EU summit, and noted Brussels wants an exit clause covering border costs if a future Conservative or Reform government reverses alignment. The friction of current rules is stark at Eurotunnel’s Calais terminal, where Marks & Spencer lorries carry over 300 pages of certificates and inspectors held a Gibraltar-bound supermarket truck for nine hours across 78 files over a mistyped date on spinach and ricotta cannelloni. Although Defra data shows small and medium-sized exporters suffer most, UK legislation for dynamic alignment means the SPS deal will not take effect before January 2028, when Getlink expects to dismantle parts of its €60 million (£50 million) border facilities. On the high street, Poundland’s US owners released withheld funds under an internal drive dubbed “Operation I Will Survive” to restock stores for Christmas. (BBC, Guardian, Guardian)

In personal finance, a survey by Ipsos UK and Surviving Economic Abuse found that nearly one in five young women and girls aged 16 to 24—almost 700,000 people—have had banking apps, digital wallets, or online credit used as weapons of coercive control by partners. One in eight had banking logins withheld or digital wallets controlled, while 7% had loans or buy-now-pay-later credit taken out in their names without consent, trapping more than a third in relationships and leaving victims with up to £40,000 in debt from unverified applications. Campaigners called for compulsory school lessons on economic abuse and stricter verification by digital lenders, alongside fresh HMRC warnings over Black Friday and tax-return phishing scams. (Guardian, BBC)

Technology

Musk and Silicon Valley rally behind ousted Nvidia chief Jensen Huang as Bessent demands shake-up and ULA’s Vulcan rocket fails

Elon Musk and Silicon Valley executives have joined more than 158,000 signatories on an open letter urging Nvidia’s board to reinstate co-founder Jensen Huang pending an independent investigation into his Thursday resignation, which ended his 33-year tenure after disclosures of extramarital affairs with a subordinate and with suspected Chinese intelligence operative Alice Huang. Backed by major shareholders Brad Gerstner and Gavin Baker and a war room lobbying the White House, Huang has discussed a comeback. US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, however, argued on Sunday that Nvidia needed a “dramatic shake-up” and an outside chief executive, giving the board ten days to share its findings and explain whether chips above the permitted H200 tier had reached China. (Guardian, The Verge)

Singapore-based Megaspeed, Southeast Asia’s largest buyer of Nvidia chips, said on Sunday it had hired external counsel for an internal review and maintained that Alice Huang—unrelated to Jensen Huang and probed by Singapore in February 2025—left her executive role in August 2025 after stepping down as a director in late 2023, though Washington security group C4ADS said she remained practically in control. House China Select Committee chair John Moolenaar confirmed Jensen Huang will testify behind closed doors before three House panels without a subpoena, while Senate Banking chair Tim Scott summoned Huang and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, and House Speaker Mike Johnson backed bipartisan legislation such as the Remote Access Security Act to tighten AI chip export rules. (BBC, The Verge)

In commercial spaceflight, United Launch Alliance suffered the first mission failure in its 20-year history on Sunday when its Vulcan rocket experienced an anomaly ten minutes after liftoff from Florida’s Space Launch Complex 41, destroying 40 Amazon Leo broadband satellites on the opening LV-01 flight of a 38-launch contract meant to rival Starlink. Meanwhile, a study by Edinburgh University’s Childlight institute revealed that tech industry reports of AI-generated child sexual abuse material—driven by deepfake “nudify” extortion apps—surged by more than 8,400% from 4,700 in 2023 to over 400,000 in 2025, with the Internet Watch Foundation recording a further 40% rise in photorealistic AI abuse imagery in the first half of 2026. (The Verge, Guardian)

Apple prepares 2027 Siri AI smart glasses and clinical health study as South Korea exam cheating scandal sparks AI crackdown

Apple is preparing to unveil its first smart glasses—codenamed N50—at next June’s Worldwide Developers Conference ahead of late-2027 shipments, pairing a pared-down visionOS build and a smartwatch-derived chip with its revamped Siri AI assistant across iOS 27 and macOS Golden Gate 27. Slightly thinner than Meta’s second-generation Ray-Bans and lacking augmented-reality displays, the frames include Wi-Fi for untethered audio, high-grade speakers for calls and FaceTime, and a Tic Tac-sized magnetic charger usable while worn. Engineers have also built prototypes of next autumn’s second-generation foldable iPhone Duo with Face ID in the outer display and a remappable Action Button, while testing of the new $299 A19 Apple TV 4K showed tvOS still caps individual apps and games at 2GB of RAM despite doubling system memory to 8GB. (The Verge, 9to5Mac, 9to5Mac, 9to5Mac)

Today Apple also launched the Apple Health AI Usage Study with clinical platform OpenEvidence, recruiting up to 200,000 US adults on an iPhone 15 Pro or newer to rate medical answers drawn from peer-reviewed journals such as The New England Journal of Medicine, with optional HealthKit data kept confidential and excluded from model training. In the UK, draft NICE guidance will expand GP-surgery “sponge on a string” capsule tests for patients with chronic acid reflux to detect Barrett’s oesophagus and oesophageal cancer without hospital endoscopies. (The Verge, BBC)

Concerns over generative AI in exams prompted emergency measures in South Korea after the top scorer in both rounds of the national tax accountant exam—who scored 397.5 out of 400 with one wrong answer across 160 questions—confessed to Seoul police that he photographed papers with a microcamera and received AI answers on a smartphone, leading Prime Minister Han Seong-sook to tighten oversight ahead of Thursday’s national college entrance exam. In England, Shadow Education Secretary Laura Trott pledged that the Conservatives would halt screen-based exams, cap secondary pupils’ classroom screen time at two hours a day, cut AI-vulnerable coursework, and shift £250 million of the £900 million EdTech budget into textbooks after more than 150 clinicians warned of harm to children’s sight, hearing, and speech. (Guardian, BBC)

Rockstar opens the doors on its $1bn Vice City in Grand Theft Auto VI as Rambert brings It’s a Sin to the dancefloor and Hollywood drops trans roles

Ahead of Thursday’s launch of Grand Theft Auto VI on PlayStation 5 and Xbox Series X/S, a hands-on preview at Rockstar North’s Edinburgh headquarters in Holyrood—where co-studio head Rob Nelson and publishing chief Jennifer Kolbe oversaw nearly 5,000 developers across a decade of work estimated at over $1 billion—revealed a more methodical approach to open-world crime across the fictional Florida state of Leonida. Players control fugitives Jason and Lucia, the franchise’s first female co-lead, who start only able to hotwire cheap cars and pick simple locks, requiring reconnaissance of security cameras and safe codes before robbing Vice City boutiques or jewellery stores, while tending to their relationship through dates, texts, and a dedicated hand-holding button in a world featuring 62 bird species to log. The release arrives as Rockstar defends a Glasgow employment tribunal claim brought by 34 dismissed union-organising staff backed by the IWGB, while rival publisher Electronic Arts added a Vice City-coloured “Don’t Delete!” pop-up inside Madden NFL 27 begging players not to uninstall the game to clear 210GB of storage. (Guardian, IGN, IGN)

On the British stage, Rambert released the first trailer today for its dance adaptation of Russell T Davies’s drama It’s a Sin, choreographed by artistic director Benoit Swan Pouffer with scripts by Kaite O’Reilly, executive production by Davies and the Pet Shop Boys, and a score by Roman GianArthur ahead of its 16 February 2027 premiere at Manchester’s Aviva Studios. Turning down musical pitches to capture the 1980s clubbing sanctuary of Manchester’s Canal Street and London alongside the trauma of the early HIV and Aids crisis, Davies and Pouffer contrasted that vitality with a sharp contraction in screen representation documented by Glaad’s Where We Are on TV study. Glaad found transgender characters across scripted US television plunged 79% from 33 last year to seven—all on cancelled or limited series, leaving zero recurring trans characters next season—after a July report found zero trans characters across 225 major-studio films, leaving actors including Trace Lysette, Vico Ortiz, Laverne Cox, and Jack Haven scrambling for work and union healthcare. (Guardian, Guardian)

Two American women who worked in European exile are also receiving fresh scrutiny in cinema and art. As Helen Mirren portrays an ageing Patricia Highsmith in 1995 Switzerland in Anton Corbijn’s film A Talent for Murder—surrounded by pet snails and glasses of cigarette butts—former editors Otto Penzler, Anna von Planta, and the late Gary Fisketjon recalled how the creator of Tom Ripley and Carol paired personal misanthropy and prejudice in her 56 volumes of diaries with a dark psychological precision that sold 40,000 copies a book in Germany even when US publishers dropped her. In Paris, meanwhile, a Musée d’Orsay exhibition transferring to London early next year gathers 80 paintings, pastels, prints, unpublished letters, and a digital reconstruction of her lost 18-metre 1893 Chicago World’s Fair mural Modern Woman to restore Mary Cassatt alongside Edgar Degas, Claude Monet, and Camille Pissarro at the heart of French Impressionism a century after her death. (Guardian, Guardian)
