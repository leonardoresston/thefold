Reform’s vetting promise collides with a year of undercover footage

Reform UK is trying to contain its most serious test of candidate vetting after an ITV investigation recorded councillors and election candidates using racist and misogynistic language and discussing extreme violence. Three councillors have left the party, while Reform says everyone shown in the programme has resigned, been suspended or is under investigation. Alan Feeney, a Solihull councillor and the borough’s deputy mayor, was filmed describing Asian homeowners as parasites and arguing that the non-white share of Britain’s population should be limited to 1%. He has left Reform and remains an independent councillor. Chris Whelan, who appeared in footage from the same campaign, resigned earlier this week; another councillor shown making offensive comments is still in office. (Guardian, BBC)

The recordings were gathered across England and Wales during a year in which undercover reporters joined local campaigns, four byelections and activity around the party’s headquarters. Another councillor was filmed calling for the king to be killed and speaking violently about migrants crossing the Channel. Reform has accused ITV and the production company Hardcash of deception, pointing to the false credentials used to gain access to its office and computer systems. The broadcaster’s methods will be argued over. The immediate political problem is simpler: Nigel Farage has repeatedly promised to remove extremists and bigots, and the party says every prospective candidate undergoes checks. The investigation found people who passed through those checks, won public office and received ceremonial responsibilities. (Guardian, BBC)

Farage has postponed a planned announcement in Essex as further footage is expected, including material recorded inside Reform’s London headquarters. The crisis arrives while the party is stalling in opinion polls and facing separate scrutiny over officials’ discussion of foreign donations and Farage’s declaration of a £5m gift. Reform’s defence is that a small number of unsuitable people can evade even improved procedures. Its opponents will press a wider question about the culture that attracted them and allowed them to advance. The answer will matter far beyond a television broadcast: these are elected representatives exercising local power, and several can continue doing so after leaving the party whose name put them on the ballot. (Guardian)

Ukraine’s Starlink slowdown reaches the drones defending its cities

Ukraine says an unexpected Starlink speed limit is disrupting battlefield missions and the drone interceptors that protect civilians from Russian attack. Connections were cut on Tuesday night for aircraft travelling faster than 100mph, with some crews finding that communication failed at roughly half that speed. Mykhailo Fedorov, Ukraine’s former defence minister, said service had partly returned by Wednesday evening but urged the removal of the restriction. Elon Musk replied that the military Starshield system is owned and controlled by the US government, so its limits are decided by the US military and ultimately the president. Washington has not explained whether it ordered the change. (BBC, Guardian)

The timing has made a technical explanation difficult for Kyiv to accept. Russia launched one of its largest recent attacks as the limit appeared, hitting power and communications facilities and leaving at least 600,000 people without electricity. Ukraine also says western partners failed for a second night to provide warning of incoming ballistic missiles, though Donald Trump and Nato secretary general Mark Rutte deny that intelligence-sharing has been paused. Starlink guides many mid-range drones behind Russian lines and connects fast interceptor drones to the system that brings down jet-powered Shaheds. It is already disabled inside Russia’s recognised borders, so throttling it has little effect on the long-range strikes against oil refineries that have angered Trump. The heavier cost falls on air defence and operations in occupied Ukrainian territory. (BBC, Guardian)

Volodymyr Zelensky used a visit to London to ask for the connection to be restored and described feeling betrayed by the US decision to buy 300,000 tonnes of Russian diesel. Prime minister Andy Burnham promised continued British support and accelerated deliveries of lightweight missiles used against Russian drones. That help addresses one part of the defensive gap. The episode also demonstrates how a commercial satellite network, a military contract and presidential diplomacy can converge inside a single drone flight. Ukrainian commanders are already replanning missions around other technologies, which can cost many times more. Every day of uncertainty reduces the margin available to intercept the faster weapons now reaching homes, hospitals and power plants. (BBC, Guardian)

Brazil approves a two-day weekend as its election tightens

Brazil’s Senate has approved a constitutional amendment ending the standard six-days-on, one-day-off working pattern, handing President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva a major legislative victory 11 days before the presidential runoff. The measure passed both rounds by 75 votes to one after senators adopted a special timetable. It reduces the normal week from 44 hours to 40, requires two weekly rest days, including Sunday where possible, and protects existing pay. The change will be phased in and still needs formal promulgation. Specific rules for platform workers, aviation and other sectors with unusual schedules will follow, leaving employers and unions to negotiate how the shorter week works in practice. (Folha, Meio)

The vote became a rehearsal for the final stretch of the campaign. Lula’s allies presented the amendment as a durable gain for workers and hope to hold the promulgation while he is in Minas Gerais. Flávio Bolsonaro voted in favour after previously calling the proposal opportunistic and supporting an hourly-pay alternative. His PL party freed its senators to vote as they wished, while opposition figures argued that a future government would need payroll-tax relief to protect jobs. Public support made resistance costly: a Datafolha survey in March found 71% backing an end to the six-day schedule. Both campaigns now claim ownership of a change that Lula’s coalition brought through Congress and Bolsonaro joined once approval became unavoidable. (Folha, Meio)

A new Quaest poll shows why neither side could afford to stand apart. Flávio has 46% of total voting intentions and Lula 44%, a statistical tie within the survey’s two-point margin of error; 9% plan to cast a blank or invalid ballot and 1% remain undecided. Among decided voters, 91% say their choice is final. Flávio led the first round by 47.03% to 45.15%, while rejection is almost identical for the two candidates. Lula performs better among people who abstained or rejected all candidates in the first round, giving him a possible route to recover the small deficit. The shorter working week now moves from Congress into campaign advertising, where implementation, business costs and political credit will be tested before voters on 25 October. (Folha)

Britain puts Eritrea’s visas behind its demand for migrant returns

The government has threatened to suspend entry for all Eritrean nationals unless Asmara accepts more citizens who have no right to remain in Britain. From today, Eritrean diplomats will need permission to travel more than 60km outside London, and fast-track visas for VIPs have been withdrawn. The Home Office says Eritrea abruptly ended cooperation on returns earlier this year. Eritreans formed the largest national group among people arriving in small boats in the 12 months to June, accounting for 18% of the total, which makes the dispute central to ministers’ wider effort to increase removals. (BBC)

Visa pressure has worked elsewhere: Angola and Namibia agreed changes before threatened restrictions took effect, and measures against the Democratic Republic of the Congo were later lifted after cooperation improved. More than 20,400 people have crossed the Channel in small boats so far this year, 40% fewer than at the same point in 2025. Returns and deportations rose 8% to more than 40,600 in the year to June, with fewer than 6,000 involving someone convicted of a criminal offence. Those figures leave the policy spanning two distinct groups that political language often merges: foreign offenders and people whose immigration claims have failed. The test is whether blanket pressure on ordinary visa applicants changes a government’s behaviour without adding another obstacle for families already separated across borders. (BBC)

Economy

Hospitality offers a first rung to 100,000 young people

Pubs, restaurants and hotels will offer up to 100,000 jobs to young people outside work or education over the next three years under a programme backed by the Department for Work and Pensions. Wagamama, Nando’s, Burger King, Premier Inn, Fuller’s and Mitchells & Butlers are among the employers providing placements of up to four weeks for people aged 18 to 24, followed by a route into permanent work. The programme follows a similar retail partnership with Marks & Spencer, Asda, Pets at Home and John Lewis. It arrives before Alan Milburn’s review of youth unemployment, which warns that the number of young people classed as Neet could reach 1.25 million within five years without intervention. (Guardian)

The placement is short, so the measure will be judged by how many participants gain stable hours, training and progression after it ends. Education secretary Lucy Powell is also reviewing school exclusions after research linked persistent absence to a nearly fourfold increase in the risk of leaving education without a job. Milburn has signalled that his recommendations will combine benefit reform with stronger job support, apprenticeships and a national readiness programme. That broader approach recognises a long path into unemployment that often begins before someone reaches a Jobcentre. Hospitality can supply the first rung quickly; schools, employers and public services still have to make sure the ladder continues. (Guardian)

American inflation rises to 3.7% as fuel carries the shock

US consumer-price inflation accelerated from 3.4% to 3.7% in September, exceeding forecasts in the final release before next month’s midterm elections. Vehicle fuel prices jumped 9.1% in a single month and 34.2% over the year, accounting for more than half of the latest increase. Fuel oil used for heating is 74.2% dearer than a year ago, while broader energy prices are up 20.4%. Disrupted Middle Eastern production and transport during the US-Israel war with Iran have pushed crude above $100 a barrel, turning a foreign-policy crisis into a visible number on filling-station signs and household bills. (BBC)

Underlying inflation is steadier: food prices were flat during September and 2.5% higher over the year, while the core measure excluding food and energy stood at 2.6%. The Federal Reserve must now decide how far the energy shock will travel into transport, manufacturing and services. Tariffs add a separate pressure. Research by the New York Fed found that overseas exporters absorbed little of the new levies and US businesses carried close to 90% of the cost; each percentage-point rise in average tariffs lifted consumer-goods prices by almost 0.25% after a year. Social Security benefits will rise 3.6% in 2027, giving about 72 million recipients an average extra $75 a month. That adjustment protects some purchasing power after prices have risen, while doing little to settle an election dominated by the cost of living. (BBC)

OpenAI’s rewards and risks grow in public together

OpenAI’s 110 London employees received an average of £2.3m each in salary and equity during 2025, according to accounts for its Westminster-based British company. Together they shared £87m in pay and £168m in shares, more than doubling the previous year’s average package. The distribution is uneven, with the largest awards aimed at frontier-model researchers during an intense recruitment contest with Meta and other rivals. London has become OpenAI’s biggest research centre outside the US, drawing on machine-learning talent from British universities and competing directly with Google DeepMind. Its UK turnover more than tripled to £293m and produced a £20.3m profit. (Guardian)

On the same day, Iowa, Kansas, Nebraska, West Virginia, Arkansas, Arizona, Idaho and Montana filed separate lawsuits accusing the company of putting profit ahead of child safety and misleading customers about ChatGPT’s risks. The complaints cite cases brought by families who allege the chatbot contributed to deaths, and say vulnerable users can encounter dangerous medical advice, reinforcement of paranoia and unhealthy emotional dependence. The states also accuse OpenAI of collecting data from minors and failing to keep under-age users away. They seek civil penalties, changes to the company’s public claims and, in some cases, deletion of data gathered unlawfully. (The Verge)

OpenAI says it has built age-specific protections, including parental controls, break reminders and models tuned for younger users, while acknowledging that no system is perfect. The litigation will examine whether those safeguards match the way the product was marketed and used. The accounts and lawsuits describe the same expansion from different directions: enormous rewards for the people pushing model capability forward, alongside a growing legal bill for harms that state authorities say were foreseeable. A planned move to King’s Cross and a possible $1tn stock-market valuation will keep the financial scale visible. The harder measure is whether safety systems mature as quickly as the product and the compensation attached to it. (Guardian, The Verge)

Sally Rooney’s books stop moving through Britain’s supply chain

Faber will no longer supply Sally Rooney’s books in the UK because it cannot pass British earnings to the author without risking an offence under terrorism law. Rooney said last year that she intended to use income from her work to support Palestine Action, which was proscribed in Britain in July 2025 but remains legal in the Republic of Ireland. Section 15 of the Terrorism Act can punish someone who provides money knowing or suspecting it may be used for terrorism, with a maximum sentence of 14 years. Rooney and Faber agreed that continuing to fulfil British publishing contracts could create that exposure. Ebook editions have already been removed; shops can sell physical copies they hold, but they cannot order more through the publisher. (Guardian, BBC)

The practical effect is unusually wide. Rooney has sold 2.3 million books in Britain and more than six million worldwide; Normal People, Conversations With Friends, Beautiful World, Where Are You and Intermezzo will all disappear from ordinary replenishment. She previously cancelled a British visit because she feared arrest, and payments connected to BBC adaptations have also become legally sensitive. More than 3,700 people are believed to have been arrested over alleged support for Palestine Action, with about 2,500 charged; trials await a Supreme Court challenge to the ban next month. The withdrawal turns that pending judgment into a question readers can see on shelves. It also gives publishers, broadcasters and other intermediaries a preview of the decisions they may face whenever an artist’s declared use of earnings meets a proscribed organisation. (Guardian, BBC)

Ocarina of Time rebuilds its old lessons for modern hands

Nintendo’s Switch 2 remake of The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time changes far more than its resolution. Link can sprint, jump on command, turn a free camera and aim with motion controls; the game runs at 60 frames a second with an orchestrated score and newly detailed environments. Those abilities reshape rooms that once taught players how to handle one of gaming’s first great 3D adventures. A boulder maze built around recentering the Nintendo 64 camera now teaches jumping. A Forest Temple fight replaces an awkward bottomless hole with acid, preserving the constraint on Link while giving the skeleton enemies a reason to cross it. Familiar puzzles, dialogue and cutscenes have been revised throughout. (IGN, Guardian)

The remake keeps loading screens between Kokiri Forest, Hyrule Field and Castle Town and leaves the compact geography intact. Its visual language makes those limits feel deliberate, using pools of light, fog and more theatrical combat to present Hyrule as a series of stage sets. That approach suits a game whose original lock-on fighting system was inspired by Japanese period drama and whose story treats Link as a child stepping into an old legend. His glossy, doll-like new face has divided early viewers, and the open field that felt enormous in 1998 now looks small beside Breath of the Wild. The hands-on previews suggest Nintendo understands that tension. It is rebuilding the logic around a classic instead of enlarging it until its identity disappears. The remake arrives on 5 November. (IGN, Guardian)

Sports

PSG absorb City’s rebellion while Liverpool find their new finisher

Paris Saint-Germain beat Manchester City 4-2 at the Etihad after producing a first half that showed why a third consecutive European title has entered the conversation. Désiré Doué cut inside to score from distance, then crossed for Ousmane Dembélé 75 seconds later. Vitinha added a third before the interval as PSG’s movement repeatedly opened City’s defence. Maghnes Akliouche made it 4-1 after the break with a shot Gianluigi Donnarumma appeared to save before the ball dropped behind him and crossed the line. The cushion proved essential. Erling Haaland scored twice, City hit the woodwork and forced a goal-line clearance, and their 27 shots produced a second half fierce enough to make an improbable comeback briefly feel available. (Guardian, BBC)

Liverpool’s 6-3 win at Lask supplied a different kind of release. Bradley Barcola scored the first goals of his Liverpool career and completed his first professional hat-trick after six appearances without a goal or assist. Ronald Araújo, Jeremie Frimpong and Lewis Koumas added the others, with Koumas replacing Steven Gerrard as Liverpool’s youngest player to score and assist in a Champions League game. Florian Wirtz also supplied an assist during a performance that gave two expensive summer signings room to settle. Lask scored three after the contest had moved beyond them, a reminder that Liverpool’s defensive control did not match the ease of their attacking work. (Guardian, BBC)

Elsewhere, José Mourinho’s Real Madrid came back from 3-1 down to win 4-3 on his return to Roma, scoring three times late in the match. Aston Villa recorded the biggest European victory in their history, beating Fenerbahce 6-0 with two goals each from Nicolas Jackson and Ross Barkley. The evening’s scale was set by attacks: PSG’s established champions cut through City, Liverpool’s new forwards found their timing, and Madrid turned a defeat into seven-goal theatre. City’s response still offered Enzo Maresca something useful. His half-time changes created chances against Europe’s most accomplished side, even if the damage accumulated before them was too deep to repair. (Guardian, Guardian)
