LEAD STORY

The handover became a test of control

Friday's strongest thread was political succession under operational stress. Keir Starmer used his first BBC interview since resigning to describe the decision to quit as intensely personal and to warn Andy Burnham that a prime minister cannot simply spend less time on diplomacy. Burnham, already expected to enter Downing Street this month, ruled out an early election and promised to stay within the 2024 Labour manifesto, while Guardian coverage put scrutiny on his more controlled media style. The handover story is therefore not only who gets the job; it is whether a new communicator can inherit the same constraints without pretending they have vanished. (BBC, The Guardian, BBC, The Guardian)

The same problem of control ran through the wider feeds. Parents were warned that ordinary images of children can be turned into AI abuse material, a freed Rochdale grooming gang leader was reported to have been judged high risk to children in 2023, and three men were cleared of murdering journalist Lyra McKee, leaving her family saying the justice system had failed. Abroad, rescuers and families in Venezuela were still navigating the post-quake disaster, Polish leaders warned of difficult months ahead over Russia, and Monaco police hunted a disguised suspect after a parcel bomb attack. In Brazil, Moraes extended Bolsonaro's house arrest and Datafolha showed the right again ahead of the left. Technology added its own version of risk: AI medical claims, AI drug-development ambitions, event surveillance and Apple leak litigation all turned trust into something that now has to be proved. (BBC, The Guardian, The Guardian, The Guardian, BBC, BBC, Folha Poder, Folha Poder, The Verge, The Verge)

NEWS

Starmer's exit interview set Burnham's first boundary

Starmer's warning on diplomacy mattered because it pushed against Burnham's domestic-first political pitch before he has taken office. Burnham also said he would not call an early election and would govern from Labour's 2024 manifesto, keeping the transition inside the existing parliamentary mandate. Guardian analysis added the immediate style question: Burnham is avoiding some traditional Westminster formats, including open question sessions after set-piece speeches. The next premiership is being framed before day one around the same three tests: foreign policy, fiscal credibility and scrutiny. (The Guardian, BBC, BBC, The Guardian)

Child protection moved from known offenders to everyday images

The NCA and child-safety groups warned parents that public images of children can be scraped and converted into abuse material using AI tools. That made the risk feel less like a hidden corner of the internet and more like a layer added to ordinary family sharing. The Guardian's separate report on Shabir Ahmed, the freed Rochdale grooming gang leader, sharpened the institutional side: a man released after failed parole attempts had previously been judged to pose a high risk to children. One story is new technology; the other is old safeguarding. Both ask whether protection systems can move before harm becomes evidence. (BBC, The Guardian, The Guardian)

Lyra McKee's family left court without the answer they wanted

Three men were found not guilty of murdering journalist Lyra McKee, who was shot while observing rioting in Derry in 2019. Her family said the verdict meant the justice system had failed her. The legal result is clear, but the public consequence is not tidy: a killing that became a symbol of dissident republican violence has ended in acquittals, leaving grief, politics and confidence in prosecution all unresolved. (The Guardian, BBC)

Disasters and security alerts kept stretching state capacity

In Venezuela, families were left identifying earthquake victims at improvised morgues while international rescuers described the affected coastal zone as a disaster landscape of rubble, heat and exhaustion. Monaco police were searching for a Ukrainian woman suspected of disguising herself before a parcel bomb attack that wounded a sanctioned Ukrainian-born oligarch. Poland's prime minister Donald Tusk warned of critical months ahead after reports of Russian planning. These were separate crises, but each put pressure on basic state functions: identify the dead, find the suspect, prepare the public. (The Guardian, BBC, BBC, BBC)

Brazil's campaign calendar turned legal and ideological at once

Alexandre de Moraes extended Jair Bolsonaro's house arrest indefinitely and ordered the delivery of weapons, while also sending Bolsonaro's Saudi jewels to the Receita Federal so the items can become Union property. Lula used the final edge of the electoral deadline for a burst of inauguration events, and the PT allocated R$126m from the electoral fund to his campaign. Datafolha then supplied the broader map: the right again sits ahead of the left nationally, women skew more left than men, and evangelicals are more clearly on the right than Catholics. The campaign is becoming a fight over courts, money, calendar access and identity at the same time. (Folha Poder, Folha Poder, Folha Poder, Folha Poder, Folha Poder, Folha Poder, Folha Poder)

British firms made control a balance-sheet story

Gymshark founder Ben Francis is in talks to buy back part of the stake he sold to General Atlantic, a move that would increase his control of the sportswear brand after the private-equity deal that helped create a £1.25bn company. Starling Bank moved in the opposite emotional direction, telling staff it would cut 130 jobs while increasing investment in AI to reduce costs and remove duplicate roles. Together they show a founder and a fintech both trying to simplify ownership and operations before markets force the issue for them. (The Guardian, The Guardian)

Travel and infrastructure priced disruption into ordinary choices

UK hotels and holiday parks saw a jump in summer bookings as Britons reacted to flight disruption, higher fares and EU border delays by staying closer to home. In London, the exit of Zipcar left the city with only 330 car-club vehicles and an 89% fall in available shared cars, pushing former users toward buying or leasing. Canada's pipeline deal added the larger version of the same economics: more than C$150bn in promised investment to make a contested energy project politically workable. The common cost is friction. When systems fail, households and governments both start paying to route around them. (The Guardian, The Guardian, The Guardian)

AI's medical claims moved faster than the evidence

Midjourney released a behind-the-scenes look at its proposed dunk-tank ultrasound scanner, but The Verge noted that the company still had not shown much proof that the system works. Anthropic announced Claude Science, an AI workbench for researchers, and framed the launch around drug development and scientific tooling. The important technology story is not that AI wants to enter medicine; that was already obvious. It is that companies are now presenting medical and scientific ambition as platform strategy before regulators, clinicians and patients have a settled way to evaluate the claims. (The Verge, The Verge)

A rare Declaration copy turned independence into an archive story

A volunteer at the National Archives in Kew found a rare copy of the US Declaration of Independence, one of 11 surviving Exeter printings and the only one known outside the United States. The timing made it irresistible: as the US entered its 250th birthday weekend, the object surfaced in a British archive because it had been taken from an American privateer ship. Culture sometimes turns history into ceremony; this did the reverse, turning ceremony back into paper, capture and custody. (The Guardian)
