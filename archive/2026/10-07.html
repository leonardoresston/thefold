Brazil’s runoff becomes a contest of coalitions

Brazil’s presidential runoff entered its first full day with the advantage moving from arithmetic to organisation. Flávio Bolsonaro began converting a 1.87-point first-round lead into a wider political front, collecting the formal support of the União Brasil–PP federation and Podemos while opening talks with Republicanos. Ronaldo Caiado, the defeated PSD candidate and re-elected governor of Goiás, also appeared with Bolsonaro and endorsed him. Eduardo Leite, the PSD governor of Rio Grande do Sul, is leaning towards neutrality, denying Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva an ally in a state where the president needs to recover ground. The final ballot is on 25 October. With 56.1 million votes already behind him and much of the organised right falling into place, Bolsonaro’s task is to keep his coalition disciplined for another two and a half weeks. (Folha, Folha)

Lula is rebuilding his campaign around the voters who did not feel spoken to in the first round. A record 21.07% of the electorate abstained, and the president finished with 45.16% of valid votes after losing 3.4 million votes from his 2022 first-round total. His team plans to warn that a Bolsonaro government could weaken social programmes, while trade-union leaders are preparing an effort aimed at workers who chose another candidate. Guilherme Boulos will travel through cities in the South and South-east, the regions where Lula’s deficit is most urgent. The deeper difficulty is economic and emotional. Lower-middle-income families have endured high food and household costs and often see a government describing its legacy rather than their present pressure. Lula needs to restore that connection without making the people who stayed home the villains of the result. (Meio, Folha, Folha)

The congressional result gives the presidential contest an unusually concrete second track. Bolsonaro has presented the new conservative majority as a mandate for constitutional change and for making it easier to impeach supreme court justices. The PL grew to 121 seats in the Chamber of Deputies, although União Brasil, PP and Republicanos still hold enough votes to decide whether ambitious reforms pass. Those parties lost ground to the ideological right and gained leverage at the same time: either president would need their cooperation from January. For Bolsonaro, their support connects his campaign to the machinery of government. For Lula, it makes the warning about institutions more immediate, while also showing the constraints he would face if re-elected. The runoff will choose a president whose room to govern has already been defined by a legislature further to the right. (Folha, Folha)

Voters should finally see the two candidates confront each other directly. Bolsonaro has committed to the second-round debates after skipping the first-round encounters, and Lula has also indicated that he will attend. Their closeness leaves little space for a cautious campaign. Bolsonaro must explain how a programme built around constitutional change, security and the family name would work in office. Lula must make a fourth-term proposition sound like an answer to 2026 rather than a defence of earlier achievements. The first round showed a country divided by less than two points and a Congress with a clearer direction than the presidency. The debates can now expose whether either candidate has a plan for governing across that divide. (Folha, Meio)

France’s school revolt becomes a national crisis

More than 250,000 pupils, teachers and parents marched across France on Tuesday, turning a protest over daily school conditions into the government’s largest domestic crisis in months. The movement began in the northern suburbs of Paris more than a fortnight ago and has spread through Marseille, Lyon, Rennes and scores of other cities. Students are demanding reliable cover for absent teachers, repairs to ageing buildings and relief from days that can run from 8am until 6pm. About 1,800 of France’s 3,700 high schools were blockaded or closed. Riot police used teargas, water cannon and baton charges as some protesters burned bins and threw projectiles; nearly 500 people were arrested during the day. (Guardian, BBC)

The toll accumulated before Tuesday was already severe. Government figures counted 215 injured teenagers, 85 injured school staff and 715 injured police officers, while individual cases include a pupil who lost a hand, one who lost the use of an eye and another whose jaw was badly injured. Prime minister Sébastien Lecornu has ordered ministers to address the practical complaints, and the interior ministry has opened investigations into allegations of police brutality. The dispute arrives seven months before a presidential election and during an argument over debt, weak growth and public spending. France’s schools have supplied the language of the protest—missing adults, crumbling rooms, unequal chances—and the state’s response will help decide whether the movement returns to classrooms or expands beyond them. (Guardian)

Ebola reaches Kenya as the outbreak accelerates

Kenya has recorded its first Ebola case and death as an outbreak centred in the Democratic Republic of the Congo pushes across borders. The patient, a Kenyan man who had lived in the DRC for seven years, became ill there, travelled overland to Kampala and flew to Nairobi on 3 October. He was isolated on arrival at hospital, tested positive and died on Monday night. Officials have identified 28 contacts among relatives and health workers and are looking for another 23 passengers and four crew members from his flight. No second infection has yet been confirmed. Uganda contained imported cases earlier in the year and was declared Ebola-free in August, showing both the reach of regional travel and the value of rapid tracing. (Guardian)

The larger emergency remains in the DRC, where 4,082 people had died among 8,463 confirmed cases by 2 October. It is the country’s seventeenth Ebola outbreak and involves the Bundibugyo strain, for which there is no approved vaccine or specific treatment. The virus has spread from Ituri to seven provinces; North Kivu now accounts for 40% of new cases and has only four treatment centres for a health zone of about two million people. Armed conflict, attacks on health workers, public mistrust and cuts to international aid all slow the response. Kenya can concentrate resources around a single chain of transmission. The DRC is trying to contain several chains at once across insecure territory, and every missed case creates another route towards a crowded city or a national border. (Guardian)

A photographer is beaten during the West Bank olive harvest

Armed Israeli settlers beat and robbed Agence France-Presse photographer Jaafar Ashtiyeh while he covered an attack near the village of Aqraba in the occupied West Bank. Ashtiyeh, 58, was wearing press identification and trying to enter a marked car when two men struck him repeatedly with rifle butts. He suffered injuries to his head and torso and remains under observation in hospital in Nablus. A second photographer was threatened at gunpoint, and a Palestinian olive picker was shot in the foot. About a dozen Israeli soldiers were visible roughly 500 metres away and did not intervene, according to witnesses. AFP and the Foreign Press Association have demanded an investigation; the Israeli military had not responded by publication. (Guardian)

The assault sits inside a recurrent struggle over the olive harvest, a crucial source of income and a cultural anchor for Palestinian communities. Settler attacks, theft and vandalism have intensified in recent months, and accountability is rare. Roughly 500,000 Israeli settlers live among three million Palestinians in the West Bank, territory captured by Israel in 1967; the settlements are illegal under international law. Media groups also describe increasing obstruction and intimidation of journalists. Nineteen have been detained in the West Bank during the three years since the Hamas-led attacks of 7 October 2023. The violence near Aqraba harmed the people harvesting, the person documenting it and the public record that his camera was there to preserve. (Guardian)

Economy

Britain prepares another layer of winter energy support

Chancellor John Healey is preparing to spend more than £1 billion on energy support in this month’s budget as forecasts point to another sharp rise in January. The leading option would add about £100 to the £150 warm home discount received by households on certain benefits, with taxpayers funding the increase. The intervention follows estimates that the Iran war could lift the energy price cap by as much as £442, wiping out the benefit of the electricity VAT cut announced by prime minister Andy Burnham. Healey is also trying to find £4.7 billion for defence and rebuild a fiscal buffer diminished by higher borrowing costs. A targeted discount is cheaper than removing levies from every bill, though it leaves many households exposed to most of the increase. (Guardian)

Officials are developing longer-term changes that would alter the price itself. A social tariff could charge lower unit rates to poorer households, but it would require tax, Treasury and supplier data to work together. A rising-block tariff would price a protected amount of essential energy cheaply and increase the rate above that level. Energy secretary Miatta Fahnbulleh has proposed moving all green and efficiency levies into general taxation, cutting average bills by up to £120 at a cost of as much as £3.2 billion. Each choice distributes the burden differently: through progressive taxation, a targeted benefit or charges linked to use. The immediate budget measure can cushion January. A durable system needs to respond before every international shock becomes a fresh emergency package. (Guardian)

Paramount and Warner Bros become one $111bn media empire

Paramount has completed its $111 billion acquisition of Warner Bros Discovery after a bidding war with Netflix and months of litigation. The combined business is called Skydance and puts Paramount and Warner Bros studios, HBO Max, CBS News and CNN under the same parent. David Ellison will lead it with former Mattel chief Ynon Kreiz as co-executive. Two of Hollywood’s five surviving legacy studios have now become one, concentrating a huge library of films and television alongside streaming, broadcast networks and two global newsrooms. For audiences, the first changes are likely to emerge through release schedules, bundled subscriptions and the decisions about which overlapping services and production teams the company keeps. (Guardian, BBC)

California, New York and ten other states challenged the merger on competition grounds before reaching a settlement last month. Skydance must release at least 30 films and more than 180 television programmes a year, and CBS News and CNN must retain independent editorial boards under the supervision of a trustee. Those conditions acknowledge the two risks created by scale: fewer stories reaching screens and one owner gaining influence over a larger share of the news. CNN chief Mark Thompson will remain in place, while Ellison has already reshaped CBS News since acquiring Paramount last year. The agreement has preserved output targets and formal newsroom protections. Their force will depend on enforcement after the attention surrounding the deal fades and the difficult work of integrating two businesses begins. (Guardian)

Google’s computing expansion meets two kinds of limit

Finland has ordered Google to suspend work at two planned data centres after the company cleared land before completing the required environmental assessments. At Muhos, a Google representative felled 330 hectares of forest; nearly 200 hectares were logged at Kajaani. The sites form part of a €13 billion programme across northern Finland. Regulators say tree removal, stripped topsoil and new access roads changed the environment before the assessment process could measure those effects. Google has accepted that it fell short of its standards and says it will follow the authority’s direction, including biodiversity and planting work. The halt captures the physical speed of the AI build-out: demand for computing capacity can move faster than small municipalities, environmental review and the landscapes asked to host it. (Guardian)

In the United States, the same expansion is being measured in electricity. Google has signed a 20-year agreement with nuclear operator Constellation to upgrade six existing power stations. Constellation expects more than $4.3 billion of investment to add a combined 890 megawatts over five years, enough capacity to serve roughly 800,000 homes. The arrangement supplies predictable revenue for equipment improvements and gives Google a route to more constant low-carbon power for data centres that run around the clock. Together, the Finnish stop order and the American energy contract reveal the size of the bargain behind cloud services and generative AI. New capacity demands land, transmission, cooling and generation long before a user types a prompt. The industry’s growth will be judged as much by those local systems as by the performance of the models they support. (Verge, Guardian)

Apple’s home strategy starts with hardware made by LG

Apple is working with LG on a family of smart-home products that could include a video doorbell, smart lock, thermostat, indoor and outdoor cameras and a floodlight camera. The devices are expected to carry LG’s brand and connect tightly to Apple’s long-rumoured home hub, which may appear at an event on 13 October. Support for Thread and Matter would give several of them a common language with products outside the Apple ecosystem. The collaboration gives Apple a faster route to a complete range without designing every appliance itself, while LG gains access to customers who already use iPhones, Apple TVs and HomePods as the centre of their homes. (Verge, 9to5Mac, MacMagazine)

The reported range suggests a reset larger than another HomePod. Locks, cameras and heating controls require long support lives, clear privacy rules and reliability when the internet or a subscription fails. Matter can simplify setup across brands, though a shared standard does not decide where video is stored, how household access is granted or which features remain free. Apple’s advantage is an installed network of phones, watches and speakers that can identify people and deliver alerts. Its challenge is trust at the front door and inside private rooms. A partnership can fill the hardware catalogue quickly; the experience will still be judged by how calmly all those devices behave after the launch event is over. (Verge, 9to5Mac)

The people who make Dungeons & Dragons organise around AI

Workers on Dungeons & Dragons, Magic: The Gathering and Duel Masters are joining the Communications Workers of America unit formed by the Magic Arena team earlier this year. The expanded Wizards of the Coast union has asked Hasbro to recognise it voluntarily by 13 October. Its demands cover job security, fair pay, remote work and creative ownership, with a particularly direct call for an enforceable policy on generative AI. Employees argue that management has pushed large language models into their work without agreement from the teams responsible for building the games. The dispute places AI policy inside collective bargaining, where questions about efficiency become terms of employment and the people producing the material have a formal voice. (IGN)

The union is also challenging a company rule that can claim ownership over intellectual property employees create in their own time, even when it is unrelated to Wizards’ worlds. That concern connects naturally to generative AI: both disputes ask who controls creative work, who benefits from it and what consent means when a large company supplies the tools and contracts. The Arena group won its union election after Wizards declined voluntary recognition, so the larger unit may again need a vote supervised by the US labour board. Hasbro owns some of gaming’s most valuable shared universes. The people expanding those universes are using collective action to define the conditions under which human work remains theirs, on the job and after hours. (IGN)

Eva Marie Saint leaves a career built on quiet force

Eva Marie Saint has died at her Los Angeles home aged 102, closing one of the last careers still connected to Hollywood’s postwar golden age. Her film debut came opposite Marlon Brando in Elia Kazan’s On the Waterfront, and it brought her the 1955 Academy Award for supporting actress. Four years later she played Eve Kendall in Alfred Hitchcock’s North by Northwest, combining composure, wit and danger in a role that could easily have been overwhelmed by the machinery of the thriller around it. Saint arrived from radio and live television, where she had learned to make small gestures register. That restraint became her signature on the cinema screen and allowed her characters to carry strength without announcing it. (Guardian)

Her six-decade film career included A Hatful of Rain, Exodus, Grand Prix and Raintree County, alongside repeated returns to television. Later audiences knew her as Martha Kent in Superman Returns, released in 2006, and she continued to appear on screen into her nineties. Longevity can flatten an actor’s work into a list of famous titles, but Saint’s importance lies in the precision within them: Edie Doyle’s moral clarity in On the Waterfront, Eve Kendall’s divided loyalties and the ability to suggest a life continuing beyond the edge of the scene. She outlived most of her collaborators and never became a relic of their era. The performances stayed alert, modern and entirely her own. (Guardian)
