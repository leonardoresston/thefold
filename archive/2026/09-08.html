Britain turns its criticism of Israeli settlements into a trade ban

Ed Miliband will set out a broad reset of Britain’s relationship with Israel today, beginning with a ban on goods and some services connected to illegal Israeli settlements. The foreign secretary plans to place the measure inside a firmer defence of Palestinian rights, international law and a two-state solution. Financing and advertising settlements are expected to fall within its reach, moving British policy beyond labelling guidance and the removal of preferential tariffs. Some elements may require primary legislation, so the announcement begins a parliamentary process as well as an immediate diplomatic confrontation. (Guardian)

The legal argument starts with territory occupied by Israel since the 1967 war. Britain regards civilian settlements there as unlawful, while the International Court of Justice advised governments in 2024 that they must avoid assisting the occupation. The E1 project east of Jerusalem has added urgency: building there from November could divide the occupied West Bank and make a viable Palestinian state harder to assemble. Israeli military officials have themselves warned that some new northern outposts lack basic security infrastructure. The policy is designed to raise the economic cost of expansion before more facts are fixed on the ground. (Guardian, BBC)

The package remains bounded. It is not expected to impose sanctions over restrictions on aid entering Gaza, and Miliband is unlikely to describe the destruction there as genocide. Britain may still join proceedings brought by South Africa at the ICJ over compliance with earlier orders. Total UK trade in goods and services with Israel was about £6bn last year, leaving the wider relationship exposed if Benjamin Netanyahu’s government retaliates. Israel has already threatened consequences over Miliband’s claim that unnecessary military controls are holding back aid, a charge its foreign ministry rejects. (Guardian)

Public opinion gives the government room to act. An Opinium survey found 46% of British adults support a settlement trade ban and 18% oppose it; among people who expressed a view, support approaches three quarters. Prime minister Andy Burnham briefed Donald Trump and Gulf allies on Monday, conscious that both Washington and Israel will test how far London is prepared to go. The practical questions now matter most: how officials identify settlement-linked trade, how quickly legislation moves and whether enforcement reaches services as firmly as physical goods. A reset becomes durable when companies can tell exactly what the law requires and ministers are willing to uphold it under pressure. (Guardian)

News

Portsmouth exposes a fast-moving form of far-right disorder

Police used batons and synthetic pepper spray after about 200 masked activists gathered at Portsmouth on Sunday night and tried to stop people seeking asylum from leaving the port. Officers were pushed from motorbikes, bricks were thrown and several were injured; one had completed training only three days earlier. Hampshire and Isle of Wight constabulary is investigating possible assaults and criminal damage. The confrontation followed a road blockade at Dover on Saturday organised through the same online network, with black clothing and balaclavas giving both events a deliberate uniformity. (Guardian, BBC)

The immediate trigger was the arrival of roughly 120 people in a single dinghy after a ten-hour journey from Normandy. It landed farther west than the usual Channel routes, raising concern that smugglers are testing a wider stretch of the south coast. Eight boats carrying 625 people reached Britain on Sunday. The policing failure now sits alongside the migration challenge: ministers and MPs want to know how an organised group closed Britain’s busiest port one day and mobilised at another site the next with so little warning. Future demonstrations could face a ban on face coverings, but intelligence sharing will determine whether officers arrive before a blockade forms. (Guardian, BBC)

Germany’s far right moves within reach of regional power

Alternative für Deutschland won nearly 44% in Saxony-Anhalt, routing Friedrich Merz’s Christian Democrats and leaving the far-right party close to forming Germany’s first state government of its kind since the Nazi era. AfD candidate Ulrich Siegmund fell short of an outright majority, so the arithmetic of the new parliament and the choices of other parties will decide whether victory becomes power. Exit polling showed how deeply the result was tied to anger with the political establishment and the economy: one survey put approval of Merz’s performance at 9%. (Guardian)

Merz has been chancellor for only 16 months and once promised to halve AfD support. He now faces pressure inside the CDU, another two state elections this month and a coalition partner seeking softer changes to pensions and healthcare. He says his economic reform programme and support for Ukraine will continue, while Germany’s institutions and constitutional protections will hold if AfD takes office in Saxony-Anhalt. That assurance will be measured against decisions in the coming weeks. A party classified as extreme right has converted grievance in the former east into a landslide, and every mainstream response risks either strengthening its claim to be excluded or normalising its programme. (Guardian)

Russia and North Korea open a more useful border

The first road bridge between Russia and North Korea has opened across the Tumen River, joining Khasan in the Russian far east to Tumangang along the countries’ short, remote border. The one-kilometre crossing adds a dependable route for lorries beside an existing railway bridge and regular flights between Vladivostok and Pyongyang. Moscow presents it as infrastructure for trade, tourism, jobs and scientific cooperation; convoys travelled from both sides after Monday’s ceremony. The bridge is named for Yakov Novichenko, a Soviet officer credited with saving North Korea’s founder Kim Il-sung from an assassination attempt. (Guardian)

Its strategic value is difficult to separate from the war in Ukraine. North Korea has sent thousands of troops to Russia’s Kursk region and supplied ammunition and ballistic missiles. South Korean intelligence believes Russia has returned air-defence missiles, electronic-warfare equipment, drones and satellite technology. Ukrainian human-rights investigators warn that the bridge can move troops and equipment with less scrutiny, creating a quiet logistics corridor in both directions. Tourism is growing too, with Russians visiting reopened North Korean beaches and ski resorts. A piece of civilian infrastructure now gives a wartime partnership more capacity, more permanence and fewer transport constraints. (Guardian)

Brazil enters the final month with Lula and Flávio Bolsonaro level

A Quaest poll puts Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva and Flávio Bolsonaro on 41% each in a presidential runoff, a dead heat inside the two-point margin of error. In the first-round test Lula has 36%, Bolsonaro 29% and author Augusto Cury 8%, with the remaining candidates in low single digits. The survey interviewed 2,004 voters from Thursday to Sunday and carries a 95% confidence level. Its stability is striking: Lula led the same runoff 42% to 41% five days earlier and was ahead 43% to 40% in mid-August, both statistical ties. (Folha)

The candidates used Independence Day to display the conflict that will dominate the campaign’s last weeks. Lula appeared in Brasília beside leaders of Congress and the supreme court as members of the crowd demanded an end to the six-day working week. Bolsonaro rallied supporters on Avenida Paulista, defended justice André Mendonça in the court’s internal crisis and tied Lula to Alexandre de Moraes. Each side also carries a personal vulnerability: investigations surrounding Lula’s son Fábio Luís have fed the opposition, while Bolsonaro faces questions over money solicited from former Banco Master chief Daniel Vorcaro for a film about his father. Rejection is almost as closely matched as voting intention, at 55% for Bolsonaro and 53% for Lula. (Folha, Folha, Folha)

The NHS discovers that a data platform also runs on trust

Ministers are considering whether to use a break clause in Palantir’s seven-year, £330m contract to operate the NHS federated data platform. The immediate warning comes from 60,000 additional patients using the national data opt-out between mid-May and mid-July. That choice leaves records available for direct care while removing them from research and planning. Health innovation minister James Frith fears mistrust of the American defence and health technology company could weaken the data needed for the government’s ten-year health plan. NHS trusts have also been told they may choose another platform that meets local needs. (Guardian)

Palantir says trusts using its software have recorded 110,000 extra operations, reduced discharge delays for long-stay patients by 15% and improved the share of people receiving a cancer result within 28 days by 6.8%. NHS England has published similar figures but says they do not establish cause and effect; the statistics watchdog is investigating. The company’s work with the Israeli military and US immigration enforcement has sharpened objections, as did the discovery that its staff could access identifiable patient information. A useful system can still lose public value when people withdraw the information that makes research possible. The contract decision therefore reaches beyond price and performance into the terms on which patients consent to a shared health service. (Guardian)

Economy

Britain’s housing slowdown becomes an annual fall

UK house prices fell 0.4% in the year to August, their first annual decline since November 2023. Lloyds put the average home at £298,468, down £685 or 0.2% from July. Buyers are facing the combined weight of stretched affordability and mortgage rates that have climbed sharply since January: the average two-year fix was 5.63% on Monday and the five-year average 5.68%. Mortgage approvals have slipped to their lowest level since early 2024. Sellers are generally waiting instead of making steep reductions, leaving the market in a quiet standoff. (Guardian)

The national figure hides a widening regional split. Northern Ireland recorded annual growth of 6.9% and Scotland 3.5%, while prices rose 2.7% in north-east England and 2% in the north-west. The south-east fell 1.6% to an average £381,729 and Greater London declined 1.5% to £534,177. Energy-driven inflation and uncertainty over the next move in interest rates are keeping buyers cautious, especially where prices already consume the greatest share of income. A small national fall can therefore feel very different across the country: a pause in one place, a correction in another and continued competition where homes remain cheaper. (Guardian)

Next wins the central argument in a long equal-pay case

Next has overturned the part of a 2024 employment tribunal ruling that required shop staff to receive the same basic hourly pay as warehouse workers doing work of equal value. The retailer persuaded the employment appeal tribunal that recruitment and retention pressures allowed it to pay the market rate for warehouse labour without raising basic store pay to match. The case began with 3,540 claimants after a six-year fight; more than 6,400 workers are now involved. Women held almost 78% of retail sales jobs during the period examined, while men made up about 53% of warehouse staff, where hourly pay could be 38p to £3.13 higher. (Guardian)

The judgment leaves several claims alive. Next failed to overturn findings on lower night and overtime premiums and the absence of paid rest breaks for store employees; Sunday premium payments must return to the original tribunal. The mixed result draws a consequential line through equal-pay law. Employers have gained room to respond to distinct labour markets even when the lower-paid workforce is disproportionately female. Workers can still challenge the individual conditions that produce disadvantage. For retailers with large warehouses and shops, the next hearings will show how much of the gap survives once every component beyond basic pay is examined. (Guardian)

Technology

Apple’s premium iPhone week arrives in a memory squeeze

Apple’s September event begins on Wednesday with three expected phones aimed entirely at the expensive end of the range: iPhone 18 Pro, iPhone 18 Pro Max and the first foldable iPhone Ultra. The Pro models are expected to bring the A20 Pro chip, an Apple-designed C2 modem, camera changes and improved battery life. The Ultra is rumoured to open into a 7.8-inch inner display, use Touch ID and cost above $2,000. The regular iPhone 18 and a second iPhone Air are expected in early 2027, removing the usual lower-priced new models from this autumn’s launch. These details remain pre-event expectations, but the shape of the range already reflects a difficult component market. (9to5Mac)

Memory has become the pressure point across phones, computers and consoles. Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron control about 90% of the market, and the most profitable capacity is moving toward high-bandwidth memory for artificial-intelligence data centres. Producing a given amount of that specialised memory can consume roughly three times the wafer capacity of conventional DRAM. At the same time, manufacturers want more ordinary memory in consumer devices so smaller AI models can run locally. Counterpoint estimates smartphone DRAM prices rose 56% in the first quarter of 2026 and another 83% in the second; the estimated cost of 16GB climbed from about $42 to $181 in a year. (Verge)

New factories offer a slow answer. Micron expects its Idaho plant to begin wafer output in mid-2027, while a vast New York complex will produce meaningful volumes around 2030 after years of construction, equipment installation and testing. Long contracts from wealthy AI customers give suppliers certainty and exceptional margins now, leaving consumer-electronics companies to absorb costs, reduce specifications or raise prices. Apple has unusual leverage over its supply chain, yet a premium-only launch would show the limits of even that advantage. Wednesday will reveal the products; their prices will say more about how a global industrial bottleneck is reaching ordinary buyers. (Verge, 9to5Mac)

WhatsApp prepares a front door for outside AI agents

WhatsApp is testing native support for as many as five third-party AI agents on one account. A user creates a dedicated chat, receives an API key and connects an agent running locally or in the cloud. The agent can read only material shared inside that conversation and cannot enter group chats, inspect contacts or open other media. Each bot can have its own name and profile image. The feature is available to a limited group of Android users, with a wider release expected, and gives agent developers a sanctioned route into a service that previously risked banning accounts used for unofficial bots. (9to5Mac)

The early version carries two important warnings: agent answers may be inaccurate, and these chats are currently outside WhatsApp’s end-to-end encryption. That combination makes the convenience easy to understand and the limits easy to miss. Messaging turns an automated system into something that feels like an ordinary contact, ready for instructions throughout the day. Clear separation from personal conversations, visible privacy labels and tight permissions will matter as much as the connection itself. The product could bring remote agents to a much larger audience; trust will depend on preserving the boundaries promised by the first design. (9to5Mac)

Two game makers test different ways of doing more with the past

Onimusha: Way of the Sword sold more than one million copies on its first day, taking the Capcom series beyond ten million after a 20-year wait for a new instalment. The publisher credits demos, public hands-on events and a broad pre-launch campaign, and says it will reactivate more properties that have gone without a recent release. That turns a successful revival into a strategy for a deep catalogue that includes Dino Crisis, Breath of Fire, Lost Planet and Viewtiful Joe. Capcom still has to choose which worlds can support a modern game. Onimusha has shown that a long absence can preserve an audience and build fresh appetite. (IGN)

EA Sports is using generative AI to expand commentary in NHL 27, based on recorded work from broadcaster John Buccigross. Human review is still catching the small errors that break sporting authenticity, including the vocabulary used for an arena crowd. EA’s new American football and football games already disclose generative-AI use on Steam; NHL 27, which launches for PlayStation 5 and Xbox Series consoles on Friday, has no equivalent store label there. The experiment raises practical questions that will follow synthetic voices into more games: how performers consent and are paid, how new lines are approved and whether players can tell where recorded performance ends. (IGN)

Sports

Arsenal begin the Champions League as narrow favourites

The Champions League league phase starts tonight with Paris Saint-Germain seeking a third consecutive title and five English clubs trying to stop them. Last season’s runners-up Arsenal enter with the highest probability in Opta’s model at 20.4%, narrowly ahead of Bayern Munich on 20%; Manchester City sit at 12.7% and PSG at 7.3%. All five Premier League sides rank in the competition’s top ten and each has a greater than 50% chance of reaching the last 16. Arsenal drew Real Madrid and Bayern from the top pot, balanced by games against the two lowest-ranked teams, Sabah and Slovan Bratislava. Martin Ødegaard’s sharp start after an injury-disrupted season gives a settled side one more route through tight matches. (BBC)

Manchester City open away to Porto with the hardest schedule among the English entrants, including later meetings with Barcelona, PSG and Napoli. Erling Haaland has scored three goals in three league wins and played every competitive minute after helping Norway to the World Cup quarter-finals. Enzo Maresca plans to rest him during the season, with Antoine Semenyo available at centre-forward and Phil Foden or Rayan Cherki able to play as a false nine. Porto have won their first five league games under Francesco Farioli and lost only once in their past 14 or 15 European matches. The new campaign begins with familiar contenders, yet the league format gives every difficult draw time to expose a thin squad. (Guardian, BBC)
