The G7 opens its emergency fuel tanks to hold off a wider squeeze

The G7 will release as many as 100m barrels of diesel and crude oil from emergency reserves, turning a week of threats and hurried calls into a coordinated intervention. The plan drawn up under France’s presidency divides the total evenly between diesel and crude. A substantial share of the diesel is due to reach the market within 20 days, and ministers will consider further releases if prices remain under pressure. Donald Trump has withdrawn the threat to stop US diesel exports after Europe agreed to use its own stores. That avoids an immediate scramble for alternative cargoes, while placing the burden of calming the market on reserves designed for a prolonged disruption. (Guardian, BBC)

The agreement arrives with Britain’s pump price at a record £2 a litre for diesel. Filling an average family car now costs about £110, nearly £32 more than it did before the war with Iran. The consequences travel far beyond drivers: most British lorries, vans and agricultural vehicles run on diesel, so transport costs pass into groceries, construction and public services. Europe’s refineries make about 70% of the diesel the continent consumes, leaving a large import gap. The US briefly became the pressure valve, exporting a record 1.9m barrels a week overseas in early August. Those sales helped Europe and Asia while draining American seasonal stocks to their lowest level since 1996 and pushing US pump prices above $5.85 a gallon. (Guardian)

The shortage still begins at the Strait of Hormuz. Disrupted Middle Eastern exports have constrained refinery output across the Gulf and in China, while Ukrainian attacks have reduced Russian fuel production to a 20-year low. Brent crude was near $72 a barrel before the US-Israeli campaign against Iran and traded above $100 on Friday. News of the G7 release briefly pulled it towards $98; by the close it had climbed past $102 again. That quick reversal is a useful warning. A reserve release can smooth a dangerous few weeks and restrain panic buying. It cannot reopen a shipping route, rebuild refining capacity or replace output lost to war. (Guardian)

Governments have already drawn deeply on this insurance. The International Energy Agency authorised a record 400m-barrel release in March, about a third of member governments’ stockpiles and more than twice the response after Russia invaded Ukraine in 2022. Another 100m barrels will buy time, though the protection left for winter is smaller and the source of the disruption remains in place. For Britain, the next test is practical: how quickly fuel reaches wholesalers, whether the promise is enough to soften forecourt prices and how much of the saving survives the journey through the supply chain. For the G7, the decision also sets a political precedent. An American threat aimed at domestic prices produced a shared intervention among allies. The barrels can settle the immediate dispute. The dependence exposed by it will last longer. (Guardian, BBC)

Britain’s pension promise will change in law before it changes in practice

Andy Burnham intends to legislate during this parliament for the end of the state pension triple lock, while keeping the existing guarantee in force until 2030. The timing lets Labour honour its promise for the current term and asks MPs to settle the replacement before the next election. Under the present rule, pensions rise by whichever is highest: inflation, earnings growth or 2.5%. The new mechanism will adjust the earnings element to prevent an unusually sharp wage rebound producing a matching jump in pensions. Voters will therefore reach the next election knowing the rule already written into law, and opposition parties will have to say whether they would repeal it. (Guardian)

The Treasury expects the adjustment to reduce annual pension spending by £15bn by the end of the 2030s and £50bn by 2050. Those long-range totals depend heavily on the pattern of wages and inflation. The Resolution Foundation found that applying the formula to different recent decades produced savings from zero to £24bn a year. A calm economy could make the reform modest; volatile pay and prices would make it much more valuable to the exchequer and more visible to pensioners. Polling gives Burnham room to proceed, with 48% backing the plan and 28% opposing it, even as unions and rival parties warn of an electoral cost. (Guardian)

The savings are intended to help fund a national care service in England, free at the point of use for care costs while accommodation remains outside the offer. Burnham also plans to exempt lower-income pensioners from income tax during this parliament. Together, the policies shift protection from an automatic annual uplift towards services and targeted relief. That bargain will be judged in households where pension income pays for ordinary living costs long before care is needed. The government has created four years between the vote in Parliament and the first altered payment. It needs to use them to define the new earnings rule clearly, build the care system and show how people with small private pensions will be protected when the guarantee changes. (Guardian)

Kyiv’s winter has begun before the temperature falls

Russia has intensified its attacks on Kyiv weeks before the coldest weather, striking a school, a hospital, offices, datacentres and one of the bridges that carries traffic across the Dnipro. Energy facilities and a thermal power plant have also been hit, leaving several districts without water and electricity by morning. The capital had preserved a degree of daily normality while Kharkiv, Dnipro and cities closer to the front endured frequent bombardment. That distance is disappearing. Parents are spending school mornings in shelters, commuters are abandoning cars on damaged crossings and the city of three million is preparing for disruption once associated with the first months of the full invasion. (Guardian, BBC)

The threat has changed as well as the tempo. Kyiv faces a shortage of Patriot interceptors for ballistic missiles while Russia deploys faster jet-powered drones that are harder to bring down. Ukrainian manufacturers are rushing new interceptor drones into service, though defence specialists expect meaningful coverage to take weeks or months. Officials have warned European governments about an intercepted Russian plan involving as many as 1,000 missiles, drones and bombs against substations, hydroelectric and thermal plants and boiler houses. The aim is to overload air defences and turn damaged infrastructure into a civilian emergency of darkness, cold and interrupted water. (Guardian)

The ground war offers a different picture. Russia captured about 361 square kilometres in September, an increase on recent months but still a slow advance bought at heavy cost. Ukrainian counteroffensives recovered roughly 126 square kilometres in the east. Air power lets Moscow apply pressure far beyond those contested fields, against a capital whose bridges, schools and power network cannot move. Kyiv can repair individual sites and spread generators through neighbourhoods. Its larger requirement is a steady supply of interceptors and air-defence systems before the campaign reaches full winter intensity. The city’s routines have always carried risk. Repeated strikes on the systems that make ordinary life possible are now turning that risk into the organising fact of each day. (Guardian, BBC)

Brazil’s final campaign weekend opens without a final debate

Brazil reaches Sunday’s presidential vote with Lula leading Flávio Bolsonaro by about 45% to 40%, enough for a narrow advantage and short of the majority required to avoid a second round on 25 October. Both candidates spent Friday asking undecided voters and supporters of smaller parties for a useful vote. Lula led a large march from Candelária to Cinelândia in central Rio, linking his campaign to sites associated with resistance to military rule and the restoration of direct elections. Bolsonaro appealed online to voters who dislike him but want to end the Workers’ party era. His promise to pardon his father, Jair Bolsonaro, and appoint five conservative supreme court justices gives that choice consequences that reach beyond one term. (Guardian, Meio)

The campaign lost its last televised encounter when Globo cancelled Thursday night’s debate hours before broadcast. Lula had already chosen an interview with the Flow podcast. Bolsonaro then withdrew shortly before airtime. The broadcaster was also navigating court decisions that changed its agreed format: the electoral court barred an empty lectern marking Lula’s absence, the supreme court ordered the inclusion of Renan Santos, whose party had fallen below the representation threshold, and an earlier ruling secured Romeu Zema’s place. Globo concluded that the programme could no longer proceed. Millions of voters were left without a direct comparison of the two leaders at the moment their campaigns were trying to consolidate tactical support. (Meio, Folha)

The public mood contains both anxiety and a firmer attachment to the system being tested. Datafolha found that 73% prefer democracy, up from 69% in 2024, while 43% describe themselves as pessimistic about the country on the eve of voting. The latest regional figures give Lula the lead in Minas Gerais and Bolsonaro the advantage in Rio, with São Paulo finely balanced. Turnout and late movement will decide whether the national gap holds. Sunday will also elect governors and legislators, distributing power around whoever reaches the presidency. Brazil enters the weekend with a close race, no closing debate and competing claims that the vote will define the democratic order for a generation. The first answer comes at the ballot box; a runoff remains the likeliest route to a final one. (Folha, Folha, Folha)

Tesco makes safety part of paid leave

Tesco has become the first British supermarket to offer dedicated paid leave to employees experiencing domestic abuse. Its 300,000 UK staff can take up to three days flexibly to attend court, arrange emergency accommodation, change locks, secure childcare or separate finances. They will not have to produce formal evidence. Managers have been trained to recognise signs of abuse and connect affected colleagues with specialist services. The policy, negotiated with the shopworkers’ union Usdaw, gives a practical form to an idea employers often express more vaguely: a workplace can be a route to safety when home is the source of danger. (Guardian)

More than three million people experienced domestic abuse in England and Wales last year, and SafeLives estimates that eight women a month are killed by a current or former partner. Paid time matters because leaving can require several appointments, sudden travel and financial decisions while an abuser may monitor money and movement. BP, Vodafone and some councils already provide related support, and Northern Ireland guarantees ten days of paid leave in law. A private member’s bill proposes the same entitlement for England and Wales. Tesco’s three-day policy falls below that standard, yet its size can shift expectations across retail. For an employee making an urgent plan, the decisive feature may be the simplest one: asking for help will not also remove the pay needed to act. (Guardian)

Apple prepares a narrower gate to the whole Mac

Apple will add controls around Full Disk Access in response to the spread of AI agents that can act across a computer. The permission gives an application broad reach into files and data that macOS normally protects, a level of access created largely for backup software and now used by launchers, automation tools, file managers, creative applications and utilities. Apple says future access will require very explicit action from the user. That appears likely to mean an extra confirmation, though the company has not yet explained the final mechanism or which categories of software will qualify. The uncertainty matters because a safeguard aimed at autonomous agents could also change the behaviour of familiar tools that have relied on the permission for years. (Verge, MacStories, 9to5Mac)

The security case is straightforward. An agent that can inspect documents, operate applications and complete tasks can also expose far more information when instructions are malicious, a connected service is compromised or the model misunderstands its goal. Mac permissions were built around software that waits for a person to press a button. Agents can chain many small actions into one large outcome, so consent granted once carries further than it used to. Apple’s challenge is to make the dangerous boundary visible without producing a ritual that people click through automatically. A warning that arrives at the moment an agent requests a protected file is easier to understand than a blanket permission granted during setup. Developers, meanwhile, need an interface that preserves legitimate background work without giving every capable assistant the keys to the entire home directory. (MacStories, MacMagazine)

The decision is an early sign of the operating system adapting to software that behaves more like a delegated worker. Camera, microphone and location prompts protect single resources; a useful agent may need messages, calendars, browsers, local files and the ability to act inside several apps. Full Disk Access compresses those separate decisions into one unusually powerful switch. Apple now has to separate genuine intent from convenience and make revocation simple when trust changes. The details will determine whether Mac automation becomes safer or merely more cumbersome. Either way, the old permission model has reached its limit: people are no longer authorising a passive utility to read a folder. They may be authorising a system that decides what to read next. (Verge, MacStories)

Warner Bros and Paramount prepare to live under one roof

Paramount Skydance expects to complete its acquisition of Warner Bros Discovery on Tuesday and has chosen Skydance as the name of the combined parent company. Paramount and Warner Bros will continue as studio brands beneath it, bringing franchises including Harry Potter, Batman, Game of Thrones, Lord of the Rings, Star Trek and Mission: Impossible into the same corporate group. Chief executive David Ellison also plans to combine HBO Max and Paramount+ into one streaming service. Their existing totals would give that product about 200 million subscribers, though there is no launch date, price or public name yet. Ellison has indicated that HBO will remain a distinct label inside the larger offering. (9to5Mac, IGN)

The merger closes after a long approval process, a settlement and a successful bid against Netflix for Warner Bros Discovery. Scale gives Skydance a deep library, major cinema releases, news operations and two streaming platforms whose costs can be combined. It also concentrates another large share of film and television history under a single management team. Subscribers may gain one service with a broader catalogue and receive a higher bill or fewer competing choices alongside it. Creators and cinemas will watch which projects survive when overlapping operations are consolidated. The first trailer can celebrate a century of recognisable characters; the harder work begins after Tuesday, when the new company has to decide how many films, shows, brands and employees its promised efficiency can support. (9to5Mac, IGN)

Arsenal chooses a quieter evolution of the Emirates

Arsenal have shelved a major expansion of the Emirates Stadium after two years of feasibility work and will focus on hospitality, retail, visitor facilities and the matchday experience. The 60,704-seat ground will gain safe standing in the Clock End this season and the North Bank next season, while the club continues upgrades with its catering partner. A large redevelopment could have required temporary home matches at Wembley, disruption that concerned Mikel Arteta. The decision leaves Arsenal with a smaller ground than Tottenham’s 62,850-seat stadium and fewer opportunities to stage the concerts and American football events designed into their neighbour’s venue. (Guardian)

The financial pressure for more seats has eased as sporting success and corporate hospitality lift existing revenue. Arsenal’s matchday income has reportedly doubled over five seasons to more than £150m, helped by a return to the Champions League and last season’s Premier League title. Owners Kroenke Sports & Entertainment have asked Boston Consulting Group to review parts of the club’s wider operation after agreeing a record $4bn purchase of baseball’s Los Angeles Angels. Arsenal say the work is focused on efficiency outside football and gives no indication of job losses among roughly 800 staff. Supporters have also received assurances that ordinary seats will not be removed to create more corporate space. The club has chosen incremental gain over years of building work: keep the team at home, earn more from the ground it has and improve the parts of matchday that expansion plans often postpone. (Guardian)
