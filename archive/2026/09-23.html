Burnham and Trump discover how far warmth can carry a difficult alliance

Andy Burnham’s first face-to-face meeting with Donald Trump delivered the cordial pictures Downing Street wanted and left the hardest questions exactly where they were. Trump praised the prime minister as a natural businessperson and suggested their relationship could be stronger than the one he had with Keir Starmer. Burnham spoke of building on the connection formed in their earlier calls. British officials were relieved by the tone after months in which the Iran war, the Chagos Islands and the regulation of artificial intelligence had pulled the two governments apart. The encounter bought the new prime minister some political room; it did not narrow those disagreements. (Guardian, BBC)

The sharpest divide concerns Iran. Burnham arrived in New York intending to ask for de-escalation as disrupted fuel supplies raise prices across Britain. Trump used his UN address to threaten Iran with destruction if it refused a peace agreement, then told allies enduring higher living costs that prices would fall when the war ended. That offers no timetable to households or businesses paying today’s bills. Britain is already assisting Saudi air operations intended to protect energy infrastructure and shipping routes, while withholding support from the American strikes that widened the conflict. Burnham’s task is to defend that distinction while persuading Washington that allied patience has an economic limit. (Guardian)

Artificial intelligence supplied a second test. Burnham wants Britain’s G20 presidency next year to pursue shared principles and safety standards, and he faces pressure to join a Finnish-Norwegian initiative requiring testing before powerful models reach the market. Trump rejected international controls in his speech and presented rapid development as a defining American frontier. The disagreement reaches beyond rhetoric. A global rule works only when leading developers and their home governments accept scrutiny, while a voluntary patchwork lets models, data and capital move towards the easiest jurisdiction. Burnham is offering Britain as an honest broker just as the White House treats brokerage itself as restraint. (Guardian)

Chagos provided the awkward closing note. Trump again attacked the agreement transferring sovereignty to Mauritius while Britain leases back the Diego Garcia base. Burnham indicated that he would look at the deal, an answer likely to invite pressure from Washington and questions at home about whether a settled policy is being reopened. He did secure a promise that Trump would raise the imprisoned Hong Kong publisher Jimmy Lai with Xi Jinping. The meeting therefore produced one useful commitment and a relationship capable of functioning in public. Its value will be measured in what follows: lower regional tension, a workable route for AI safety and an alliance in which praise does not substitute for agreement. (Guardian, BBC)

Police disrupt a suspected attack on Manchester’s Jewish community

Two men in their 30s have been arrested over a suspected terrorist plot targeting Jewish people in Manchester. Counter-terrorism officers detained them in the Northern Quarter on Sunday afternoon, the eve of Yom Kippur, when synagogues across Britain were operating with heightened security. Investigators described the suspected plan as fairly imminent and in its later stages. The Metropolitan Police, which is leading the case, obtained warrants allowing both men to remain in custody until 27 September while searches were carried out at properties in Liverpool and Salford. Officers say there is no continuing threat to the public connected with the investigation. (BBC, Guardian)

The timing deepened the alarm for a community already entering its holiest day behind guards and police patrols. Home secretary Shabana Mahmood said Jewish people should not have to live behind ever higher walls and promised action against antisemitism at its source. That promise covers two different forms of protection: intelligence work able to interrupt a plot before anyone is harmed, and the slower civic work that allows schools, places of worship and families to live without treating permanent fortification as normal. The arrests are allegations and the evidence will have to pass through court. For now, the immediate danger appears to have been contained, and the wider burden placed on British Jewish life remains plainly visible. (BBC)

An Arctic defence deal gives Greenland sovereignty and America two bases

The United States, Denmark and Greenland have ended more than a year of confrontation with an agreement allowing two additional American military bases on the island. Washington can establish defence areas at Narsarsuaq in the south and Mestersvig in the east, and expand its work at the existing Pituffik Space Base in the north-west. Narsarsuaq has a large runway and deep-water access from its history as a second world war base. Mestersvig is a remote outpost used by a Danish naval dog-sled unit. Each site strengthens surveillance and logistics in an Arctic where Russia operates heavily and melting ice is opening new commercial and strategic routes. (BBC)

The political language matters as much as the map. The pact recognises Danish sovereignty, Greenland’s right to self-determination and the territorial integrity of the island; no land is transferred to the US. Details of construction and operation still require agreement from all three parties. That is a substantial retreat from Trump’s earlier threats to take Greenland, which united Greenlanders, Denmark and European allies in opposition. Copenhagen and Nuuk have now answered his stated security concern without accepting his claim over the territory. The updated 1951 framework also bars non-Nato countries from maintaining forces or installations there. America gains the presence it wanted, while Greenland secures a written boundary around how that presence can grow. (BBC)

Lula takes Brazil’s election and its sovereignty to the UN

Lula opened the UN general assembly for the tenth time with a speech aimed simultaneously at the hall and Brazil’s electorate. He invoked Yasser Arafat and Yitzhak Rabin as leaders whose pursuit of peace was defeated by hatred, then warned that Brazil’s 4 October election would be transparent and competitive despite domestic actors aligning themselves with foreign interests. The target was clear even without naming Donald Trump or Flávio Bolsonaro. Brazilian intelligence has raised the risk of American interference, and Trump’s pressure on the country has become part of the campaign. Lula presented sovereignty, democratic procedure and peaceful diplomacy as a single case for another term. (Meio, Folha)

His harder electoral work begins after the flight home. Lula remains resilient after three presidencies, prison and the collapse of his chosen successor, yet polls place him in a close race with a challenger carrying the Bolsonaro name. A new Datafolha cut shows 47% of women who head households supporting Lula and 27% backing Flávio, a valuable lead in the group likely to settle the contest. The government is trying to connect with those voters through a crackdown on online betting, which has pushed debt and instability into many homes. At the UN, Lula attacked betting companies and large technology platforms for harming families and evading responsibility. Foreign-policy stature may reinforce his claim to leadership; practical action on household pressures offers the stronger bridge from that stage to the ballot box. (Folha, Meio)

England’s green farming fund disappears in six hours

The entire £233m budget for England’s sustainable farming incentive was allocated between 10am and 4pm on the day applications opened. The scheme pays farmers for public goods such as healthier soil, wildlife habitat and cleaner water, replacing the European Union system that was tied more closely to land ownership and production. Many applicants had waited since the programme closed suddenly in March 2025, and the next round will not open until 2027. Some spent as long as five hours trying to reach a helpline while the available money vanished. A first-come system turned long-term environmental planning into a race won partly by whoever had a working connection and a trouble-free form. (Guardian)

The rush is evidence of financial pressure as well as enthusiasm. Farms have endured crop losses from heat and drought while fertiliser costs have risen with the Iran war. Nature groups warn that thousands of businesses will now postpone work that supports the legal target to restore 30% of nature by 2030. The programme’s popularity gives the government a useful answer: farmers are ready to make the transition when the funding is dependable. Its design produced the opposite signal, asking them to invest in multi-year changes through a six-hour lottery. Ministers will need a budget and allocation method that reward environmental value, regional need and deliverability. Leaving capable farms idle for another year weakens food security and nature recovery together. (Guardian)

A simple test could spare women invasive womb-cancer checks

Doctors in Manchester have developed a low-cost test using urine and vaginal-fluid samples to help assess women with bleeding after menopause. That symptom triggers an urgent referral, though cancer is found in only about 5% to 10% of cases. Current checks can include an internal ultrasound, a biopsy and a hysteroscopy in which a camera passes into the womb. In a study of 1,864 women at seven hospitals in north-west England, the new approach identified 80 of 99 cancers and correctly returned a negative result for 93% of women without the disease. The findings offer a way to move the highest-risk patients forward quickly and spare many others an immediate invasive procedure. (Guardian)

The test belongs at the triage stage. Its sensitivity is insufficient to rule out cancer by itself, persistent symptoms would still require full assessment, and the study relied on specialist cytologists whose accuracy may be difficult to reproduce everywhere. It has also been tested only in women with symptoms. Those limits define the next stage of research, while the potential benefit remains substantial. A reliable first screen could shorten the anxious wait for people who need urgent treatment, give earlier reassurance to lower-risk patients and reduce pressure on hospital services. The best diagnostic pathway will combine the new samples with clinical judgment and a clear route back for anyone whose bleeding continues. (Guardian)

Technology & Culture

AI companies compete on cost, autonomy and the right to act on your computer

OpenAI has added GPT-6 Sol and GPT-6 Luna to ChatGPT Work and Codex, extending the advances of its larger Astra model into faster and cheaper tiers. Sol is aimed at complex coding and longer agentic work; Luna is designed for narrower, high-volume tasks. Both are priced at half the promotional API cost of their GPT-5.6 predecessors, and Luna is available to free desktop users. Anthropic released Claude Opus 5.5 on the same day, continuing a rapid upgrade cycle at the high end of the market. The useful shift is economic: models capable of working through multi-step professional tasks are becoming affordable enough to run more often, for longer, and across routine work that could not justify premium inference costs. (9to5Mac, MacMagazine, 9to5Mac)

Rabbit is making a different bet with OS3, an agent that runs in the cloud while operating locally across Windows, macOS and Linux. One account can connect five devices, choose among AI models and reach files or applications needed to complete a task. Access can come through a browser, Telegram, iMessage or the company’s R1 device. Production of the poorly received R1 has stopped, although Rabbit is planning a new programmable “cyberdeck” for OS3. The move turns the company’s main product from a gadget into an orchestration layer, placing it in direct competition with the agent features being built into larger platforms. (Verge)

Lower prices and wider computer access make the governance argument at the UN immediately practical. An agent that can select a device, open an application and manipulate a file holds more power than a chatbot answering a question. Rabbit says it will neither copy nor sell users’ data, while chats and derived memories remain on its servers and linked model providers apply their own policies. That chain can be difficult for an ordinary user to inspect. Competition will increasingly turn on visible permission boundaries, logs that show what an agent did, and a dependable pause before consequential actions. Speed and cost decide whether people try these systems. Control decides whether they keep trusting them. (Verge, Guardian)

The Booker shortlist makes room for old winners and a first novel

Marlon James and Douglas Stuart have returned to the Booker prize shortlist alongside Pulitzer winner Elizabeth Strout, speculative-fiction veteran M John Harrison, poet and novelist Luke Kennard, and debut author Rebecca Perry. James’s 640-page The Disappearers follows queer men attacked while rehearsing a play in Aids-era Jamaica. Stuart’s John of John brings its protagonist home to the Outer Hebrides, while Strout leaves her familiar characters behind for a Massachusetts teacher whose settled life is threatened by an old secret. Harrison’s The End of Everything places its apocalypse in a Kent coastal town. Together they give the list an unusual breadth of scale, from private histories to civilisational collapse. (Guardian, BBC)

Kennard’s Black Bag is a comic campus novel about an actor hired to attend lectures inside a leather bag. Perry’s May We Feed the King, the sole debut, follows a curator whose work dressing historic rooms turns into an obsession with a medieval monarch. The judges spent about three hours choosing the six from a longlist of 13 and stressed the human risks taken by the finalists. Emma Cline and Gwendoline Riley were prominent omissions. The winner of the £50,000 award will be announced on 9 November, leaving readers seven weeks to discover whether familiarity helps the former champions or whether the prize prefers the sharper surprise of a new voice. (Guardian)

Apple Music opens an intimate London room inside Battersea Power Station

Apple is opening a 600-capacity live venue at Battersea Power Station, where its UK offices and flagship store already sit. Apple Music Hall will host concerts recorded with Spatial Audio and distributed through Apple Music, giving a relatively small audience access to performances designed for a much larger digital life. The company has begun taking registrations for ticket alerts but has yet to announce the opening programme. London gains a purpose-built room at a moment when smaller venues are struggling with costs, and Apple gains a regular supply of exclusive performances that tie hardware, streaming and place together. The test will be whether the hall develops its own musical identity once the novelty of the brand and building has passed. (MacStories, 9to5Mac)

Arsenal secure Arteta’s future and survive an uneven European night

Mikel Arteta has verbally agreed a long-term contract extension that should keep him at Arsenal for at least another three years. His current deal expires at the end of this season, and the new terms will include a substantial rise from a package believed to be worth about £10m a year plus a £5m Champions League bonus. The reward follows Arsenal’s first Premier League title since 2004 and a run to the Champions League final, where Paris Saint-Germain won on penalties. Arteta is now the division’s longest-serving manager after Pep Guardiola’s departure from Manchester City. A surprise 3-0 loss at Brighton ended seven straight wins, but the agreement removes a question that could otherwise have followed every setback through the season. Arsenal can turn next to Declan Rice, whose contract has entered its final two years. (Guardian)

The women’s side needed a late Mariona Caldentey penalty to draw 1-1 with HB Køge in the Champions League. Arsenal had entered the competition among the English teams expected to challenge a weakened Barcelona, so dropping home points against the Danish champions was an early warning about how little reputation buys in the new campaign. Manchester City also had to recover, earning a 2-2 draw away to Bayern Munich through a late goal from Lilly Wamser. Both results preserve room for repair while making the next fixtures more important. For Arsenal, the day carried the whole range of long-season management: certainty around the person directing the club, irritation at a performance below its level and a point rescued before the damage became larger. (Guardian, Guardian)

Xbox’s reset leaves one studio independent and another facing closure

Microsoft has begun the formal consultation required to close Ninja Theory after two proposed rescue agreements failed. The Cambridge studio created the Hellblade games and announced a third entry, Senua, at the Xbox showcase in June, days before a corporate reset placed the developer in danger. Microsoft says it remains open to another buyer or structure during the consultation, but the new game now has no assured path to release. The sequence is especially bruising for staff: a project was presented to players as part of Xbox’s future while its maker was already being considered for closure or sale. Commercial struggles around Hellblade have collided with a strategy concentrating money on Minecraft, Halo, Fallout and The Elder Scrolls. (IGN)

Elsewhere in the same restructuring, State of Decay developer Undead Labs has separated from Xbox as an independent, employee-owned company and says State of Decay 3 remains in development. Rare has been placed under Activision, as has Halo, prompting anxiety among fans and an immediate example of the human cost when a Halo Studios cinematic director was promoted and laid off in the same week. Microsoft has shown that independence can work for some teams, with Double Fine and Compulsion also finding routes out while retaining projects or intellectual property. Each outcome depends on financing and which assets Microsoft is prepared to release. The policy may produce leaner accounts and stronger concentration around the biggest franchises. It is also reducing the range of studios able to build the next franchise before anyone knows how large it might become. (IGN, IGN, IGN)
