LEAD STORY

Europe called an emergency meeting on Ceuta

EU interior ministers will meet urgently on Tuesday after more than 50,000 people crossed from Morocco into the Spanish enclave of Ceuta. The mass arrival has opened sharp divisions between member states over responsibility for one of Europe’s most exposed borders. (Guardian, BBC)

Officials from EU governments and the Frontex border agency agreed to the meeting after talks on Saturday. Spain is pushing for a common response as the humanitarian emergency, border control and free movement inside the bloc converge on the same decision. (Guardian, BBC)

NEWS

A missile attack on Kyiv killed at least 10

Ballistic missiles hit five districts of Kyiv early on Saturday, killing at least 10 people and injuring 22. Emergency crews rescued 105 people from affected areas as Ukraine pressed for stronger air defences and permission to use Starlink in attacks inside Russia. (Guardian)

Westminster faced its drinking culture

A report described parliament as an alcohol-soaked workplace where long hours, loneliness, lobbying and ready access to drink reinforce each other. MPs can be offered free alcohol from midday, and one parliamentarian said colleagues have required hospital treatment after drinking. (Guardian)

Naples recorded its strongest quake in 40 years

A magnitude-4.7 earthquake near Naples injured at least 26 people and forced about 300 from their homes. The tremor cut power, disrupted public transport and damaged a pier in Campi Flegrei, the volcanic crater at the centre of the region’s seismic swarm. (Guardian)

A Nazca Lines flight crashed with 13 aboard

A tourist plane crashed in southern Peru after departing from Pisco, killing 11 tourists and two pilots. The Cessna Grand Caravan came down in Pueblo Viejo, about 12km from the Nazca Lines archaeological site. (Guardian)

Nirmal Purja died in a Broad Peak avalanche

Nepali mountaineer Nirmal Purja, a former member of the UK Special Forces, died while leading an expedition on Pakistan’s Broad Peak. An avalanche struck the 11-person group on Thursday, killing Purja and other members of the expedition. (Guardian)

The WHO challenged food-industry lawsuits

The World Health Organization accused major ultra-processed food companies of suing governments that adopt healthier-diet measures. Director general Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus said the cases obstruct action on obesity and impose billions in healthcare and legal costs. (Guardian)

Brazil’s PT put Congress at the centre of the election

The Workers’ party aims to increase its Chamber delegation from 66 to 90 and its Senate group from nine to 14, seeking firmer support for a possible fourth Lula government. It reserved R$326m of its electoral fund for legislative campaigns, including R$61.9m for Senate contests. (Meio)

Brazil’s right fixed more names on the ballot

Tarcísio de Freitas was formally nominated for re-election as São Paulo governor beside presidential candidate Flávio Bolsonaro, while keeping his message centred on the state. Bia Kicis announced that Michelle Bolsonaro will run for the Senate in the Federal District. (Folha, Folha)

The Lulinha inquiries strained Brazil’s investigators

Advances in inquiries involving Fábio Luís Lula da Silva deepened distrust between the Federal Police and supreme court justice André Mendonça, who oversees the cases. Mendonça also authorised a second inquiry into suspected influence peddling by the president’s son. (Folha, Folha)

Reform UK’s finances drew wider scrutiny

The relationships around Nigel Farage’s £5m gift from billionaire Christopher Harborne, the Cottrell family’s links and donations, and Richard Tice’s business interests have brought Reform UK’s financial structure under closer examination. (Guardian)

Heat pushed Britain back towards imported vegetables

Pubs, restaurants and supermarkets are importing Spanish lettuce and broccoli during the UK growing season after drought and extreme heat reduced domestic yields. Wholesale tomato prices rose more than 60%, iceberg lettuce 90% and potatoes more than 40%, while farm water reserves ran low. (Guardian)

A cooperative model entered the water-company fight

Labour MPs and mayors urged the prime minister to convert failing water companies into not-for-profit cooperatives. The mutual model would put customers and the public into control while avoiding the increase in government debt projected for nationalisation. (Guardian)

High-protein demand reached infant formula

Rising demand for whey protein, driven partly by high-protein diets and the use of weight-loss drugs, could raise the price of infant formula. The same ingredient sits inside a growing fitness market and a tightly regulated staple for babies. (Guardian)

The ECB widened its financial-stability climate test

ECB executive board member Frank Elderson warned that climate disruption and the loss of natural systems pose a rapidly growing threat to the global economy. The central bank is increasing its monitoring of risks tied to damaged ecosystem services as wildfires intensify across Europe. (Guardian)

TECHNOLOGY

Chinese technology advances unsettled US industry

New Chinese AI models, robots and specialised chips rattled markets and divided US technology executives over how to respond. The advances have pushed competition with China from a standing policy argument into a more immediate product and industrial challenge. (Guardian)

Rare books were pulled into the AI supply chain

Australian secondhand booksellers fear rare titles are being bought, scanned and destroyed to supply AI training material. The practice threatens editions whose bindings, notes, provenance and scarcity carry value beyond the words on their pages. (Guardian)

Pixel 11 pricing leaked before Google’s launch

A leak covering the Pixel 11 line points to a $100 increase for the standard model, taking its starting price to $899 with 256GB of storage. The details broadly match earlier information ahead of Google’s 12 August event. (The Verge)

Minnesota’s water hacks became a political claim

The FBI, Environmental Protection Agency and US cyber agency stopped short of attributing attacks on Minnesota water systems, while suspicion centred on Iran. Donald Trump blamed state governor Tim Walz without presenting a federal finding to support the claim. (The Verge)

Brazil sought mining warnings on Meta platforms

Federal prosecutors asked a court to require Facebook and Instagram to show preventive notices to users posting about mining and wildcat gold extraction. The case would put a public-interest warning directly into Meta’s publishing flow in Brazil. (Folha)

CULTURE

Vincent Pastore died aged 80

Vincent Pastore, best known as Salvatore “Big Pussy” Bonpensiero in The Sopranos, died at 80. His role as Tony Soprano’s friend turned FBI informant made him one of the defining faces of the drama’s early seasons. (Guardian, BBC)

The Odyssey became the summer’s shared film

Christopher Nolan’s The Odyssey has already outgrossed Oppenheimer and widened its audience towards an even gender split by its second weekend. Its box office and the argument around it have restored the old spectacle of one film occupying the cultural calendar. (Guardian)

A Spider-Man bootleg reached millions on X

A bootleg of Spider-Man: Brand New Day remained on X for more than seven hours, reaching 5.9m accounts and collecting more than 143,000 likes before removal. Disney then moved more quickly against reposted copies. (The Verge)

Boy Throb turned a visa case into a live show

Boy Throb are petitioning the US government for an extraordinary-ability visa for singer Darshan Magdum, who remains in India. Magdum joined the group’s New York show by video link while the band’s audience and record deal continue to grow without all its members in one country. (Guardian)

SPORTS

Infantino abandoned the World Cup sell-off

Gianni Infantino scrapped plans to sell stakes in the World Cup and other Fifa competitions after Uefa threatened a boycott. Uefa said it had lost confidence in his leadership and demanded a fundamental governance review, leaving the Fifa president under direct pressure to survive. (Guardian, BBC)

Welbeck joined Chelsea as Brentford broke their record

Chelsea signed 35-year-old Danny Welbeck from Brighton for a reported £5m on a two-year contract. Brentford completed a club-record deal for Mali international Ibrahima Sangaré, reported at £39m, as Premier League squads moved into their final three weeks of preparation. (Guardian)

LIFESTYLE

Britain’s switch-off became a social activity

Phone-free supper clubs, walks, floating retreats, mobile-free pubs and sauna book groups are turning disconnection into something people do together. London’s Offline Project begins events with a lockbox, giving participants a defined few hours beyond the reach of a screen. (Guardian)

Purple took over the British garden

Lavender, salvias and catmint have become British gardeners’ favoured colours after years of grey paving and immaculate lawns. Royal Horticultural Society research found purple leading the shift back towards colour, even through a summer of scorched grass. (Guardian)

Booking complaints exposed the refund gap

Travellers described arriving at unsafe or badly substituted accommodation and then struggling to recover money through Booking.com. Cases ranged from stained replacement apartments to melted electrical sockets, leaving customers caught between the platform and property owners. (Guardian)

Mini fridges moved into the bathroom

Sales of bathroom mini fridges are rising as households store GLP-1 medicines and skincare products close at hand. Most cosmetics do not require refrigeration, but weight-loss drugs and wellness routines are reshaping where consumers expect cold storage at home. (Guardian)
