Britain is putting a public company back on the electricity grid

Andy Burnham will use his first Labour conference speech as party leader and prime minister to create GB Grid, a government-backed company that can build electricity connections and compete with private network owners including National Grid. The organisation will have access to a pot of £4bn already allocated to Great British Energy, although officials do not expect it to use the whole sum. Its first job will be practical: connect new homes, factories and renewable-energy projects faster. Developers can wait years for a place on the network even when they have land, planning permission and finance ready. Those delays add cost, hold back clean power and leave growing businesses unable to secure the capacity they need. GB Grid gives the state a way to add infrastructure directly instead of relying entirely on the investment timetables of regulated companies. (Guardian)

The plan also expands the right of businesses to build their own connections and tap public funding towards the work. A similar change in Ireland cut connection times by 11 months, according to officials. Britain’s grid needs a much larger overhaul as heating, transport and industry move towards electricity and as offshore wind and solar generation are built far from the places that consume the most power. Network investment is usually recovered through bills, so speed alone cannot be the measure of success. GB Grid will have to choose projects transparently, avoid duplicating private investment and show that public capital lowers the total cost of the transition. Its arrival still changes the balance of the system. For the first time since privatisation, a publicly owned operator will be able to build alongside the companies whose plans ministers currently influence mainly through regulation. (Guardian)

Burnham is placing the grid inside a broader promise to restore public control over energy, water and housing after four decades of privatisation. He wants British energy costs to move into line with other European countries within ten years. The target is ambitious because the price on a household bill reflects gas markets, generation, networks, taxes and policy costs at once. Public ownership of one new company will address the bottleneck in wires and substations; it will not by itself insulate consumers from a global fuel shock. The government is already living with that distinction as war involving Iran pushes up oil products and a threatened American diesel-export ban exposes Britain’s dependence on foreign refineries. A durable energy policy has to join the two timescales: relief from today’s cost pressure and construction that reduces the next one. (Guardian, BBC)

The speech will also return to Burnham’s pledge for a national care service and the question of how to pay for it. Chancellor John Healey has declined to guarantee the pensions triple lock beyond the next election; the policy is forecast to cost £15.5bn by 2030, and any review would open one of the hardest arguments in British politics. That makes GB Grid the more immediate test of the government’s approach. It has a defined capital pot, a visible infrastructure problem and an outcome that can be measured in months saved on each connection. If the public operator begins projects quickly and helps renewable power reach customers, Burnham will have evidence for his claim that the state can shape markets rather than simply compensate people when they fail. If it becomes another layer of approval, the queues will remain and the larger case for public control will weaken with them. (Guardian)

News & Economy

Brazil’s election turns on a false story, a real betting ban and an empty chair

Brazil enters the final week before Sunday’s first round with Lula ahead of Flávio Bolsonaro by five points in two separate polls and no secure path to a first-round victory. Quaest places the president on 39% and Bolsonaro on 34%; BTG/Nexus has them at 42% and 37%. A Quaest runoff scenario is tied at 42%. Beneath those headline numbers, Lula has recovered support among groups that had been drifting away. His backing among Catholics rose from 43% to 48% in a week, and among evangelical voters from 23% to 27%. Bolsonaro’s support among women fell from 32% to 29%, while the overall share of undecided voters dropped from 8% to 5%. Each movement sits close to a poll’s margin of error. Their common direction has nevertheless tightened the question of who can speak to a small group of lower-middle-income women making a late decision. (Folha, Folha, Meio)

Two events have cut through. A false claim spread through WhatsApp and social networks that Flávio Bolsonaro planned to remove Our Lady of Aparecida as Brazil’s patron saint. The title comes from a 1980 law; archived proposals to change it were not his, and he had made no such promise. Bolsonaro then accused people inside the national bishops’ conference of helping the PT spread the story, deepening the dispute with a Catholic institution. Lula’s government, meanwhile, issued a provisional measure banning online betting, casino games and football wagers. Users have until 5 October to withdraw balances. State finance officials estimate that Brazilian families lost R$62.5bn to betting last year, making the decision immediately legible in household budgets even as operators prepare legal action and football clubs count the loss of sponsorship money. (Meio, Meio)

Lula now risks giving away some of that advantage. He plans to skip Thursday night’s Globo debate and appear on a podcast at the same time, against the advice of campaign coordinators. Bolsonaro says he will attend, and the broadcaster intends to leave Lula’s lectern visible and empty. The image has history: Lula missed Globo’s final first-round debate in 2006 while expecting to win outright, finished on 48.6% of valid votes and had to contest a runoff. This year both leading candidates have avoided every debate so far, and the Record event was cancelled. Thursday is therefore the only national confrontation left before the vote. Many choices are settled, but 21% of women told Quaest in August that they decide during the final week. Lula’s absence would allow every rival to address that audience without an answer from the candidate leading the race. (Meio, Folha)

A threatened US diesel ban reaches every British delivery route

Britain is asking Washington to preserve diesel exports after Donald Trump said he was seriously considering a ban designed to hold down prices for American consumers before the midterm elections. The UK buys about a third of its imported diesel from the United States. Chancellor John Healey says the government is discussing the proposal with US officials and preparing domestic stocks in case it goes ahead. The timing is already painful: the average British diesel price reached a record 199.33p a litre on Monday, just above the previous peak after Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022. Petrol has climbed to 174.23p. Seven months of war involving Iran have disrupted both production and transport, while Russia’s war continues to constrain supply. (BBC, Guardian)

The pump price travels quickly into food, construction, parcels and public services because lorries and much of the commercial fleet run on diesel. A US ban would redirect buyers towards the same limited cargoes and raise wholesale prices beyond Britain. Healey is preparing an October budget while the fuel-duty freeze is due to end in December; current plans add 3p in January and another 2p in March. Those increases were set before diesel approached £2 a litre. The Treasury can defer duty, release stocks or offer temporary support, but none creates refinery capacity. Britain’s exposure is the result of years in which domestic refining shrank while demand for diesel remained substantial. The immediate negotiation is with Washington. The longer task is to reduce the number of essential journeys whose cost depends on a decision made for American elections. (BBC)

A vast screen-time review finds a real link, with important limits

An umbrella review covering more than 2.8 million children in 64 countries has linked greater screen exposure with lower overall school achievement, poorer language development and more emotional, peer and behavioural difficulties. The work, published in JAMA Pediatrics, combined previous large reviews across phones, televisions and other devices and included social media, gaming, streaming and video calls. The associations were comparable in size to several established influences on childhood outcomes, including family wealth, parenting behaviour and premature birth. Screen use is also a factor families and policymakers can change, which gives the findings practical weight as Britain considers restrictions on social media for under-16s. (Guardian)

The review cannot establish that screens caused every poorer outcome, and the average associations were small. It also lacked enough consistent data to distinguish a FaceTime call from a late-night feed, a homework video from a game or passive viewing from creative work. Specific analyses found no clear link with maths, reading or science results, though those subjects were among the least studied. That leaves a useful, measured conclusion. Duration matters across a very large population, while content, timing, age and the activity displaced by a screen probably matter too. A household response can start with sleep, shared meals, conversation and schoolwork rather than treating every illuminated rectangle as the same exposure. (Guardian)

Technology

AMD spends $8.2bn to bring Fei-Fei Li’s world models inside

AMD is acquiring World Labs in an all-stock transaction worth about $8.2bn, bringing one of AI’s best-known researchers and a young specialist in spatial intelligence into the chipmaker. Fei-Fei Li co-founded the laboratory in 2024, and investors valued it at $1bn within months. Its first commercial product, Marble, generates interactive three-dimensional environments from a prompt. The deal is expected to close by the end of this year. Li will report to AMD chief executive Lisa Su as executive vice-president and chief scientist, while the World Labs team continues its model research. AMD gains a direct view of how emerging systems use compute, memory and software; World Labs gains the hardware organisation and distribution needed to turn research into widely deployed tools. (Verge)

The price shows how aggressively chip companies are buying model expertise. Nvidia announced a deal of nearly $13bn for Hugging Face earlier this month. AMD’s wager is more specific: three-dimensional world generation could support robotics, design, simulation, games and systems that reason about physical space. Those workloads may demand a different mix of processors and software from the language models that shaped the first phase of the AI boom. By putting researchers beside its hardware teams, AMD can design for those demands before they become a mature market. It is paying more than eight times World Labs’ early valuation for that advantage, so Marble’s current revenue is only part of the calculation. The larger asset is the chance to influence what comes after text-and-image assistants while AMD is still trying to narrow Nvidia’s lead in AI infrastructure. (Verge)

AI gives small-time hackers scale that small institutions cannot match

AI coding agents are lowering the amount of expertise and time required to search for weak systems, write convincing messages and run attacks across many targets. A single operator can now attempt work that once needed a skilled group, which changes the economics of cybercrime. In one case disclosed in 2025, a criminal used Claude Code to help steal data and pursue extortion across healthcare providers, emergency services, religious organisations and government bodies in a single month. Large technology companies are using advanced defensive models to find flaws at the same speed. Access to the strongest tools is generally restricted to major companies, critical infrastructure providers and important open-source projects, leaving local hospitals, community banks, councils, shops and charities with older software, smaller teams and far less money. (Verge)

The imbalance matters most where an outage immediately harms people. Hospitals depend on connected records for allergies, medicines, imaging, billing and bed management; ransomware at one facility can divert patients and lengthen waits across a whole region. A small Alabama nonprofit, Vivian’s Door, spent about $3,000 and lost three working days after fraudulent fundraising emails were sent from its systems. It could repair the vulnerability it found, with no way to know whether another remained. Basic security work therefore grows more valuable as offensive tools improve: supported software, multifactor authentication, tested backups, limited user permissions and a rehearsed response plan. AI may eventually make strong defence cheaper, but today its benefits are concentrated among organisations that already have specialist teams. The institutions with the most fragile budgets are absorbing the first wave of automated scale. (Verge)

Starship completes its first trip around Earth

SpaceX’s Starship reached orbit for the first time on its 14th test flight, a milestone for the enormous vehicle NASA hopes will carry astronauts towards the Moon. The 120-metre rocket lifted off from Starbase in Texas at 1.48pm BST. An engine failed during ascent, briefly putting the orbital burn in doubt, but controllers judged the vehicle safe to continue. Starship climbed to about 275km and the upper stage accelerated towards the roughly 28,000km/h needed to remain in orbit. It deployed 26 next-generation Starlink satellites, including three with cameras trained on its heat shield, while the Super Heavy booster returned to a controlled offshore landing in the Gulf of Mexico. (Guardian, BBC)

The original plan called for six orbits over almost ten hours and a Pacific splashdown west of Chile. SpaceX brought the ship home after about three hours instead, aiming for the north Pacific. Four flaps controlled the descent, the vehicle turned upright above the water and then disappeared in a ball of flame after the planned landing sequence. That ending still leaves a successful orbital flight, satellite deployment and controlled re-entry after several tests ended in explosions or loss of control. Reuse remains the decisive challenge. Starship’s promise rests on flying the booster and ship again with short turnaround times, and this mission did not attempt to catch the booster in the launch tower’s mechanical arms. The flight nevertheless moves the programme from proving that its stages can survive pieces of the journey towards demonstrating a complete launch system. (Guardian, BBC)

Apple faces a record patent award over the Taptic Engine

A federal jury in San Diego has ordered Apple to pay more than $5.7bn to Taction Technology for infringing two patents covering haptic feedback. Taction filed the case in 2021 and argued that Apple’s Taptic Engine, introduced with the first Apple Watch and later spread across the company’s products, used its inventions without a licence. Apple won an earlier stage in 2023, before a federal appeals court revived the case in August 2025. The company says its technology works differently and will appeal. Taction’s lawyers describe the award as the largest ever in a US patent-infringement case. The verdict is therefore the opening figure in another legal phase, with the final amount and liability still exposed to post-trial motions and appeal. (MacMagazine, 9to5Mac)

Claude Sonnet gets faster while keeping its price

Anthropic has released Claude Sonnet 5.5 across its own products, developer platform, Amazon Web Services, Google Cloud and Microsoft Azure. The company says it generates responses more than 30% faster than Sonnet 5 and can lower the cost of many completed tasks by up to 30% because it uses fewer tokens. List prices remain $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens. The model is aimed at daily coding, documents, presentations, spreadsheets, visual work and computer use, and it brings cyber safeguards and fallback systems closer to those in the larger Opus 5.5. That pairing is significant: capable models are becoming cheaper to operate at the same moment their coding and security skills require stronger controls. (MacMagazine, 9to5Mac)

Manchester City’s verdict exists, while its consequences remain hidden

Manchester City have been found guilty by an independent commission on the vast majority of the Premier League’s 115 charges over financial rules, yet the judgment and any punishment remain unpublished. Rival clubs received a broad outline last Thursday and are pressing the league to release the full decision. City chief executive Ferran Soriano addressed the European Football Clubs board on Monday and told members that the club rejects the ruling and expects ultimately to clear its name. The intervention surprised a group that included senior representatives of Manchester United and Everton. Eight years have passed since the underlying allegations first surfaced, and Soriano warned that the process could continue for considerably longer. (Guardian, BBC)

The delay leaves the competition in an awkward gap between liability and consequence. Players, supporters, commercial partners and other clubs know the commission has ruled, but cannot read which transactions or disclosures it accepted, how it treated each season or what sporting sanction follows. City can appeal, extending uncertainty over titles, qualification and the league table. The Premier League also has to explain why a case central to confidence in its financial system took so long and why even a guilty verdict emerged through private briefings before public reasons. Publication will not end the dispute. It will at least let everyone argue from the same document, separate established breaches from rejected allegations and judge whether the proposed penalty fits both the conduct and the years consumed in reaching it. (Guardian, Guardian)

Four Stevie Wonder songs emerge after half a century

Stevie Wonder has released four previously unheard recordings from the sessions for Songs in the Key of Life, extending the 1976 double album on its 50th anniversary. It’s Easier carries a bossa nova sway; My Life Story of Love follows a blossoming relationship; I Can See the Sun in Late December is a slow jazz ballad; and I’m Into Livin’ turns towards funk, with backing vocals from Thelma Houston, Deniece Williams and Shirley Brewer. Wonder wrote and produced all four, with Susaye Greene sharing a credit on My Life Story of Love. Original engineers John Fischbach and Gary O Adante returned to mix the recordings. Wonder begins an arena tour performing the album in full on 13 October in Birmingham, followed by European dates, London, Manchester and Glasgow, then five US cities. (Guardian)

The Witcher 3 remaster treats an old game like a living platform

CD Projekt releases The Witcher 3: Wild Hunt — Remastered today for PC, PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X and S, and Nintendo Switch 2. Anyone who owns a physical or digital edition can upgrade free within the same platform family. The update rebuilds lighting, materials, vegetation and visual effects; adds a native multithreaded DirectX 12 renderer; resets and reorganises the skill tree; improves monster behaviour, movement and riding; and expands photo mode with characters, props, lights, weather and camera controls. Console players gain cross-platform mod support through mod.io. PC players get path tracing, DLSS 4.5, FSR 4 and XeSS 2.0, alongside higher system requirements. (IGN)

The most thoughtful addition may be a new accessibility preset called Bard’s Ballad. It can reduce cognitive load through options such as vitality regeneration, unlimited breath and durability, ignored item weight, faster boats and a toggle for Witcher senses. Players can enable the pieces separately, preserving choice rather than defining a single simplified mode. The update also groups nearby loot, lets Geralt collect it without dismounting Roach, condenses quest notifications and adds a quick way to leave combat. A technically extravagant remaster could easily become a showcase for path-traced reflections aimed at expensive PCs. Here, the same release gives console players mods and offers returning players a gentler route through a game that can demand well over 100 hours. That combination makes the free upgrade feel closer to long-term stewardship than another sale of familiar scenery. (IGN)
