LEAD STORY

US strikes and a Hormuz toll pushed the Gulf crisis into markets

The US launched a third consecutive night of strikes on Iran after Donald Trump reinstated a maritime blockade and said ships would be charged for safe passage through the Strait of Hormuz. US Central Command said the strikes were aimed at degrading Iran's ability to attack civilians and commercial shipping in the strait. (The Guardian)

Brent crude rose 5% to $79.37 a barrel as the blockade threat reached the trading screen. The UK also moved against Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps under national-security powers, banning support for the group in a step officials said came close to proscription. (The Guardian, The Guardian)

NEWS

Burnham secured the Labour leadership before the contest could form

Andy Burnham is on course to become prime minister after receiving the backing of 349 Labour MPs, including all eligible members of Keir Starmer's cabinet. The extra nominations made it impossible for any rival to reach the 81 nominations needed to enter the leadership contest. (The Guardian, BBC)

Counter-terror police took over the Widdecombe investigation

Counter-terrorism police are now leading the investigation into Ann Widdecombe's death after new information and evidence changed the case. A 28-year-old man from Rotherham remains in custody on suspicion of murder, and the home secretary told MPs the suspect was not known to Prevent. (The Guardian, BBC)

Twelve were arrested over a suspected far-right threat in Suffolk

Police arrested 12 people over a suspected far-right threat against an Islamic event at Shrubland Hall in Suffolk. Three were detained on suspicion of conspiracy to murder, and counter-terror officers described the investigation as linked to extreme rightwing terrorism. (The Guardian)

Europe moved toward a shared missile shield

Ukraine and nine countries including the UK, Germany and France agreed to build a shared European ballistic-missile defence programme. The group said Kyiv's wartime experience would shape the system as leaders met Volodymyr Zelenskyy in Paris. (The Guardian)

A learning-disability mortality report demanded national attention

More than half of adults with learning disabilities do not live past 65, a new report found. The government called the findings stark, while campaigners said the figures should command headline-level attention across health and social care. (BBC)

A British survivor described the Almería wildfire deaths

Malcolm Timbrell survived a Spanish wildfire in a car while his wife and friends are believed to have died after trying to run to safety. The Almería disaster remained a story of individual choices made in minutes as flames tore through the village. (BBC)

Brazil's emendas inquiry widened around Valdemar and Cunha

Brazil's federal police accused Valdemar Costa Neto and Eduardo Cunha of using congressional amendments despite holding no mandate, with investigators pointing to alleged authorisation from the lower house presidency for an aide to direct funds. A Transparency Brasil report also said the chamber concealed the authorship of R$1.3bn in committee amendments, reviving the logic of the secret budget fight. (Canal Meio, Folha Poder, Folha Poder)

Moraes cut Flávio Bolsonaro off from his father

Justice Alexandre de Moraes barred Flávio Bolsonaro from visiting Jair Bolsonaro for 90 days during the former president's house arrest, a period running beyond Brazil's first electoral round. The order landed after a letter reaffirming Flávio as political voice and presidential candidate, while polling kept Lula and Flávio in a technical tie at 47% to 44% in a second-round test. (Folha Poder, Folha Poder)

Volkswagen prepared another 50,000 job cuts

Volkswagen chief executive Oliver Blume confirmed plans to cut 50,000 jobs after the supervisory board rejected factory closures in Germany. The proposal was presented to staff as the largest realignment in the company's history, with European carmaking still under pressure from China, costs and weaker demand. (The Guardian)

Britain's grid operator faced an external legal inquiry

Great Britain's energy system operator brought in an independent legal firm to examine whistleblower claims that staff concealed blackout-risk concerns during June's heatwave. The allegation turns grid stress from a technical warning into a governance test for a government-owned operator. (The Guardian)

The Swiss deal gave Britons faster border lanes

Britons will be able to use e-gates in Switzerland after a £5.2bn trade deal kept terms in place for goods including medicines, cars, art and jewellery. Roaming charges are also being scrapped, and exit checks at Zurich airport are expected to lead the border changes. (The Guardian)

TECHNOLOGY

Apple's public betas put Siri AI in users' hands

Apple released public betas for iOS 27 and its other major operating systems, with Siri AI as the central test. The beta cycle puts a more capable assistant on iPhone and Apple Watch, while macOS 27 Golden Gate arrives with toned-down Liquid Glass interface changes. (The Verge, The Verge, MacStories, The Verge)

The Apple-OpenAI lawsuit moved into personnel claims

Apple's trade-secret lawsuit against OpenAI continued with new detail about former employees and alleged manoeuvres around a planned hardware device. The case keeps the companies' AI relationship in a legal lane as Apple tries to protect hardware plans and OpenAI pushes further beyond software. (MacMagazine)

Apple's hardware business had a stronger quarter

Counterpoint and Omdia data pointed to record iPhone sales in the second quarter of 2026. Other Apple items showed the company's operating perimeter: an Intel arrangement that may have softened tariff exposure, easier pairing for Meta wearables in the EU, and a new SigScalr acquisition around app observability. (MacMagazine, MacMagazine, MacMagazine, MacMagazine)

Platform cleanups ran beside platform retreats

Microsoft began testing a cleaner Windows 11 search menu without recommended content and ads, while OnePlus and Oppo appeared to be moving toward an exit from the US and European markets. Security researchers also identified CrashStealer, a macOS malware strain found inside an apparently legitimate app. (The Verge, The Verge, MacMagazine)

CULTURE

Sam Neill died at 78

Sam Neill, the New Zealand actor whose career ran from The Piano to Jurassic Park and Peaky Blinders, died aged 78. His death turned attention back to a screen presence that could carry romance, villainy and blockbuster wonder without overwhelming the films around him. (The Guardian, The Guardian)

Nolan's Odyssey became a format pilgrimage

Christopher Nolan's The Odyssey became a destination release before opening week, with fans travelling to see it in one of 41 cinemas equipped for Imax 1570 projection. The film was shot entirely on the high-resolution format, requiring heavy cameras, frequent film changes and new soundproofing work to record dialogue. (The Guardian)

Disney's live-action Moana opened below scale

Disney's live-action Moana remake took $43m in North America and $95m globally in its opening weekend against a reported $250m production budget. The result left a No 1 release looking smaller than the franchise machinery built around it. (The Guardian)

The Louvre heist inquiry kept finding excess

Two men suspected in the €88m Louvre crown-jewels heist told investigators the alleged mastermind believed the group could have taken more. The burglary damaged Empress Eugénie's gem-encrusted crown and forced the museum into a public security reckoning. (The Guardian)

SPORTS

England waited on Rice before Argentina

Declan Rice is expected to recover from illness and start England's World Cup semi-final against Argentina. Thomas Tuchel substituted him at half-time against Norway after three days in bed, leaving England's midfield fitness as the last major selection question before Atlanta. (The Guardian, BBC)

Argentina gave England a Messi problem with weaknesses attached

Argentina's route to the World Cup semi-final still runs through Lionel Messi, but England's staff also have evidence of congestion and pressure slowing the champions. Switzerland crowded central lanes in the quarter-final and forced Argentina to rely on a late Julián Álvarez moment. (The Guardian)

Spain and France carried the other semi-final into leadership tests

Rodri put Spain's confidence on record before facing France, while Didier Deschamps reached the week with a redesigned attack and a chance to become only the second manager to win two men's World Cups. The other semi-final is now a contest between Spain's control and France's remade front line. (The Guardian, The Guardian)

The Premier League coaching carousel reached Liverpool and Chelsea

Andoni Iraola began his Liverpool tenure by stressing connection with the club and city after three years at Bournemouth. At Chelsea, Xabi Alonso arrived with trophies on the walls and the familiar Stamford Bridge question of whether a decorated manager can survive the churn. (The Guardian, The Guardian)

LIFESTYLE

Extreme cleaning became a care job in public view

Bea Elton has built a large audience by cleaning homes overwhelmed by mould, waste and neglect, but the work begins with trust. The homes she enters can take months of relationship-building before the protective suits, masks and hard physical labour begin. (The Guardian)

Yorkshire Water's mistaken payment found the wrong account

Yorkshire Water sent thousands of pounds to a customer's bank account by mistake and initially told the household to keep the money. The error was later traced to wages owed to company staff, turning a consumer oddity into an internal payroll failure. (The Guardian)

Parkrun's ordinary appeal kept outlasting performance culture

Parkrun's pull remained its weekly simplicity: free 5km events where speed is secondary to showing up. The habit works as community infrastructure, with modest personal records sitting beside volunteering and repeat attendance. (The Guardian, The Guardian)

Television turned the property ladder into a stunt course

Channel 4's How to Trick Your Way onto the Property Ladder put the housing crisis through absurd tests, from hypnotising estate agents to asking whether prison cells and grave plots expose the limits of affordability. The jokes stayed close to the central fact: younger buyers are still searching for any route into ownership that feels possible. (The Guardian)
