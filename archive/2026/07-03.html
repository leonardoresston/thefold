NEWS

Britain apologised for forced adoption, then faced a live safeguarding test

Starmer's formal apology acknowledged the state's role in taking babies from unmarried mothers between 1949 and 1976, with new money promised for records access and family reconnection. The news was historic, but it sat beside a present-tense safeguarding story: the NCA said online forums were helping offenders arrange drug-facilitated sexual assaults, often inside relationships where victims trusted the perpetrator. The hard point is continuity. Past harm required a state apology; present harm is asking whether agencies can identify dispersed, private networks before victims are left to prove what happened to them. (The Guardian, The Guardian, BBC)

Burnham's inheritance became a fiscal credibility exam

McSweeney's BBC interview put language around Labour's first problem in office: it had not moved quickly enough because it had not prepared deeply enough. Burnham then had to defend his economic discipline while promising cost-of-living relief, possible business-rates changes, cheaper utilities and free bus travel for 16- to 18-year-olds. The defence plan kept pressing from the other side, with both BBC and Guardian coverage framing the next prime minister's first weeks around funding choices already made elsewhere. (BBC, The Guardian, BBC)

Security moved to the copy buffer and the password prompt

MacMagazine reported Opera's Paste Protect feature, designed to block potentially malicious commands copied from web pages, and later covered Jamf research on PamStealer malware that uses macOS authentication flows to capture a system password. The useful signal is practical: attacks are moving through small, trusted actions, so browsers, operating systems and password tools are trying to put friction exactly where users are least likely to notice risk.
