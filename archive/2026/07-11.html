LEAD STORY

Ann Widdecombe's death became a murder investigation

A man was arrested on suspicion of murder after Ann Widdecombe, the former Conservative minister and MP, was found dead with serious injuries at her home in Haytor, Devon. The 78-year-old's death had first been announced without an immediate suggestion of suspicious circumstances, before police opened a murder inquiry. (The Guardian, BBC)

The investigation turned a day of Westminster tributes into a live criminal case in Dartmoor. Political figures across parties marked Widdecombe's combative public career while Devon and Cornwall police worked through the circumstances of her death. (The Guardian, BBC)

NEWS

Spain counted the dead from a fast-moving wildfire

At least 12 people were killed and 23 remained missing after a wildfire tore through part of Almeria during Spain's second heatwave of the summer. Four Britons were thought to be among those who died while trying to flee the blaze, leaving Andalusia with one of its deadliest fire emergencies in years. (The Guardian)

Burnham prepared to inherit No 10 and tour Labour's danger zones

Andy Burnham was expected to become prime minister within two weeks and was planning a summer tour of seats where Labour support has weakened. The handover comes with welfare, defence, living costs and geopolitics already waiting on the desk, making the first trip around the country an early governing test. (The Guardian, The Guardian)

Reform's funding pressure moved to police interviews

George Cottrell, an ally of Nigel Farage, and his mother Fiona Cottrell were understood to have been interviewed under criminal caution by Scotland Yard. The interviews formed part of an investigation into donations to Reform UK before the 2024 general election, at a moment when large gifts and political funding rules are again under parliamentary pressure. (The Guardian, The Guardian)

A Bedfordshire murder case reached South Africa

Ndodana Mkhanyisi Tshuma was charged with murdering his wife and two young daughters after being arrested in a Johannesburg suburb. The Crown Prosecution Service authorised three murder charges after an international search, moving the Bedfordshire case back toward UK courts. (The Guardian, BBC)

Brazil's emendas investigation closed in on Valdemar Costa Neto

Justice Flavio Dino ordered R$119m in assets blocked from PL president Valdemar Costa Neto after federal police said he had helped direct parliamentary amendments despite holding no congressional mandate. Valdemar denied wrongdoing and said the decision criminalised politics, while investigators identified Mariangela Fialek, known as Tuca, as a central figure in the alleged scheme. (Folha Poder, Folha Poder, Folha Poder)

The Banco Master affair widened into an influence case

Federal police scrutiny of Banco Master reached Thiago Miranda, a publicist tied to campaigns supporting the bank. Investigators described payments of up to R$2m to influencers who attacked the Central Bank and the production of dossiers against critics including journalist Malu Gaspar and Itau chief Milton Maluhy Filho. (Canal Meio, Canal Meio)

Reeves put AI retraining on the City agenda

Rachel Reeves prepared a City skills compact that would commit financial firms including Barclays and Lloyds to retraining thousands of workers for AI-driven changes in finance. The plan puts automation risk and worker redeployment into the same Mansion House conversation as growth and competitiveness. (The Guardian)

easyJet attracted a second US bidder

Apollo entered the bidding for easyJet and topped Castlelake's offer, turning the airline's exit from the London market into a competitive auction. The approach gave shareholders a higher price while renewing concern that undervalued UK-listed companies remain vulnerable to overseas buyers. (The Guardian)

The High Pay Centre prepared to close

The High Pay Centre, created in 2011 to track executive pay and inequality, faced closure after years of work on the gap between chief executives and ordinary employees. Its disappearance would leave one fewer institutional voice measuring boardroom rewards at a time of renewed pressure over wages and corporate accountability. (The Guardian)

TECHNOLOGY

Apple sued OpenAI over hardware secrets

Apple filed a lawsuit alleging that OpenAI used former Apple employees to obtain trade secrets for a planned hardware device. The case also named IO Products, Jony Ive's hardware startup, turning the companies' uneasy AI relationship into a direct fight over designs, people and confidential material. (The Guardian, The Verge, BBC, MacMagazine)

The FCC moved against DJI workarounds

The US Federal Communications Commission began targeting companies suspected of helping DJI technology reach the American market under different brands. The action follows scrutiny of Xtra and Skyrover products and shows the drone ban moving from law and procurement rules into enforcement against supply-chain substitutes. (The Verge)

SK Hynix rode the AI memory boom to Wall Street

SK Hynix, a major supplier of memory chips for Nvidia systems, made a Wall Street debut that opened at $170 a share and raised $26.5bn. The listing gave the AI infrastructure trade another public-market marker, this time in the less glamorous but essential layer of high-end RAM. (The Verge)

Spotify gave listeners more control over Release Radar

Spotify added controls for Release Radar, letting listeners narrow the weekly playlist by genre, new-to-you artists and other filters. The change turns one of the platform's most important discovery products into a more adjustable recommendation surface. (The Verge, MacMagazine)

CULTURE

A pianist lost his discrimination case against the MSO

Classical pianist Jayson Gillham lost his case against the Melbourne Symphony Orchestra over the cancellation of a 2024 concert after remarks about Gaza. The judgment leaves arts institutions with another test case on speech, contracts and political statements from performers. (The Guardian)

Lord Woodbine returned to the Beatles story

A new BBC drama will put Harold Phillips, better known as Lord Woodbine, into the story of the Beatles' Hamburg years. The six-part series gives a larger role to the mentor whose influence has often sat at the edge of the band's early mythology. (The Guardian)

Micheal Ward was cleared of rape and sexual assault charges

Top Boy actor Micheal Ward was acquitted of two counts of rape, two counts of assault by penetration and one count of sexual assault after a 10-day trial. The charges related to a woman he met at a New Year's Day party in London in 2023. (The Guardian)

Pokemon Go marked 10 years by finally staging the giant battle

Pokemon Go's 10th anniversary event in New York put nearly 2,000 players into a coordinated Mewtwo battle, delivering the kind of mass public play promised in the game's earliest pitch. The Times Square takeover made the mobile game's decade feel more like a civic spectacle than a nostalgia lap. (The Verge, The Guardian)

SPORTS

Spain joined France in the World Cup semi-finals

Mikel Merino scored in the 88th minute to send Spain past Belgium and into a World Cup semi-final against France. Belgium lost Thibaut Courtois to injury, and Spain again found a late tournament goal from the bench when the match was almost gone. (The Guardian, BBC)

France carried momentum and tension from the Morocco win

Kylian Mbappe's leadership remained central to France's run after the win over Morocco secured another semi-final place. Celebrations around the result also brought disorder on London's Edgware Road, where a police officer was hurt and four arrests were made. (The Guardian, The Guardian)

England trained key players before Norway

Marc Guehi, Declan Rice and Reece James all trained before England's World Cup quarter-final against Norway. The FA's bonus plan also came into view, with players and staff set to receive around half of the £38m Fifa prize money if England win the tournament. (BBC, The Guardian)

LIFESTYLE

Vape packaging moved closer to tobacco rules

UK ministers considered plain packaging for vapes, limits on device colours and tighter display rules in shops as part of an attempt to reduce their appeal to children. The plans would bring parts of the e-cigarette market closer to tobacco-style restrictions after data showed one in five teenagers had tried vaping. (The Guardian)

Heat advice turned practical again

Doctors cautioned that sunscreen discomfort is common and usually means changing formula while keeping protection in place. The advice landed during another hot spell in which daily routines, skin care and outdoor plans kept bending around summer heat. (The Guardian)
