Britain faces a diesel squeeze as Washington turns on its allies

Britain is negotiating with European allies over a release of emergency diesel stocks after Donald Trump threatened to stop American exports unless France and Germany draw down their own reserves. Ministers spoke on Thursday with the European Commission, Germany, France, Italy and Ireland, while EU governments prepared an urgent meeting with the commission and the International Energy Agency. The immediate American demand is aimed at lowering US fuel prices before November’s midterm elections. Its effect is to push the cost of that intervention towards Europe, where governments are already balancing expensive fuel against the need to keep a winter reserve. (Guardian, BBC)

The UK has more at stake than most of the continent. European refineries produce about 70% of the diesel used in the region, but Britain lacks enough refining capacity to cover domestic demand and bought roughly a third of its imported diesel from the US last year. A ban that included the UK would force suppliers to compete for cargoes elsewhere, lifting prices even if forecourts continued to receive fuel. Diesel reached a record average of 199.72p a litre on Wednesday, according to the RAC. That price runs through haulage, food distribution, farming, construction and public services as well as the family car, so another rise would spread rapidly across the economy. (Guardian)

The shortage begins with war. The US-Israel campaign against Iran has disrupted energy flows through the Strait of Hormuz and lifted Brent crude from about $72 a barrel in February to more than $101 on Thursday. Ukrainian attacks on Russian refineries have added pressure, prompting Moscow to extend its diesel export ban until the end of October. Washington is asking Europe to supply the market from stores held precisely for severe interruptions such as a prolonged Gulf conflict. Those tanks can calm prices now, but every barrel released reduces the protection available if shipping deteriorates during winter. The US energy secretary argues that the approaching harvest and heating season makes a coordinated release urgent. European governments have to decide how much insurance they can spend before they know how long the emergency will last. (Guardian, BBC)

The dispute also exposes a choice Britain has postponed. A diverse import network can cope with a refinery outage or a brief disruption; simultaneous political pressure, war and winter demand test the system at a different scale. The government says supplies remain resilient and has raised its concerns directly with Washington. Its talks with Europe now need to secure fuel while avoiding a rush that drives prices higher. A stock release will buy time. New refining capacity and secure shipping lanes require a longer answer. For households, the signal is already visible beside every pump. For ministers, the task is to use that time to keep essential users supplied, coordinate releases and decide how a country that depends on imported diesel protects itself when its largest supplier treats exports as election leverage. (Guardian)

Labour’s welfare overhaul puts disabled young people at the centre

Ministers are considering removing the health element of universal credit from claimants under 25 and replacing it with subsidised jobs, targeted employment support and mental-health provision. The payment has already been cut to £217 a month for most new claimants from next April; officials are examining whether a further change could include people who already receive it. A separate regime for younger claimants of personal independence payment is also under discussion as the government prepares to replace Pip with a new assessment of disability costs. People with the most severe conditions would be protected, though the boundaries and the treatment of everyone else remain undecided. The proposals await two autumn reviews and would require legislation next year. (Guardian)

The scale explains both the urgency and the danger. One million people aged 16 to 24 are outside education, employment or training, and about 184,000 receive the universal-credit health element. Half of young people who move on to disability benefits remain out of work 15 years later, according to the review led by Alan Milburn. Yet disability charities estimate that about half of households with a recipient under 22 are already in poverty, rising to more than nine in 10 if the payment disappeared. Employment help cannot cover rent, food and the extra daily costs of illness when suitable jobs are absent. Labour MPs defeated an earlier attempt to tighten Pip, and the government is now presenting investment and benefit reform as one package. The credible test will be whether support reaches a young person before income is withdrawn, and whether a job can accommodate the condition that brought them into the system. (Guardian)

Families are likely to see a quicker change in the government’s smaller consumer measures. Airlines are to be barred from charging for a child under 14 to sit beside a parent or accompanying adult, and passengers will be able to correct a booking-name error without a fee. Sitting together can currently add as much as £104 to a return journey for one parent and child. Ministers want the rules in force before next summer and plan to use the civil aviation bill now passing through Parliament. Airlines will still be able to sell a particular seat, but the basic act of keeping a family together will become part of the fare. (Guardian)

Tennessee stops executions after two lethal doses fail

Christa Pike remained alive and in critical condition on Thursday after Tennessee tried twice to execute her with pentobarbital. Staff spent an hour attempting to establish an intravenous line, her lawyer counted at least seven needles used in one arm, and witnesses were left behind a closed curtain after the second dose while Pike continued to breathe. She was taken to hospital for life-saving care. Governor Bill Lee has suspended every execution in the state for the rest of the year and ordered an independent review, though he has yet to say who will conduct it. Pike’s lawyers have asked him to commute her sentence and are considering further legal action once she can take part in the decision. (BBC, Guardian)

Pike was sentenced to death in 1996 for the torture and murder of 19-year-old Colleen Slemmer, committed when Pike was 18. Slemmer’s mother travelled to witness the execution and described the failed procedure as a mess. The state prison department maintains that staff followed the approved protocol, including the second dose, and says that protocol allowed no further procedure once it had been given. This is Tennessee’s second failed execution in 2026: Tony Carruthers received a one-year reprieve in May after staff could not find a vein. The latest failure leaves a prisoner grievously injured by an execution the state could not complete, a victim’s family without the finality they were promised and a protocol that has now broken down twice in five months. A review can establish the sequence. The harder question is whether the state can plausibly promise that another attempt will avoid the same suffering. (BBC, Guardian)

Brazil enters its final campaign weekend with 17% still movable

Lula holds 45% of valid first-round votes against Flávio Bolsonaro’s 40% in the latest Datafolha survey before Sunday’s election. The calculation excludes blank, spoiled and undecided responses, as the electoral court will when it declares the result. Lula remains short of the 50% plus one vote required to win immediately. In a possible second round, the president leads by 48% to 45% among all respondents, a technical tie inside the poll’s two-point margin of error. The survey covered 2,506 voters in 152 municipalities between Tuesday and Thursday. It also found that 17% could still change their choice, 13% were undecided when asked without a list and almost one voter in five did not know their candidate’s number. Turnout has been close to 80% in recent elections, leaving abstention capable of moving a close result as much as late persuasion. (Folha, Meio)

The two leading candidates each carry a rejection rate of 45%, giving smaller shifts unusual weight. Lula has gained among women and Catholics after banning online betting and benefiting from a false claim that Bolsonaro would strip Our Lady of Aparecida of her status as Brazil’s patron saint. Bolsonaro has strengthened his lead among evangelical voters. Economic help has also entered the final stretch: Lula raised Bolsa Família by 15%, although recipients will receive the money only after the first round. His government is rated bad or terrible by 41% and good or excellent by 35%, while personal approval and disapproval are effectively level. These numbers describe an electorate choosing under pressure, with neither candidate attracting a broad consensus. The weekend will decide whether Lula’s narrow advantage becomes a first-round victory, a second-round contest or another disputed argument about legitimacy. (Folha, Folha)

France’s school revolt spreads from broken buildings to the streets

Protests over crowded classes, missing teachers, long timetables and decaying schools have expanded from the Paris region into about 900 demonstrations and blockades across France. More than 160 high schools and 30 university campuses were closed or obstructed on Thursday. Nearly 3,000 pupils have been detained since the action began, including 1,949 in one day, while more than 300 police officers and dozens of firefighters, teachers and students have been injured. Fires damaged a school in Nantes badly enough to close it for a month, a teacher in Marseille was splashed with petrol and transport was disrupted in Strasbourg. Prime minister Sébastien Lecornu called a crisis cabinet meeting as some schools prepared to move classes online. (Guardian)

The violence can obscure the ordinary conditions that brought pupils out: 35 students in a room, too few toilets, leaking buildings and classrooms that became unbearable during summer heatwaves. Teachers have been threatened at some blockades, while others have stood between their pupils and riot police. The interior ministry has opened an inquiry into allegations of excessive force against three students. Lecornu’s government accuses the radical-left France Unbowed of encouraging the movement; the party says it supports non-violent protest and condemns police brutality. The education minister will meet pupil representatives today. That conversation needs to separate those committing arson and assault from the far larger group asking why a wealthy country cannot provide a safe, staffed classroom. Closing buildings restores order for a day. Repairing trust requires an answer to the water leaks, heat and vacancies waiting inside. (Guardian)

Technology & Gaming

Microsoft moves Office inside Copilot and gives its agent a sober new job

Microsoft is rebuilding Copilot as the main surface for work, bringing Word, Excel and PowerPoint editing directly into the assistant alongside chat, coding and an autonomous agent called Autopilot. The design unifies the consumer and corporate versions under one product chief and abandons much of the friendly personal-assistant style introduced two years ago. An always-on desktop agent previously called Scout has been put into maintenance mode while its cloud version becomes Autopilot, a name and presentation chosen for enterprise buyers. The shift follows a cool response to the earlier $30-a-month Copilot add-on for Office. Microsoft now wants the assistant to contain Office itself, allowing a person to produce and edit a document without moving back to the traditional applications. (Verge)

The redesign acknowledges the obstacle that has slowed workplace agents: autonomy is useful to an employee and alarming to the administrator responsible for data, access and mistakes. Microsoft is leaning on the controls and corporate relationships it already has and concentrating its effort on business customers. At the same time, a US federal judge has dismissed lawsuits from Chegg and Penske Media claiming that Google coerced publishers into supplying material for AI search summaries while taking away their traffic. Judge Amit Mehta found that the publishers had an expectation of referrals, not an agreement that antitrust law could enforce, and pointed the wider economic problem towards lawmakers. Publishers continue to lose visits as AI answers more questions on the results page. The two developments meet at the same destination: the chat window is becoming the place where people read, write and act, while the applications and websites underneath it fight to preserve their value. (Verge, Verge)

Riot reveals the impossible audience behind 2XKO’s collapse

Riot Games now says its League of Legends fighting game 2XKO needed high hundreds of thousands, perhaps millions, of monthly active players to justify its cost. No established fighting game sustains an audience on that scale. Street Fighter 6, the genre’s largest current title, averages roughly 16,000 to 20,000 monthly players on Steam, with an unreported PlayStation audience alongside them. Riot began the project about eight years before Street Fighter 6 arrived, then lost time through an unclear strategy, divided management and a drawn-out path from closed beta to early access. The company will end new content after December, keep online play running and refund purchases. A beautiful game reached its public carrying a business model the genre could never comfortably support. (IGN)

Sony is moving in the opposite direction by extending a premium feature to hardware people already own. Quick Spectral Super Resolution brings AI image upscaling to the standard PlayStation 5, using a lighter model derived from Sony and AMD’s Project Amethyst work. It cannot preserve as much detail as the PSSR system in the $900 PS5 Pro, but early demonstrations show cleaner distant buildings, grass and fine objects than the base console’s conventional temporal upscaling. Marvel’s Wolverine and Ghost of Yotei receive the first patches, and Sony is making the tool available to every developer. If it performs across more games, a software update will narrow a hardware divide that had begun to make the original PS5 feel like the lower tier of its own generation. (IGN, Verge)

Bodhana Sivanandan reaches a grandmaster title from a booster seat

At 11, Bodhana Sivanandan has become the youngest woman grandmaster in chess history after crossing the required 2,300 rating at the Chess Olympiad in Uzbekistan. She played from a car booster seat, returned to her north London school for a design-and-technology lesson two days later and now has to catch up on a fortnight of classes. Her rise has been startling even by prodigy standards. She learned at five, beat her father within a year, defeated a former British champion at eight and in July became the youngest girl to beat a grandmaster rated above 2,600. Her new rating of 2,382 places her close to international master status; 2,500 would make her an unrestricted grandmaster. Achieving that within 11 months would set another world age record. (Guardian)

The numbers sit beside a recognisably young life. Bodhana has won three smartphones at tournaments but is still not allowed to use one, studies piano and violin, and fits eight hours of weekly chess tuition around school. She prefers the controlled endgames of the 1920s Cuban champion José Raúl Capablanca, yet has already tested herself in speed chess against world No 1 Magnus Carlsen. Her family plans tournaments around term and the schedules of her parents and younger twin sisters. That balance may be her quiet advantage. Records provide a deadline that adults can see; Bodhana describes a simpler ambition to become a grandmaster quickly because she enjoys the game. The booster seat will eventually disappear. The positional patience that has unsettled experienced players since she was six is likely to remain. (Guardian)

Manchester City’s verdict becomes an early test of political distance

Andy Burnham’s sympathy for Manchester City after an independent commission found more than 100 breaches of Premier League financial rules has forced Downing Street to clarify that punishment must follow if wrongdoing survives appeal. The commission concluded after a 42-day hearing and 7,000 pages of evidence that City used about £900m of disguised funding and sponsorship contracts that did not reflect their stated commercial basis. Burnham warned against forcing the owners to sell and praised their investment in Manchester. Several clubs fear that the prime minister’s long relationship with Abu Dhabi’s leadership could place political pressure around the appeal. Government officials are also unhappy that the judgment arrived without warning soon after his Labour conference speech. (Guardian, BBC)

The Treasury committee has asked HM Revenue & Customs whether it has sought the unredacted judgment and is examining any tax consequences. City’s appeal is expected to assign the funding of sponsorship agreements directly to the Abu Dhabi government; the commission rejected that account and found the explanation had been constructed later to conceal the scheme. The new Independent Football Regulator is monitoring the case and can assess the honesty and integrity of owners and executives, making this its first major encounter with political and commercial power. Burnham previously accused the league of unfairness when Everton received a points deduction, and his instinct to question the process is well established. The office he now holds changes the effect of that intervention. A regulator can protect the game only if clubs believe evidence, appeal and sanction remain insulated from whoever owns the team or knows the prime minister. (Guardian, BBC)
