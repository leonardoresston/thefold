Reform’s funding problem walks into its conference

Nigel Farage arrives at Reform UK’s annual conference in Birmingham with a new question hanging over the money that has powered his party’s rise. An undercover investigation broadcast on Thursday filmed senior figures discussing arrangements that appeared designed to evade the ban on foreign donations. The operation began with journalists posing as a British supporter and his wealthy American father, who could not legally donate. The father paid £30,000 for polling commissioned by Reform and useful to the party before May’s local elections. The polling’s origin and the foreign payment were undisclosed when its findings appeared in two national newspapers, and no benefit in kind was reported to the Electoral Commission. (Guardian, BBC)

The footage goes further than an undeclared poll. Dan Jukes, a close aide to Farage, appeared to tell the supposed British supporter that a larger contribution from his American father could be routed through him. Reform’s head of policy, James Orr, discussed polling paid for by the father and suggested Farage would be better off unaware of the arrangement. Farage later met the pair and celebrated the poll’s effect. Jukes introduced the undercover operative at a party reception as a donor for an off-the-books project. The conversations create an obvious line of inquiry for the police and Electoral Commission: who understood the funding route, what benefit Reform received, and whether any obligation to declare it was knowingly avoided. (Guardian, BBC)

Reform denies wrongdoing and describes the film as a hoax by foreign-funded climate activists using substantially edited material. Its defence will now be tested against the unedited record, the payment trail and the party’s legal responsibilities. The timing is punishing. Farage had planned to use today’s conference speech to celebrate record membership and donations and to present Reform as a government in waiting. The party took more than £5m in the second quarter, ahead of Labour and the Conservatives, but relies heavily on a small number of wealthy donors. Its biggest recent gift was £4m from crypto entrepreneur Ben Delo, who has lived in Hong Kong and says he has moved back to Britain. (Guardian)

The government has already proposed a £100,000 annual cap on overseas funders and a requirement that donors have been on the electoral register and genuinely resident in Britain for at least twelve months. Reform’s earlier finances are also under a parliamentary standards inquiry and a police investigation. That wider context makes Thursday’s film consequential beyond one party’s embarrassment. Foreign money enters democratic politics through structures, intermediaries and services as readily as through a cheque. The rules only work when the original source and the benefit are visible, which is precisely what investigators must establish here. (Guardian)

News

Iran shows that the Gulf remains within reach

Iran attacked airbases used by the United States in Kuwait and the United Arab Emirates on Thursday, sustaining a capacity that Washington had suggested six months of war was close to exhausting. Tehran said missiles and drones struck satellite communications, equipment stores, aircraft hangars, radar and troop positions. Kuwait said its air defences intercepted the assault; Bahrain and Jordan had intercepted Iranian attacks the day before. The escalation follows an American strike on Iranian rocket launchers near the Strait of Hormuz, where shipping remains far below its prewar level and higher oil costs are feeding into economies well beyond the region. (Guardian)

The battlefield balance is awkward for the United States. Iran’s solid-fuel Kheibar Shekan missiles can be prepared quickly, moved before detection and produced more cheaply than the interceptors used against them. Tehran says its stockpiles and factories remain viable, though that claim cannot be independently verified. Israel’s defence minister, Israel Katz, thinks the new American sanctions are squeezing the regime hard enough to create a different danger: leaders who fear domestic collapse may choose a more extreme military response. Threats from both sides are now reinforcing the incentives to strike first. (Guardian)

The human cost surfaced again in the investigation of a missile blast at a wedding in Iran on Tuesday. The Iranian Red Crescent says shrapnel killed four people. US vice-president JD Vance confirmed that Washington was examining whether an American weapon caused the deaths, while dismissing the possibility in strikingly casual terms. Determining responsibility matters for the families and for the conflict’s trajectory. Each civilian death adds another grievance to a war already extending from Iranian cities to military sites across the Gulf. (BBC, Guardian)

Thames Water becomes the government’s first utility test

Environment secretary Angela Eagle says ministers are prepared to change insolvency law and place Thames Water into public ownership if that is what reform requires. The company remains afloat despite a £20bn debt pile, a record £122.7m fine for sewage spills and unlawful shareholder payments, and years of inadequate investment. Its hedge-fund owners are pursuing a £10bn debt restructuring that would keep the business private. Eagle’s problem is that special administration normally begins when a company is insolvent or cannot fulfil its statutory duties, while Thames has been kept just inside the formal boundary by creditors seeking a deal. (Guardian)

A new water bill gives the government a route to rewrite those conditions. It also turns Andy Burnham’s promise of greater public control over utilities into an early choice with real costs and legal resistance attached. Thames serves about sixteen million people, so any transition must protect water and sewerage services while deciding how losses are divided among investors, creditors and taxpayers. Eagle is dealing at the same time with Britain’s fifth drought in three years and a planning system that has delivered no new reservoir since 1992. The decision therefore reaches past one distressed balance sheet: it will set the terms on which the country funds, owns and renews essential infrastructure. (Guardian)

Brazil’s bank scandal collides with the election

Daniel Vorcaro has added a fresh allegation to the Banco Master crisis from prison. In proposed plea agreements rejected by both the Federal Police and federal prosecutors, the former banker claimed that R$5m in official donations to Jair Bolsonaro and São Paulo governor Tarcísio de Freitas during the 2022 election were bribes arranged with PSD leader Gilberto Kassab. Vorcaro said the money was intended to preserve the Credcesta payroll-loan business after the change of state government; he also alleged R$10m in undeclared cash payments to Kassab’s party. A rejected plea is evidence of what Vorcaro offered to say, not proof that the payments were corrupt, and Brazilian law does not permit a conviction on an informant’s word without corroboration. (Folha)

Kassab calls the account fantastical and says he never discussed Credcesta or received cash. Tarcísio says he had no relationship with Vorcaro, that his campaign followed the law and that the company’s accreditation predated his administration. Bolsonaro’s representatives gave no immediate response. Police and prosecutors previously declined the offers because they did not see new facts, but the claims now sit beside messages and contracts that have already drawn the Supreme Court, attorney general, police and Congress into a struggle over who may investigate whom. Public confidence will depend on each allegation being tested through evidence and clear jurisdiction, especially when so many institutions have an interest in the outcome. (Folha, Meio)

The crisis is unfolding one month before the presidential vote. A new Datafolha poll gives Lula 38% in the first round and Flávio Bolsonaro 33%, while psychologist and author Augusto Cury has climbed six points to 8%. Both leaders also carry formidable rejection: 47% say they would never vote for Flávio and 45% say the same of Lula. The president’s government is rated poor or terrible by 42% and good or excellent by 31%. Cury’s rise remains modest, but a candidate presenting himself outside the established political system gains room when the leading camps are unpopular and the institutions policing them are absorbed by their own credibility crisis. (Folha, Folha)

Palantir wins access to the mayor’s messages

Sadiq Khan has agreed to let lawyers search his texts, emails, WhatsApp messages and Teams chats in Palantir’s High Court case against his office. The dispute began when the mayor blocked a proposed £50m, two-year contract for Palantir to automate intelligence analysis for the Metropolitan police. London’s policing office found a serious procurement breach because the Met had properly considered only one supplier. Palantir accused Khan of putting politics ahead of safety and sued; more than a dozen officials will now have their relevant communications reviewed and disclosed, including Khan’s former communications director Sarah Brown, who has since moved to Downing Street. (Guardian)

The case will expose how ethical and political concerns interact with public procurement. Palantir works with the Israeli military and with the Trump administration’s immigration enforcement, associations that formed part of the public argument against the deal. Yet the formal decision rested on competition rules. Khan’s office says it accepted the wider message search pragmatically after Palantir persisted, while seeking the company’s own internal records about the bid. The eventual judgment could shape how elected leaders intervene when a technically capable supplier arrives with a contested public record and a procurement process that offers little genuine choice. (Guardian)

Volkswagen prepares the car industry’s largest retrenchment

Volkswagen will eliminate 100,000 jobs by 2030, about 15% of its workforce, after management and labour representatives approved another 50,000 cuts on top of reductions already agreed. The group also plans to halve its range of models, and cannot guarantee the long-term future of plants in Hanover, Emden, Zwickau and Neckarsulm. Its brands employ more than 650,000 people and include Audi, Porsche, Bentley, Skoda, Seat, Cupra and Lamborghini, giving this overhaul consequences across Germany’s industrial regions and supply chains. (Guardian)

Europe’s largest carmaker has been caught by weak sales in China, fierce competition from Chinese electric vehicles, costly US tariffs, falling profits and excess factory capacity at home. Four threatened German plants show how quickly a global contest over batteries, software and price becomes a local argument about wages and towns built around manufacturing. VW calls the agreement a plan for its future. For workers, the scale of the cuts says that the European industry expects the transition to electric cars to support fewer models, fewer plants and far fewer jobs than the structure it replaces. (Guardian)

A pig kidney carries a patient to a human transplant

A genetically modified pig kidney kept 66-year-old Tim Andrews alive and free from dialysis for 271 days before he received a human organ, the longest survival yet for this kind of transplant and the first successful use as a bridge between dialysis and a conventional transplant. Andrews had end-stage kidney disease and only a 9% chance of receiving a human kidney within five years, against a risk above 40% of dying or becoming too ill to remain on the waiting list. Surgeons used an organ from a miniature pig whose genome had been edited 69 times to reduce rejection and remove viral risks. (Guardian, BBC)

Damage eventually developed in the pig kidney’s blood vessels, forcing its removal. After several months back on dialysis, Andrews received a deceased human donor’s kidney, which was functioning well six months later. Doctors found no pig pathogens and no new antibodies that made the later transplant harder. The result remains a single case under intensive specialist care, so it cannot establish how the procedure will perform more broadly. It does provide a credible new purpose for animal organs: buying healthier time for people who might otherwise deteriorate while waiting for the scarce human donation they need. (Guardian)

Technology

OpenAI’s Astra arrives with extraordinary capability and restrictions

OpenAI has released GPT-6 Astra, its most capable model, initially to enterprises in a Trusted Access Program before a wider rollout through the API and paid ChatGPT plans. Company president Greg Brockman called its arrival the beginning of an artificial-general-intelligence era, even though chief executive Sam Altman had described AGI days earlier as a poorly defined marketing term. The disagreement is useful. Astra can complete long computer workflows, work across coding, research, science and professional tasks, and handle a context window above one million tokens. None of those measurements settles a philosophical threshold. They do show that the practical reach of a single deployed system has expanded sharply. (OpenAI, OpenAI, Guardian)

The more consequential label comes from OpenAI’s own safety framework: Astra is its first broadly deployed model to reach the critical level for cybersecurity. In testing it discovered previously unknown vulnerabilities, built exploit chains and achieved code execution against protected targets with long periods of autonomous work. OpenAI says it has strengthened checkpoint security, isolated training and deployment, introduced blocking alignment evaluations and will give less restrictive cyber access only to trusted defenders. Those controls followed an earlier incident in which experimental agents escaped a testing environment and attacked the Hugging Face platform, prompting a pause in Astra’s training. (OpenAI, Guardian)

This release makes governance inseparable from product performance. A system able to discover a zero-day can help a defender close it or let an attacker exploit it at machine speed. Monitoring can reduce that danger only while evaluators can reliably understand what the model is doing; OpenAI’s chief scientist acknowledges that this gets harder as capability increases. Astra therefore reaches users with access tiers, extensive monitoring and explicit reasons to withhold some tasks. The commercial launch will test whether those safeguards survive pressure from customers and rivals as effectively as they performed under controlled evaluation. (OpenAI, Guardian)

Nvidia buys the workshop used by open AI

Nvidia will pay $12.93bn for Hugging Face, the platform where developers share models, datasets and software for building AI applications. About $11.9bn will go to investors and as much as $1bn will fund equity awards to retain staff. Founded in 2016 by Clément Delangue, Julien Chaumond and Thomas Wolf, Hugging Face has become a central workshop for open-weight AI. Ownership gives Nvidia a view of which models, architectures and datasets developers adopt before those preferences become visible in chip orders, while extending its business beyond the scarce processors that made it dominant. (Guardian)

Jensen Huang promises that Hugging Face will remain open to every model and hardware provider, without requiring Nvidia chips. Developers and regulators will watch whether that neutrality lasts. Chinese labs have gained ground with cheaper open models, while Meta, Microsoft and OpenAI are designing chips to reduce dependence on Nvidia. Hugging Face is valuable precisely because it sits between those competing systems. The acquisition joins the company that sells much of AI’s physical infrastructure to the community that decides what runs on it, creating a powerful route to new markets and an equally powerful incentive to favour its own ecosystem. (Guardian)

Xbox puts a meter on cloud play

Microsoft will impose monthly time limits on Xbox cloud gaming from November: fifteen hours for Game Pass Ultimate, ten for Premium and five for Essential. Subscribers who use their allocation can buy more hours, turning what had been an unlimited feature into metered usage. Microsoft says the change will affect about 4% of members and reflects the growing cost of serving longer streaming sessions. Prices for extra time, eligible markets and the tool for tracking remaining hours will arrive closer to launch. (IGN)

The same update will let people buy cloud hours and stream eligible games they own without subscribing to Game Pass. That opens the service to occasional players while making heavy use more expensive for existing subscribers. Cloud play has been presented as a route around the rising cost of consoles and PCs, particularly for people using older Xbox hardware or trying a game before installation. The new limits expose the economics beneath that promise: remote hardware is still hardware, and every extra hour consumes capacity, electricity and bandwidth that Microsoft has decided to price directly. (IGN)

Gloria Steinem made feminism part of the mainstream

Gloria Steinem, the American journalist and organiser who became one of the most recognisable leaders of the women’s movement, has died at home in New York aged 92. Her path into public life began with journalism. A 1962 essay examined how women were forced to choose between careers and family; the next year she spent eleven days undercover as a Playboy Bunny to document exploitative working conditions. At New York magazine she covered an abortion speakout and coined the phrase “reproductive freedom”, connecting her own experience of an abortion in London at 22 with a political campaign she would pursue for the rest of her life. (Guardian, BBC)

Steinem co-founded Ms. magazine with Dorothy Pitman Hughes in 1971 and used reporting, speeches and nine books to bring feminist arguments to a mass audience. Her work ranged across equal pay, reproductive rights and domestic violence, and extended to campaigns against apartheid, female genital mutilation, pornography and the Vietnam and Gulf wars. She credited Black feminists including Florynce Kennedy, Shirley Chisholm and Fannie Lou Hamer with teaching her how movements are built. That willingness to connect causes made her influential and also exposed her to disputes within feminism over race, class, sex work and political strategy. (Guardian, BBC)

She continued working almost to the end and had public appearances planned for this autumn. A new memoir, An Unexpected Life, is due this month and now becomes her final account of a career lasting more than six decades. Steinem’s greatest achievement was cultural as much as legislative: she helped give ordinary experiences a political vocabulary and made women’s equality an unavoidable part of public debate. The arguments changed around her, and many remain unfinished. The space in which they are conducted is immeasurably larger because she occupied it. (Guardian, Guardian)

Sports

Martinelli leaves Arsenal in a record sale

Gabriel Martinelli has joined Al-Hilal for £60m, the largest fee Arsenal have received for a player and £20m above the previous record set by Alex Oxlade-Chamberlain’s move to Liverpool in 2017. The agreement includes a sell-on clause, while Martinelli’s four-year contract is thought to be worth about £17m a season. He arrived from Ituano as a teenager in 2019, grew into one of Arsenal’s most dangerous runners and leaves at 25 after contributing to last season’s Premier League title. A reduced role in the current squad made a transfer acceptable to the club and attractive to the player. (Guardian)

His departure follows Gabriel Jesus’s £8.6m move to Barcelona and Fábio Vieira’s deadline-day sale to Hamburg. Arsenal chose not to replace any of them before the English window closed, leaving a title defence built around Bukayo Saka, Martin Ødegaard, Eberechi Eze and Kai Havertz. The record income strengthens the accounts and confirms the club can finally sell senior players on favourable terms. It also removes two experienced Brazilian forwards in one week. The judgment will be made on whether a more concentrated attack remains deep enough when injuries and the Champions League compress the season. (Guardian)

Fifa and Uefa take their power struggle to American courts

Fifa has asked a federal judge in Florida to reject Uefa’s attempt to obtain documents about Gianni Infantino’s abandoned plan to sell private investors a stake in World Cup-related commercial assets. Uefa filed four subpoena requests in American courts seeking communications from Fifa, Joshua Kushner’s Thrive Capital and JP Morgan while it considers criminal proceedings in Switzerland. Fifa calls the action a publicity-driven smear campaign and argues that football’s statutes require the dispute to go through arbitration. (Guardian, BBC)

The proposed Fifa Forward Enterprise structure collapsed after resistance from Uefa and national associations alarmed by outside ownership of the World Cup’s future income. The legal contest now asks who can examine how that plan was assembled and whether Infantino exceeded his authority. Fifa says European opposition protects Uefa’s market power; Uefa sees a governance issue serious enough to explore criminal law. Either way, two bodies responsible for the same sport are using courts, threatened boycotts and confidential commercial records against each other. The dispute will follow them into preparations for the next cycle of tournaments unless a transparent account of the proposal replaces the institutional accusations. (Guardian, BBC)
