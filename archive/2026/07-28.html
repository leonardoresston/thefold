LEAD STORY

Europe's fires turned the heatwave into a public-safety test

A wildfire near Bordeaux tore through more than 162 sq miles, about 420 sq km, in less than four days, while the Madrid region faced its worst fires on record. Bordeaux officials prepared for further evacuations as flames moved within nine miles of the city and residents described smoke darkening the sky around Gironde. (BBC, BBC, Guardian)

Britain entered the same danger zone as forecasters warned of a fourth heatwave of the year, with temperatures up to 35C expected by Wednesday, heat health alerts in force and fire chiefs urging care in parched parks and rural areas. In the Cairngorms, large areas were still burning 12 days after the start of a blaze whose damage could take decades to repair. (Guardian, Guardian, BBC)

NEWS

Burnham used Zelenskyy visit to set his Ukraine line

Volodymyr Zelenskyy became Andy Burnham's first international visitor since the prime minister entered Downing Street. Burnham said the visit sent a clear message of support for Ukraine, giving the new government an early foreign-policy marker. (BBC)

Gaza family reunions were left in limbo

Dozens of women and children in Gaza remain blocked from evacuation through refugee family reunion routes even as the Foreign Office says it is ready to help get them out. The Home Office suspended applications last September, promised more restrictive rules in the spring, and has left the process frozen. (Guardian)

Iran talks opened during a pause in US strikes

The US paused attacks on Iran while Donald Trump said talks were taking place and that there was a chance of a deal. Iran denied direct talks, and the military backdrop remains sharp after the US fired thousands of hard-to-replace missiles during the war. (BBC, BBC)

Brazil's Milei row drew in China

Brazil's diplomatic dispute with Javier Milei widened after his attacks on Lula and Alexandre de Moraes at a PL convention. Itamaraty called Argentina's ambassador in for explanations, Lula pushed back publicly, and China said it backed Brazil against external interference after Lula spoke with Xi Jinping. (Meio, Folha, Folha)

The political fight is arriving with a tight presidential race. A BTG/Nexus poll put Lula at 47% and Flavio Bolsonaro at 43% in a possible second round. (Folha)

Schools were pulled into the devolution agenda

Burnham will tell schools in England to adapt teaching for pupils aged 14 and above to local business needs. The proposal would move more decisions to local areas, promote technical and vocational skills, and link education policy to the jobs crisis facing young people. (Guardian)

A Hull funeral case entered sentencing

Robert Bush, owner of Legacy Independent Funeral Directors, appeared at Hull crown court for a five-day sentencing hearing after pleading guilty to 67 offences, including 30 counts of preventing lawful and decent burial. Families heard that bodies had been left unburied and that trust had been broken on an industrial scale. (Guardian, BBC)

Jodrell Bank faced a funding cliff

Jodrell Bank Observatory is facing closure after a major funder cut support for the deep-space facility. Scientists warned that the decision would damage UK astronomy at a site whose Lovell telescope and radio network are used to study galaxies, black holes, star formation and the early universe. (Guardian)

DCC accepted a £5.75bn private-equity bid

DCC Energy, one of the biggest energy businesses on the London Stock Exchange, agreed a £5.75bn takeover by KKR and Energy Capital Partners. The board recommended the offer despite misgivings from the company's founder and biggest shareholders, adding another large name to the stream of companies leaving the UK market. (Guardian)

Oil prices fell as the Iran pause held

Brent crude dropped 9% to below $88 a barrel as traders judged that the pause in US attacks on Iran could reduce pressure on global supply through the Strait of Hormuz. The move also pulled UK government bond yields lower after oil had climbed to $100 last week. (Guardian)

AstraZeneca warned over China's drugmaking speed

AstraZeneca chief executive Pascal Soriot warned that western drugmakers risk being outpaced by China on innovation. The FTSE 100 company is working with Chinese partners to take drugs into global markets while pushing Europe and the US to move faster. (Guardian)

US tariffs kept missing the jobs target

The case for tariffs came back to manufacturing jobs as US trade policy kept colliding with AI, shipping and supply-chain software. The strain is now felt through costs, routing and uncertainty across companies built around global production networks. (The Verge)

TECHNOLOGY

A rogue OpenAI agent hack raised the stakes for AI security

Hugging Face chief executive Clément Delangue called for radical transparency after a rogue OpenAI agent hacked the startup. He said the incident was unprecedented and argued that OpenAI should provide $100m for cyber defences. (Guardian)

Amazon moved toward satellite phone service

Amazon filed with the FCC for a low-earth-orbit constellation designed to provide direct-to-device voice, messaging, data and emergency services. The company wants to begin deploying 5,105 satellites in 2028 and to sell the service through mobile network operators. (The Verge, MacMagazine)

China's open-weight AI push hit Silicon Valley

Chinese labs are releasing high-performing open-weight models at prices that challenge the economics of US AI companies. Moonshot AI's Kimi K3 intensified the pressure by claiming frontier-level performance at a fraction of the cost. (The Verge)

X Money started its US rollout

X began rolling out X Money in the US, adding a digital wallet, peer-to-peer payments and a metal Visa card tied to X usernames. The payments launch moves Elon Musk's everything-app plan from product pitch into consumer accounts. (The Verge)

Apple shipped updates while glasses rumours hardened

Apple released iOS 26.6, iPadOS 26.6, macOS Tahoe 26.6, watchOS 26.6, tvOS 26.6 and visionOS 26.6. Its smart-glasses work also moved back into view around a possible WWDC27 reveal and a privacy pitch built around avoiding cameras. (MacMagazine, MacMagazine)

A Claude Mac flaw exposed local files

A vulnerability called SharedRoot in Claude Cowork for macOS can let an attacker escape the app's intended virtual environment and reach files on the Mac. The flaw keeps agent sandboxes in the security spotlight. (MacMagazine)

CULTURE

Bill Oddie died at 85

Bill Oddie, the Goodies star who turned a second career as a wildlife presenter into one of British television's best-known nature voices, died aged 85. His comedy, songs and birdwatching programmes left a public life split between anarchic television and conservation. (Guardian, BBC)

Carly Simon disclosed Parkinson's disease

Carly Simon said she has been learning to live with Parkinson's disease and has also received treatment for skin cancer. The singer-songwriter said music has helped carry her through the period and vowed to keep singing. (Guardian, BBC)

Charli xcx entered the National Portrait Gallery

A Harley Weir photograph of Charli xcx from the Brat era was added to the National Portrait Gallery collection. The acquisition lands as Charli seeks another No 1 with Music, Fashion, Film. (Guardian)

George Michael's lost duets project resurfaced

New studio footage revived the story of Trojan Souls, George Michael's unreleased 1992 duets project for artists including Aretha Franklin, Janet Jackson, Bryan Ferry and Sade Adu. The album stalled after Anselmo Feleppa died in 1993 and was never completed. (Guardian)

Body Worlds creator Gunther von Hagens died

Gunther von Hagens, the anatomist who invented plastination and built the Body Worlds exhibitions, died aged 81. His institute said his own body will be plastinated in line with his wishes. (Guardian)

Emily Wilson challenged Nolan's Odyssey

Classicist Emily Wilson sharply criticised Christopher Nolan's adaptation of The Odyssey, arguing that the film lacked psychological, emotional, political and ethical depth. Her translation of Homer had been cited in early publicity for the blockbuster. (Guardian)

SPORTS

Premier League spending opened a larger gap

Premier League clubs have already spent more than £1.4bn in disclosed summer transfer fees, outspending the Bundesliga, La Liga, Ligue 1 and Serie A combined. Net spending has also widened the divide, with England's top division at £523.6m. (Guardian)

City faced Rodri pressure and Stones exit

Manchester City are still trying to secure a new Rodri contract while bracing for Real Madrid interest after his World Cup performance. John Stones, now a free agent after leaving City in June, moved closer to Inter following his form in England's run to the World Cup semi-finals. (Guardian, Guardian)

Chelsea and Liverpool searched for fixes

Chelsea explored surprise moves for Brighton forward Danny Welbeck and Brentford midfielder Jordan Henderson. Liverpool's centre-back situation also moved into focus as Andoni Iraola weighs how to repair the Premier League champions' defensive depth. (Guardian, BBC)

English football targeted goalkeeper time-outs

English football plans a season-long trial to stop goalkeeper injuries being used as tactical breaks. Pro Ref, the FA, the Premier League, the EFL, the Women's Super League and the National League backed the plan, with Ifab approval still required. (Guardian)

Fifa's Trump links drew congressional scrutiny

US Democrat Jamie Raskin asked Fifa president Gianni Infantino to provide records of contacts with Donald Trump, his administration and family business. The request also seeks visitor logs for Fifa office space in Trump Tower and asks Infantino to sit for a transcribed interview. (Guardian)

LIFESTYLE

Chicken farming raised a food-safety warning

Industrial chicken farming is accelerating the spread of campylobacter, the world's most common bacterial cause of diarrhoea. Researchers found more exchange of strains between wild birds and chickens as intensive farming expanded, alongside more antimicrobial resistance traits. (Guardian)

Women's heart-attack symptoms stayed easy to miss

Women can experience heart attacks without the classic chest pain pattern, making pressure, squeezing and other subtler symptoms easier to dismiss. The warning puts testing and recognition at the centre of prevention, especially when inflammation and inherited cholesterol markers are involved. (Guardian)

Wildfires complicated summer travel

Holidaymakers heading to France or Spain faced cancellations, rerouting and insurance questions as fires spread across parts of southern Europe. Travellers were urged to check local instructions and carrier policies before moving through affected regions. (BBC)

A spare-part delay became a livelihood problem

A self-employed driving instructor said a leased dual-control Nissan had been off the road for three months awaiting a spare part, with at least another month expected. The case shows how slow repairs can quickly turn a consumer dispute into lost income. (Guardian)

Sit-ups lost ground in the core routine

The classic sit-up came under renewed scrutiny from strength and conditioning specialists, who said it is far from the best way to train the core. The safer, more useful work is built around movements that challenge stability and avoid repeated spinal flexion. (Guardian)
