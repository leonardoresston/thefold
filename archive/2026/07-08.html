LEAD STORY

Farage's by-election gamble opened under a money-laundering shadow

Nigel Farage began a new Clacton fight after bankers reported the £5m gift he received from cryptocurrency donor Christopher Harborne to the National Crime Agency over money-laundering concerns. The Reform UK leader is already waiting for the standards commissioner to decide whether his failure to declare the money breached parliamentary rules. His resignation as MP and decision to stand again put that scrutiny straight into the by-election campaign. (The Guardian, BBC)

The strain on public rules reached beyond Westminster. Donald Trump arrived at the Nato summit in Ankara renewing his call for the US to acquire Greenland, threatening to withdraw American troops from Europe and attacking the alliance's stance on Iran. In Hungary, public media channels tied to Viktor Orbán halted broadcasting after Péter Magyar's election victory began dismantling the old information system. (The Guardian, The Guardian, BBC)

NEWS

Clacton received another Farage campaign with little appetite

Farage's constituents faced the prospect of a second high-profile contest two years after sending him to Westminster. The by-election announcement followed two parliamentary probes into financial support he received, including the Harborne gift. Local reaction was weary, with voters questioning why the seat was being asked to settle a Westminster standards dispute while everyday constituency work still needed an MP. (The Guardian, BBC)

Harry's Mail case ended with a vast costs risk

Prince Harry and six other claimants lost their High Court case against the publisher of the Daily Mail over alleged unlawful methods used to source stories. The court dismissed the claims and said the group had not proved that information was obtained unlawfully. The defeat leaves the claimants facing a possible legal bill of up to £50m and landed during the Duke of Sussex's return to UK engagements. (The Guardian, BBC)

Europe's fire season exposed the cost of slow adaptation

Wildfires had burned 28,000 hectares in France and 50,000 hectares in Spain by 1 July, more than double the usual area for that point in the year. Scientists called for better land management alongside cuts in the emissions driving the heat, as floods earlier in the year gave way to dry ground, smoke and emergency evacuations across the Mediterranean. (The Guardian)

Brazil's tariff fight pulled Flávio Bolsonaro into two fronts

Flávio Bolsonaro used a US hearing to argue that tariffs on Brazilian products would help Lula and that the timing was the worst possible for such a levy. Allies had already concluded that an earlier document calling for suspension of the tariff had landed badly, while Brazilian and American groups pressed for the duties to end. At home, Alexandre de Moraes ordered the Federal Police to hear Flávio in a slander inquiry involving Lula. (Canal Meio, Folha Poder, Folha Poder)

Debt warnings moved from Westminster models to mortgages

The Office for Budget Responsibility warned that UK public debt would move on to an unsustainable upward path from the 2040s without action on ageing, health, pensions and defence costs. Households received the nearer version of the same pressure: another million homeowners are expected to move on to more expensive mortgage deals over the next two years, adding about £45 a month on average. (The Guardian, BBC)

Banks and Thames Water tested the edges of rescue policy

The Bank of England moved toward easing capital rules for large lenders, even as policymakers pointed to risks from fast AI development and debt-funded stock investments. Thames Water's creditors kept pursuing a bid for the company, including in the event of temporary nationalisation, while a group holding about £14bn of senior debt discussed a £10bn rescue proposal with Ofwat. (The Guardian, The Guardian)

Retailers cut services and prepared for hotter stores

John Lewis put about 200 jobs at risk as it prepared to close bureau de change desks in 30 stores and gift-wrapping services in 25. Marks & Spencer moved in the opposite direction on infrastructure, investing in refrigeration able to withstand 45C weather after struggling through June's extreme heat and stronger demand for ice-cream. (The Guardian, The Guardian)

TECHNOLOGY

AI's footprint reached Brick Lane and Big Tech climate pledges

Campaigners in east London opposed plans for a datacentre in Brick Lane, arguing that affordable housing should take priority over more computing capacity for finance and AI. The fight sits beside a larger energy problem for the industry, as AI investment pushes Google and Amazon further away from their climate targets and turns datacentre power into a local political issue. (The Guardian, The Guardian)

AI assistants moved deeper into phones and social apps

Meta launched Muse Image, the first image model from its Superintelligence Labs division, across the Meta AI app, Instagram and WhatsApp, with Facebook and Messenger to follow. Anthropic opened Claude Cowork on mobile and web for Max subscribers before a wider rollout, and Notion released an iOS app built specifically for interacting with agents. (The Verge, The Verge, MacMagazine)

Xbox cuts reached one of gaming's oldest studios

Id Software, the Doom studio owned by Microsoft, was hit by deep layoffs as the wider Xbox restructuring continued. Game Developer put the losses at roughly half the studio, with one source citing more than 90 redundancies and another pointing to a heavy impact on quality assurance. The cuts extend a difficult year for game workers into one of the medium's foundational names. (The Verge)

Google set a Pixel date while apps tuned everyday workflows

Google scheduled its next Made by Google hardware event for 12 August in New York, where the Pixel 11 line is expected to lead the evening launch. Elsewhere, Chrome and Edge began removing a delay that affected copy and paste in web apps, while Flighty added connection assistance, gate prediction and other travel tools to its flight tracker. (The Verge, MacMagazine, MacMagazine)

CULTURE

Nolan's Odyssey turned its London premiere into an awards signal

Christopher Nolan's three-hour version of Homer's epic premiered in London with early reaction already treating it as a major Oscar contender. The film arrives with Matt Damon, Robert Pattinson, Tom Holland and Anne Hathaway in a historical spectacle that moves from myth to postwar disillusion, giving the summer release calendar one of its clearest prestige events. (The Guardian)

Patrice Lawrence took the children's laureate role

Patrice Lawrence, author of Orangeboy, Indigo Donut and Is That Your Mama?, became the UK's next children's laureate. Her two-year term will focus on children who feel cut off from reading, placing access and confidence at the centre of a post held before her by Jacqueline Wilson, Quentin Blake, Michael Rosen, Julia Donaldson, Malorie Blackman and Frank Cottrell-Boyce. (The Guardian)

BTS returned to the UK as Dolly Parton prepared Broadway

BTS played their first UK show in seven years at Tottenham Hotspur Stadium, bringing a catalogue that moved from rap to bright pop with the scale expected of the world's biggest K-pop group. Dolly: A True Original Musical will begin previews at New York's St James Theatre on 7 December and open on 19 January, Parton's 81st birthday. (The Guardian, The Guardian)

Shahrnush Parsipur left a feminist literary record

Shahrnush Parsipur died at 80 after a career that made her one of Iran's central feminist novelists. Women Without Men and Touba and the Meaning of Night challenged patriarchal power directly, and the costs were severe: she was imprisoned four times under the Shah and the Islamic Republic before living much of her later life in exile. (The Guardian)

SPORTS

Messi pulled Argentina back from the edge

Argentina reached the World Cup quarter-finals after Lionel Messi led a late escape against Egypt. The champions were two goals down with 11 minutes left and still avoided extra time, leaving Egypt furious over what it called injustice and another chapter of Messi rescue work inside a tournament that keeps stretching his final act. (The Guardian, BBC, BBC)

Belgium ended the US run and left Fifa exposed

The United States went out of its home World Cup after Belgium punished Mauricio Pochettino's side in Seattle. The defeat followed Trump's intervention over Folarin Balogun's red card and Fifa's decision to lift the suspension, leaving the tournament with a governance argument that outlived the host nation's team. Pochettino now waits for talks with US Soccer over his future. (The Guardian, The Guardian, The Guardian)

Ronaldo closed his World Cup chapter

Cristiano Ronaldo confirmed he had played his last World Cup match after Portugal's 1-0 defeat by Spain in Dallas. At 41, he leaves the tournament as the only man to score at six World Cups and with 233 Portugal appearances behind him. He has not yet decided whether to end his international career entirely. (The Guardian)

England's quarter-final build-up came with a new warning

England players will be told not to jump advertising hoardings after Jordan Henderson suffered a freak injury during celebrations. The instruction arrives as the squad prepares for the quarter-finals and Jude Bellingham's influence keeps growing after the win over Mexico, with Thomas Tuchel balancing momentum against the avoidable risks that come after the whistle. (BBC, The Guardian)

LIFESTYLE

Heatwave sleep became a household planning problem

Hot nights are becoming a practical part of British summer life. Sleep specialists warned that heat can cut sleep quality and reduce deep sleep, while household advice moved toward shading, airflow and lower-cost cooling before portable air conditioning. The useful work starts before bedtime: shade rooms, keep air moving and reduce the heat trapped during the day. (The Guardian, The Guardian)

Summer tables stayed simple and seasonal

Tomato tart and strawberry elderflower trifle kept the cooking close to British summer produce, while cafetiere advice returned coffee to scales, grind size and patience. The pattern is ordinary but useful: good weather food and drink work best when small technical details are sorted before the appetite arrives. (The Guardian, The Guardian)

AI skincare entered the advice gap

People are sending selfies to skincare apps and chatbots for diagnosis, routines and product advice, even as dermatology specialists warn that more than 3,000 skin conditions can sit behind similar symptoms. The risk is plain: a confident regimen is no substitute for medical judgement when a rash, mole or infection needs a trained eye. (The Guardian)

A Lime bike collision exposed a responsibility gap

Jane Ouartsi suffered a fractured collarbone, two spinal fractures and a broken femur after being hit by a child riding a Lime bike in central London. Three years later, the case remains a consumer and urban-transport problem as much as a personal injury: app-based mobility can move quickly through the city while liability moves slowly after serious harm. (The Guardian)
