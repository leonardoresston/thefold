News

Zelenskyy accuses SpaceX of throttling Starshield across the front line and reveals US envoys floated a Donbas-for-Nato deal

Volodymyr Zelenskyy has accused Elon Musk’s SpaceX of imposing a covert speed cap on Ukraine’s military-grade Starshield satellite network, warning that the restriction has degraded air defence and strike operations along the entire front line. For five consecutive days, Ukrainian drones connected to the system have lost satellite links whenever their speed exceeds 161km/h (100mph), preventing interceptors from catching jet-powered Russian Shahed munitions and hampering mid-range strikes inside occupied territory. After Ukraine’s defence ministry raised the issue with SpaceX, the Pentagon, and the State Department, the company claimed no restrictions existed and blamed a technical glitch—an explanation Kyiv rejects after daily front-line tests confirmed uniform throttling. (Guardian)

Officials in Kyiv suspect the slowdown is a political lever meant to force concessions following last week’s US-brokered talks in Abu Dhabi and a shuttle mission by Donald Trump’s envoys, Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff. After meeting Vladimir Putin in Moscow, the American pair travelled to Dublin on Thursday and asked Zelenskyy, for the first time, whether Ukraine would withdraw its forces from the eastern Donbas region in return for Nato membership or equivalent Western security guarantees. Zelenskyy noted that the vague overture excluded US ground troops and ruled out ceding territory, adding that any formal proposal would require ironclad guarantees, unrestricted armed forces, reconstruction funds, and a national referendum. He also dismissed Putin’s proposed five-day humanitarian truce in Kostiantynivka as a ploy to occupy the city unopposed. (Guardian, BBC)

The battle over satellite-guided munitions underscores how rapidly unmanned aircraft are transforming warfare, with lethal consequences where air defences are absent. In Sudan, where 42 months of civil war between the armed forces and the paramilitary Rapid Support Forces have killed between 150,000 and 400,000 people, long-range drones have become the primary cause of civilian deaths. UN monitors recorded at least 306 civilian fatalities from drone strikes in the first three weeks of October, including 139 killed in a double-tap attack on a transport hub in Al-Dibab and 103 at a market in Nyala, while open-source investigators at the Centre for Information Resilience have identified 21 drone models supplied from China, Russia, Belarus, Iran, and Turkey. (Guardian)

Ministers weigh staggered age of consent after Russell Brand allegation as ethics watchdog chief steps back and NHS cancer backlog grows

The government will examine proposals to reform the age of consent after a woman waived her anonymity to allege that she was orally raped by Russell Brand when she was a 16-year-old schoolgirl and he was 30. Mia Latter, now 37 and known as Alice during the 2023 investigation into the entertainer, spoke out days after Brand was jailed for ten years and six months on separate rape and sexual assault convictions. Latter is campaigning for a staggered age of consent that would keep teenage relationships legal while criminalising sexual relationships between adults in their thirties or older and anyone under 18. De facto deputy prime minister Louise Haigh said the proposal deserved thorough parliamentary scrutiny. (Guardian, BBC)

Across Whitehall, the head of the government’s flagship standards body has stepped aside after disclosures that he faced sexual misconduct inquiries in a former role. Stuart Sterling, appointed in October 2025 as founding chief executive of the Ethics and Integrity Commission to advise the prime minister on public probity, withdrew while officials establish the facts. While serving as a junior officer with Northamptonshire Police in 2002, Sterling was suspended and fined two weeks’ pay after a misconduct hearing into claims that he inappropriately touched a female colleague at a police station, before facing a second professional standards investigation in 2008 over an alleged off-duty incident that he denies. (BBC)

Ministers also face pressure over clinical capacity after Radiotherapy UK analysis showed NHS England is on course to miss its March 2027 interim target of treating 80% of cancer patients within 62 days by nearly 9,500 patients. Performance slipped to 70.7% in September—leaving a monthly shortfall of 2,850 patients—with on-time treatment falling to 66.6% in the Midlands, 60.9% for lung cancer, and 56.5% for head and neck cancers, while only 55.4% of patients receive a diagnosis within 28 days. Meanwhile, freedom of information data from the NHS Business Services Authority revealed that community pharmacies dispensed 341 prescriptions for GLP-1 weight-loss drugs such as Wegovy and Mounjaro to under-18s between April and July, including 15 for children under 12, prompting calls for specialist monitoring of growth and puberty. (Guardian, Guardian)

Democrat captures Alaska governorship after 28 years as federal judge blocks Trump’s Washington golf course closure and Teddy Riner enters French race

Former state legislator Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins has won the Alaska governor’s race, becoming the first Democrat to lead the state since 1998 and extending his party’s midterm sweep to 30 US governorships. Called ten days after polls closed, the result saw the 37-year-old capture more than 50% of first-preference votes under Alaska’s ranked-choice system, defeating three Republicans, including a Trump-endorsed candidate, outright. Following Democratic victories in Republican strongholds including Texas, Ohio, Georgia, Iowa, and Kansas, Kreiss-Tomkins campaigned on restoring Alaska’s coastal ferry network, taxing oil and gas producers, funding public schools, and pausing new data centre construction until state regulations are enacted. (Guardian)

In Washington, US District Judge Ana Reyes issued a 30-page restraining order blocking the Trump administration from closing the 106-year-old East Potomac Golf Links on Sunday for a 15-month luxury redesign after the National Park Service felled hundreds of trees with 15 hours’ notice, ruling that the closure violated federal law. Meanwhile, Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy disclosed that Friday’s four-hour nationwide flight ground stop—the longest since September 2001—stemmed from routine maintenance by contractor L3Harris to replace a discontinued copper wire with fibre optic cable at a remote facility, when a new router flooded air traffic control networks with more than 1,000 connection attempts every four to five seconds. (BBC, The Verge, Guardian)

In France, five-time Olympic heavyweight judo champion Teddy Riner has shaken up next April’s presidential race by launching an independent candidacy under the slogan La France qu’on aime. Although the 37-year-old athlete has no party machine or manifesto beyond a visit to a Seine-Saint-Denis hospital and an appeal to voters beyond left and right, his announcement drew 28 million views online and a 22% potential support rating in an Ifop poll, forcing mainstream strategists to reckon with him alongside Marine Le Pen and Jean-Luc Mélenchon. (BBC)

Banco Master scandal puts Brazil’s Supreme Court under unprecedented strain as Lula maps out R$2.6tn fourth-term cabinet

Nearly a year after the November 2025 arrest of banker Daniel Vorcaro, the expanding Banco Master investigation has plunged Brazil’s Supreme Federal Court (STF) into an institutional crisis that justices privately consider more damaging to its credibility than the 8 January 2023 ransacking of its headquarters. Since a Federal Police report on Justice Dias Toffoli’s ties to the bank forced him to hand oversight of the case to André Mendonça in February, Court President Edson Fachin has demanded every police reference to sitting justices. A forthcoming tranche of files is expected to mention six members of the eleven-seat bench—Alexandre de Moraes, Gilmar Mendes, Luiz Fux, Kassio Nunes Marques, Toffoli, and Mendonça—prompting congressional allies to press Senate President Davi Alcolumbre to curb the court’s expansive inquiries. (Folha, Folha)

Calling from the COP31 climate summit in Antalya, Turkey—where international delegates formed a protective cordon on Saturday so Turkish LGBTQ+ and climate activists could march with rainbow flags under heavy police surveillance—President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva has scheduled talks next week in Brasília to assemble his fourth-term cabinet. The blueprint covers R$2.6 trillion (£350 billion) in 2027 budget allocations and 574,292 civil servants across 38 ministries, with plans for a standalone Public Security Ministry, a Ministry for Older People, and a Digital Communication Secretariat threatening to pass the post-1985 record of 39 ministries set under Dilma Rousseff, while Workers’ Party figures Camilo Santana and Gleisi Hoffmann are lined up for senior posts. (Folha, Folha, Guardian)

New Superior Electoral Court (TSE) figures published on Saturday showed that Brazilians aged 70 and over—for whom voting is optional—played a decisive role in Lula’s 25 October runoff comeback. Turnout among over-70s jumped from 51% in the first round (8.8 million voters) to 58% in the second (10.1 million), accounting for more than four in ten of the three million additional ballots cast between the two rounds despite representing a tenth of the electorate, as Brazil marks 137 years since its 1889 republican proclamation amid institutional battles over judicial reach, religious caucuses, and executive power first diagnosed by constitutional architect Rui Barbosa. (Folha, Meio)

Economy

Bank of England chief economist signals December interest rate hike as Milburn prepares radical overhaul of youth benefits

Bank of England chief economist Huw Pill has warned that policymakers have “further to go” on monetary tightening after this month’s quarter-point increase to 4%, signalling that another rate rise on 17 December and further hikes in early 2027 are probable. With the US-Israeli conflict with Iran keeping energy prices high and pushing UK inflation towards a forecast 5% peak next spring, Pill argued the Bank had waited too long to act and risked second-round wage demands after 0.7% third-quarter GDP growth. He added that market expectations of rates approaching 5% next year were reasonable—a stance that threatens steeper payments for 2.3 million households due to remortgage in 2027 and has drawn warnings that aggressive hikes could squeeze construction and manufacturing when consumer spending is flat. (Guardian, Guardian)

Higher borrowing costs coincide with a major welfare intervention on Tuesday, when former Labour cabinet minister Alan Milburn publishes his year-long review into the nearly one million Britons aged 16 to 24 who are not in education, employment, or training (NEET). Coordinated closely with Prime Minister Andy Burnham, Milburn’s blueprint proposes barring under-25s from claiming the main sickness and disability elements of Universal Credit and Personal Independence Payment, shifting the 25-to-1 ratio of youth benefit spending over employment support to two-to-one while legislating an “opportunity guarantee” offering every young person a job, apprenticeship, training place, or health treatment. (BBC)

Young jobseekers, however, point to a shrinking entry-level market as employers trim graduate recruitment and automate junior roles. Recent postgraduates from Oxford, Cambridge, and London Russell Group universities describe submitting up to 1,000 applications over two years for media, administrative, and retail jobs, encountering automated rejections or silence after interviews while relying on Universal Credit. Even as secondary schools introduce Year 10 lessons on eye contact and handshakes to prepare pupils for work, graduates warn that the social contract has frayed as companies demand experience while cutting the junior positions that once provided it. (Guardian, BBC)

Burnham seeks £17bn EU trade reset as Eurotunnel unveils drive-through biometric border and Greenpeace boulders hit windfarm site

Addressing the Welsh Labour conference in Llandudno on Saturday ahead of next week’s UK-EU summit, Andy Burnham declared that Britain has “lost more than we gained” since the 2016 referendum and accused the Conservatives of burning bridges with Brussels. While keeping manifesto red lines against rejoining the single market or customs union, the prime minister is pursuing food and farming, emissions trading, and youth mobility agreements estimated to lift GDP by 0.4% (£17 billion) while aiding Welsh lamb and shellfish exporters, alongside proposals to spend up to £450 million a year on discounted tuition for EU students in exchange for access to the Made in Europe industrial programme. (Guardian)

Border friction across the Channel may also ease after Eurotunnel operator Getlink won European Commission approval to install 120 airport-style drive-through biometric kiosks at Folkestone and Calais. Built to prevent tailbacks when the delayed Schengen Entry/Exit System takes full effect, the lanes use overhead “eagle eye” cameras so British and French police can supervise fingerprinting and facial scans remotely without motorists leaving their cars, part of a 40-page Getlink proposal to make the tunnel a testbed for automated border tech. (Guardian)

In the North Sea, however, developer RWE is surveying 15 tonnes of two-tonne boulders dropped off the Norfolk coast by Greenpeace to block bottom trawling—including stones signed by Benedict Cumberbatch and Simon Pegg—after discovering activists had unwittingly placed them across the seabed earmarked for the £12 billion Norfolk Vanguard offshore windfarms backed by £1 billion in public funds. Meanwhile, the Soil Association stripped British farmed salmon of its organic certification after 20 years, concluding that Scotland’s £1 billion aquaculture industry fails welfare and environmental standards amid sea lice infestations, formaldehyde treatments, high mortality, and wild Atlantic salmon declines exceeding 70%. (Guardian, Guardian)

Technology

Italy fines Meta €805m over Android photo gallery access as SpaceX catches Starship upper stage and Edinburgh marches against AI data centres

Italy’s data protection authority has fined Meta €805 million ($887 million) for accessing the photographs, videos, and metadata of roughly 20 million Android users without specific consent. The penalty—about 0.4% of Meta’s $201 billion annual revenue and among Italy’s largest tech fines—targets Facebook’s gallery suggestions tool, introduced in 2023. Regulators found that Meta bundled camera-roll scanning into a single blanket app permission behind a generic notice and cited a lack of cooperation during the inquiry; Meta, which was separately ordered in late October to pay $25 billion in New Mexico over data disclosures, said it would appeal. (Guardian)

In Texas, SpaceX achieved a major milestone on Saturday when the mechanical “chopstick” arms of its Mechazilla tower at Starbase caught the 52-metre (171ft) Starship upper stage in mid-air for the first time, ending a 22-hour 15th test flight that orbited Earth 14 times. Although the lower Super Heavy booster aborted its return minutes after Friday’s liftoff and exploded over the Gulf of Mexico due to low fuel and a trajectory check, catching the orbital spacecraft itself brings Elon Musk’s company closer to rapid reusability ahead of Nasa’s 2028 lunar landing target. (Guardian, The Verge, BBC)

In Scotland, thousands of demonstrators marched down Edinburgh’s Royal Mile to the Scottish Parliament on Saturday to oppose hyperscale AI data centres planned for sites including Chapelcross, Cockenzie, and Larbert. Rejecting Innovation Minister Alyn Smith’s claim that server farms are the “whisky bonds of the AI sector”, residents warned of massive electricity consumption, lost farmland, and constant cooling noise as ministers prepare new national planning guidance. (BBC)

Tesla attaches $299 monthly subscription to personal Cybercab sales as Apple’s M5 Ultra Mac Studio reshapes local enterprise AI

Tesla has invited select customers in supported Robotaxi cities such as Austin to order its two-seat, steering-wheel-free Cybercab for personal use at $29,990, plus a $1,000 non-refundable deposit for a tentative December delivery window. The fine print, however, mandates a $299 monthly “Autonomy Subscription” that adds $17,940 over five years—bringing the baseline cost to roughly $47,600—and renders the pedal-free car unusable on public roads if cancelled, while contractual terms bar early buyers from placing the vehicle on Uber or Tesla’s own Robotaxi network to earn passive income ahead of a tentative second-quarter 2027 target. (The Verge)

In enterprise computing, IT teams are increasingly deploying clustered Apple silicon desktops as a low-power alternative to data centre GPU racks for running open-weight models locally. Apple Machine Learning Research benchmarks show four M5 Ultra Mac Studios—each offering up to 512GB of unified memory and 1.2TB/s bandwidth via the MLX framework—running Meta’s 816-billion-parameter Muse Spark Open model at over 36 tokens per second while drawing 300 watts per unit, alongside open-source tools like Osaurus that redact personal data on device before querying cloud models and Apple’s new HomeView smart display with ambient-light dark mode switching. (9to5Mac, 9to5Mac)

Culture

Publishing confronts an AI ‘wild west’ of cancelled book deals and prize expulsions as Sotheby’s $180m Van Gogh faces dual provenance storms

Book publishing is struggling to police generative AI controversies that have derailed seven-figure advances and literary prizes even as machine-assisted titles flood digital stores. While romance writer Coral Hart reports using AI to publish 370 Kindle Unlimited novels under 21 pen names since 2025—and a Stony Brook University study estimates a fifth of recent self-published books contain at least 25% AI text—traditional publishers have seen Hachette pull Mia Ballard’s Shy Girl, agents drop Nigerian debut novelist Jerry Falade from a $2 million deal, and France’s Goncourt Academy remove Thélyson Orélien from its longlist after chapters were flagged by the Pangram detector. Falade and other industry figures warn that every writer dropped in the recent scandals has been an author of colour whose syntax draws heightened suspicion, whereas white author Steven Rosenbaum kept his contract in May after AI research inserted fabricated quotes into his book. (Guardian)

In the art market, Sotheby’s flagship New York auction on Wednesday of twelve Impressionist and modern works from the Blaquier-Arrieta collection—led by Vincent van Gogh’s 1890 Châtaigniers en fleurs, estimated at a record $180 million (£136 million), alongside Cézanne’s Arlequin and Monet’s Le Jardin des Tuileries—faces two historical challenges. Lawyers for the heirs of German publisher Samuel Fischer have contacted Sotheby’s arguing the Van Gogh may have been sold under duress during the Nazi era in the 1930s, while human rights advocates point out that collector Carlos Pedro Blaquier, late head of Argentina’s Ledesma sugar company, was charged in 2012 over the July 1976 “Night of the Blackout” in Jujuy, when company vans were used by security forces to abduct roughly 400 workers and unionists, up to 55 of whom remain disappeared. (Guardian, BBC)

Philippines wins inaugural Eurovision Asia in Bangkok as Rockstar details empathy mechanics in Grand Theft Auto VI ahead of launch

Nineteen-year-old Filipino singer Carmelle Collado won the inaugural Eurovision Song Contest Asia in Bangkok on Saturday night, finishing on 211 points—41 ahead of hosts Thailand, with Vietnam third—after topping both the jury and public votes across 11 competing countries with Sobra-Sobra, a Tagalog and English pop-rap song backed by traditional kubing and kulintang instruments. Closer to home, BBC One debuted Honey, a brisk 1982 East Berlin espionage comedy starring Ann Skelly as a young Carolyn Martens prior to Killing Eve alongside Rory Kinnear, while small businesses from North Carolina to London’s Borough Market are increasingly posting CCTV footage and enforcing filming bans to counter fabricated reviews and freebie demands from social media influencers. (Guardian, Guardian, Guardian)

In gaming, ahead of Thursday’s PlayStation 5 and Xbox Series X/S launch of Grand Theft Auto VI, Rockstar North co-studio head Rob Nelson revealed that the studio has introduced gameplay systems that encourage player empathy for the first time in the series, reflecting protagonists Jason and Lucia’s predicament in Leonida, even as a retail leak of the $50 double-LP vinyl exposed the full licensed soundtrack ahead of December’s score release by Justice. Meanwhile, Riot Games used the League of Legends World Championship Finals to unveil League of Legends: New Dawn, a major 2027 overhaul of the 18-year-old game’s client, Summoner’s Rift terrain, and runes, while Wizards of the Coast and Netflix previewed Planeswalker, an animated Magic: The Gathering series arriving in 2027. (IGN, IGN, IGN, The Verge)
