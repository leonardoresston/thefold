Greenland gets a security deal after months under threat

The United States and Denmark have reached an agreement over Greenland’s security, drawing a line under a dispute that began when Donald Trump repeatedly threatened to take the self-governing Danish territory, including by force. Trump says the deal gives Washington permanent control over Greenland’s security needs, costs the US nothing and allows it to block military deployments or sensitive investments by American adversaries. Denmark’s prime minister, Mette Frederiksen, offered a more careful description: the agreement strengthens security in the Arctic and North Atlantic while recognising the sovereignty and territorial integrity of the Danish kingdom and Greenlanders’ right to determine their future. The text has yet to be published. (BBC)

Frederiksen said the agreement will be signed at the UN general assembly next week, then pass through the required parliamentary procedures. Greenland’s prime minister, Jens-Frederik Nielsen, welcomed an arrangement that recognises the island’s interests and place in international cooperation. An unnamed US official said it would survive any future move by Greenland to independence, grant permanent access, basing and overflight rights, and let the US build additional military structures without seeking approval from Copenhagen or Nuuk. It would also bar non-Nato countries from establishing bases and restrict investment by Russia and China in sensitive industries. Those claims matter, and their exact legal form will remain uncertain until all three governments release the document. (BBC)

The US already enjoys wide military access under a 1951 agreement with Denmark and keeps more than 100 personnel at Pituffik Space Base in Greenland’s north-west. The new terms appear designed to make that presence permanent and to place stronger American controls around the island’s mineral wealth and strategic position. Greenland sits across the routes linking North America, Europe and the increasingly accessible Arctic. It also contains large, mostly untapped deposits of rare-earth minerals used in phones, electric vehicles and defence technology. Trump has tied the island to his proposed Golden Dome missile shield and portrayed Russian and Chinese activity as an immediate security danger. (BBC)

The agreement eases a crisis inside Nato, where one member had threatened the territory of another, while leaving a difficult question about how it was obtained. Months of coercive language have produced additional American powers over a place whose people did not invite an annexation debate. Denmark and Greenland have secured explicit recognition of sovereignty and self-determination; Washington has secured durable access and a veto over rival powers. The practical balance may satisfy the immediate security interests of all three governments. Its political legacy will depend on whether Greenlanders experience the arrangement as protection they shaped or a settlement reached after a powerful ally made refusal feel unsafe. (BBC)

Washington puts Russia’s biggest energy customers in reach

Trump has signed the Lindsey O Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act, giving the White House authority to impose tariffs of up to 100% on the five largest buyers of Russian oil and gas. China and India sit at the centre of the measure: between December 2022 and August this year they bought 50% and 37% respectively of Russia’s crude exports. Turkey and the European Union each accounted for about 5%, with European purchases declining. Countries that obtain less than 15% of their natural gas from Russia and are taking steps to reduce that dependence can qualify for exceptions. At Trump’s request, the 48-page law also reaches Iran’s energy and weapons sectors. (BBC)

The law adds sanctions aimed at Vladimir Putin, senior Russian officials, banks and the shadow fleet of tankers used to work around earlier restrictions. Its title honours Senator Lindsey Graham, the Republican advocate for Ukraine who died in July after years of pressing for tougher economic pressure on Moscow. The sharpest effect may arrive beyond Russia: a 100% tariff would force Washington to weigh punishment for the Kremlin against a much wider confrontation with China or India. The legislation gives Trump considerable discretion over whether, when and how hard to use it. That makes the signing a powerful threat to Russia’s revenue, while the next decisions will show how much disruption the administration is prepared to accept in enforcing it. (BBC)

Britain’s police cloud carries risks recognised in 2017

Highly sensitive information held by more than 40 UK police forces sits on Microsoft Azure despite an official assessment warning that the platform could expose it to hostile hackers and the US government. The material includes criminal records, victim statements, internal email, intelligence, body-camera footage, digital evidence and case files. Some exceeds the normal “official” security classification and may fall into official-sensitive, secret or top-secret categories. At a 2017 meeting that approved migration to Microsoft’s cloud, police accepted that data could be transmitted and stored around the world, that its precise location might be unknown and that US government insiders presented a route to compromise. Almost every force now depends on Azure for at least part of its systems. (Guardian)

The National Police Chiefs’ Council says forces require UK-only datacentres as standard, restrict access to those with a genuine need and do not expect Microsoft to share data with Washington without British permission. Microsoft says it has never supplied UK government data in response to a US or other foreign request, and rejects the suggestion that its services automatically expose customers to governments. The unresolved risk comes from jurisdiction as much as geography. US law can compel an American cloud company to produce data held abroad and prevent it from telling the customer, while support engineers and subcontractors across many countries may require some level of access. Britain spends at least £1.9bn a year on Microsoft software, and up to 60% of government IT infrastructure is hosted on commercial clouds. A decade of convenience has created a dependency whose real resilience may remain unknowable until it is tested. (Guardian)

Brazil’s campaign turns public benefits into an argument about power

Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva has raised Bolsa Família payments by 15% and promised free weight-loss drugs through Brazil’s public health system with two weeks remaining before the presidential election. The monthly minimum will rise from about £98 to £113 on 19 October, shortly before a likely runoff on 25 October. Around 50 million people qualify for the cash-transfer programme, including roughly 27 million voters. Lula calls the change a permanent inflation adjustment that restores purchasing power after four years without an increase. Flávio Bolsonaro, his far-right challenger, calls it an attempt to buy poor voters, though he has avoided joining a court action to suspend a benefit relied on by such a large electorate. (Guardian, Meio)

The argument carries the memory of 2022, when Jair Bolsonaro increased the same monthly payment by 50% and added temporary support for taxi and lorry drivers before the election. Lula’s campaign then accused him of abusing political power. This year’s increase is limited to accumulated inflation and has no planned expiry date, distinctions that may matter in court while doing little to remove the electoral advantage of announcing it now. A separate Datafolha survey found distrust of the supreme court at a record 48%, and 47% of voters said the court’s crisis and the Banco Master scandal would influence their presidential choice to some degree. Lula and Flávio Bolsonaro are already separated by a margin small enough for institutional trust, household costs and the timing of state help to move together through the closing fortnight. (Folha, Folha)

A millisecond defect, followed by days of disruption

A software defect inside the system managing UK airspace produced corrupted data in a millisecond on 8 September, according to the first account from National Air Traffic Services. Controllers reduced traffic to preserve safety, more than 2,000 flights were cancelled and hundreds of thousands of passengers were affected. The defect struck at 10am, took two and a half hours to emerge as a serious problem, and was followed by a system restart after 3.15pm. Restrictions remained until 7.30pm, leaving aircraft and crews out of position for days. It was the third national air-traffic incident in three years, after a 2023 shutdown affected 700,000 passengers and a smaller outage caused 150 cancellations in 2025. Airlines want Nats to cover their costs; passengers face limited compensation because regulators treat air-traffic failures as extraordinary circumstances. The Civil Aviation Authority is reviewing why testing failed to catch the defect and whether the system has enough resilience when one obscure error can cascade through an entire network. (BBC)

Economy & Technology

Warren Buffett completes the handover at Berkshire

Warren Buffett is stepping down as chair of Berkshire Hathaway at 96, completing the transfer of authority at the company he turned from a failing textile business into a conglomerate worth about $1tn. His son Howard, 71, will become chair; Greg Abel has served as chief executive since Buffett left that role in May 2025. Buffett will remain a director and take the title of chair emeritus. The structure separates daily management from stewardship of the culture that has held a collection of insurers, manufacturers, retailers and large listed investments together for decades. Howard Buffett has been a director since 1993 and was long expected to inherit the chair, while Abel runs the businesses. (Guardian)

The transition closes one of the longest acts in American business. Buffett built Berkshire over roughly 60 years and became its public language as well as its capital allocator, turning annual meetings and shareholder letters into lessons on patience, incentives and corporate character. Berkshire now owns dozens of operating companies and holds major stakes in Nvidia, Apple, Amazon, Alphabet and Meta. The handover also rests on a family relationship: Buffett has said Howard receives the chair because he is his son and because he trusts him. Investors will now learn how much of Berkshire’s cohesion came from principles that can be institutionalised, and how much depended on the presence of the person who selected the managers, explained the mistakes and made a vast company feel governed by a single temperament. (Guardian)

The web’s AI economics appear in the companies’ own files

Newly unsealed documents in the New York Times copyright case show people inside Microsoft and OpenAI recognising the threat that generative AI posed to the publishers whose work helped train it. An internal Microsoft paper described a “doom loop” in which AI products weaken the economic foundations of their content suppliers, damaging both the open web and the models that depend on it. Microsoft director Brent Hecht characterised large-scale training as an immense appropriation of human labour and argued that the company’s legal position hollowed out fair use. Microsoft says those comments reflected one employee’s deliberately adversarial, academic perspective and do not represent its legal view. Other records are harder to isolate: Satya Nadella acknowledged chatbots replacing search journeys, and OpenAI personnel discussed memorisation, verbatim reproduction and the absence of a good reason to click through once a user has received an answer. (Verge)

The commercial loop is easy to see. Publishers pay reporters and creators, search sends readers to their pages, and advertising or subscriptions fund the next piece of work. An answer engine can absorb the work, deliver its substance without a visit and then return to a smaller web for future training. OpenAI’s own experts attributed sharp falls in referral traffic to AI summaries and considered declines of as much as 60%. The court will decide the contested copyright questions. The business problem already exists: licensing, prominent links and payment mechanisms need to preserve the production of reliable material before lower traffic removes the very supply of information that makes the systems useful. (Verge)

A separate security episode showed how quickly the tools are changing the cost of technical work. Three independent researchers used Anthropic’s Claude models to compromise OpenAI employee accounts through a flaw in the image-processing system of Discourse, which hosts OpenAI’s community forum. In less than 72 hours they reached the company’s GitHub environment and submitted a pull request from an employee’s Codex account as proof, without opening the internal code repository itself. They adapted the method to multiple companies using under $3,000 in model tokens. The flaws have been fixed and OpenAI paid a $6,500 bug bounty. A tiny authorised team achieved the result with consumer AI subscriptions; hostile groups can buy the same acceleration without stopping at proof. (Verge)

Apple’s new AI asks for more space on its newest phones

The iPhone 18 Pro and Pro Max, Apple Watch Series 12 and Ultra 4, and AirPods 5 have reached shops worldwide, with several iPhone configurations already backordered for weeks. The foldable iPhone Duo follows in October. Launch day also makes the physical cost of Apple’s on-device AI clearer. The iPhone 18 Pro models, iPhone Duo, iPhone 17 Pro models and iPhone Air may need 14GB of free storage for all iOS 27 intelligence features, up from the 8GB required on other compatible phones. The extra space supports a larger local model for more expressive Siri voices and more accurate dictation. Keeping those computations on the device can improve speed and privacy, while turning storage into a continuing software requirement rather than capacity reserved entirely for the owner’s files. (9to5Mac, 9to5Mac)

iOS and iPadOS 27 also add more than 30 languages and regional variants across dictionaries, keyboards, Siri activation and Visual Look Up. QuickType now covers languages including Afrikaans, Basque, Guarani, Kalaallisut, Nheengatu, Xhosa and Zulu, while Visual Look Up adds Brazilian Portuguese among several others. The expansion brings basic system participation to communities often added long after English and a small group of large languages. Its unevenness also shows the layers involved: a keyboard may gain language support before autocorrection, predictions, voice recognition and advanced AI arrive. For users, a device becomes meaningfully local one function at a time. (9to5Mac)

The UN changes the shape of the familiar world

The UN general assembly has voted by 164 countries to one to adopt the Equal Earth map projection, an eight-year-old design that shows continents at their true relative size. The United States cast the only opposing vote and six countries abstained. Equal Earth is intended to replace the Mercator projection gradually on school walls and government websites. Mercator remains excellent for navigation because it preserves direction, but its stretching of the globe enlarges land near the poles. Greenland and Europe appear far bigger beside Africa than they really are, giving a visual advantage to northern powers each time the map is treated as a neutral picture of the planet. Equal Earth preserves area while giving continents shapes that its creators hope people will find easier to read than earlier equal-area designs. (Guardian)

The resolution was proposed by Togo and has already drawn disputes over the colours and borders on sample maps, including the Kuril Islands and Russian-occupied Crimea. Those markings came from downloadable versions prepared by cartographer Tom Patterson rather than from the mathematical projection endorsed by the UN, and are being revised. Patterson created Equal Earth in 2018 with software engineer Bojan Šavrič and Monash University cartographer Bernhard Jenny. Their design still makes compromises: countries at the edges are compressed, New Zealand is stretched, and the map is unsuitable for navigation. That limitation contains the useful lesson. A spherical world cannot be flattened without choosing what to distort, where to put the centre and which way to face. Changing the classroom map can correct one inherited bias while teaching why every map expresses a purpose. (Guardian)

Max Dowman makes the quiet approach harder for Arsenal

Arsenal have tried to protect Max Dowman from the noise that gathers around every gifted teenager, and two goals against Ipswich have made restraint more difficult. His second compressed the qualities causing the excitement into a few seconds: a short movement to create space, control of a bouncing ball, feet quicker than the nearby defender and a finish chosen without breaking stride. The first came from the other side, with a small feint and another calm finish past Cédric Kipré. Dowman is already six feet tall, growing stronger and comfortable inside the tactical and physical systems that define the modern game. His creativity appears through tiny windows of time rather than freedom from structure. (Guardian)

The club’s task is to let ability become reliability without turning each appearance into a referendum on his future. Today’s academies measure workload, recovery and stress with a precision unavailable when Michael Owen or Wayne Rooney emerged. That care can protect Dowman’s body, though it cannot remove the instant scrutiny surrounding elite football. He looks unusually prepared because the senior game asks for a faster, more intense version of the patterns he has learned for years. Arsenal can ration his minutes and keep expectations attached to the present: a young player has produced a brilliant performance, understands the spaces around him and has earned another opportunity when his development allows it. (Guardian)

A $40m game offers an alternative to blockbuster scale

The Blood of Dawnwalker cost about $40m to develop and sold more than one million copies within days of release, a striking result in an industry where large games commonly spend five times as much before marketing. Rebel Wolves began the open-world role-playing game in Warsaw with a small, self-funded team led by former Witcher 3 and Cyberpunk 2077 designer Konrad Tomaszkiewicz. NetEase later financed the project and bought 26% of the studio. The workforce grew from about 90 people in 2024 to 160, a size Tomaszkiewicz now wants to preserve so he can remain involved as a creator. Reviews have been strong and the player base has held after launch, giving publisher Bandai Namco a success with a much lower financial threshold than a conventional blockbuster. (IGN)

The contrast arrives as other games keep expanding in size as well as cost. Square Enix says Final Fantasy VII Revelation, the conclusion to its remake trilogy, will require roughly 200GB when it launches in April 2027. Director Naoki Hamaguchi says the team refused to reduce the quality of a game representing more than a decade of work, even as a physical edition will contain only part of it. Both projects are ambitious, and they point towards different kinds of abundance: one builds a focused studio and accepts boundaries; the other uses storage and production scale to finish a generational undertaking. Players ultimately experience the worlds, characters and time demanded of them. The budgets and download sizes reveal the industrial choices waiting behind the title screen. (IGN)
