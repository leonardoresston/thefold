Reform’s conference becomes a test of Farage’s control

Nigel Farage ended Reform UK’s conference day with two senior aides suspended, a likely police investigation taking shape and the party he built arguing over who should carry the blame. Dan Jukes, his closest adviser for more than a decade, and James Orr, Reform’s head of policy, stepped back after undercover footage appeared to show them helping an ineligible American donor route money into polling that benefited the party. The Metropolitan police are assessing a complaint concerning Jukes, Orr and Farage. All three deny breaking political funding law. Cambridge University, where Orr is an associate professor of divinity, is also examining the matter. (Guardian, BBC)

The apparent scheme began with a £30,000 payment by an American company to JL Partners for polling intended to strengthen Reform’s message before May’s local elections. The work was not declared to the Electoral Commission. Jukes then discussed moving a proposed £500,000 donation through the donor’s British son, also an undercover reporter, and introduced the supposed family to Farage over lunch in Clacton. Farage thanked them and asked whether the arrangement was above board. On Friday he described the episode as entrapment, called the aides’ conduct unauthorised and said the party’s internal controls would have prevented an unlawful donation. (Guardian)

Those answers have shifted the dispute from the footage itself to Farage’s command of his organisation. Jukes was presented by allies as a contractor who carried bags and booked transport, a description difficult to reconcile with his long role in Reform’s strategy and communications. Home affairs spokesperson Zia Yusuf publicly distanced himself from both aides, while party figures speculated about future leadership rivals and a possible reconciliation with Rupert Lowe’s Restore Britain. A conference intended to display discipline instead exposed the personal factions beneath Reform’s rapid growth. (Guardian)

Farage’s speech promised action within a first hundred days in government and hinted at political influence over judicial appointments. It landed after a summer of questions about his finances, including an undeclared £5m gift, and while police already examine allegations that other donations reached Reform through intermediaries. The legal questions will turn on payment trails, declared benefits and knowledge. The political one is immediate: a leader who asks voters to entrust him with the machinery of government must show that he knows how his own senior team raises money and who is authorised to speak in the party’s name. (BBC, Guardian)

News

Russia reaches the heart of Ukraine’s security state

A Russian drone struck the central headquarters of Ukraine’s SBU intelligence agency in Kyiv on Friday, injuring at least nine people and sending smoke above a government district beside St Sophia Cathedral. The aircraft appears to have been aimed at the office of the SBU’s chief. Government buildings in central Kyiv are rarely hit, particularly in daylight, and Ukraine’s foreign minister described the attack as a major escalation. President Volodymyr Zelenskyy ordered the service to prepare a tangible response. The SBU has organised some of Ukraine’s most ambitious operations inside Russia, including last year’s destruction of military aircraft in Operation Spiderweb. (Guardian)

The strike came after more than a week of daily missile and drone barrages and two days before US negotiators Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner were expected in Kyiv. New Russian jet-powered drones can fly at about 250mph, while Ukraine faces a critical shortage of the American Patriot interceptors able to defeat fast incoming weapons. Zelenskyy proposed a mutual pause in attacks while the envoys visit Kyiv and Moscow, with Ukraine guaranteeing their safety in Russian airspace. Diplomacy has stalled as Washington’s attention has moved towards its war with Iran. The damage to the SBU building now raises both the pressure to retaliate and a harder question about whether Kyiv can still shield its most sensitive institutions. (Guardian)

Brazil’s Supreme Court crisis turns into open conflict

A struggle over the Banco Master investigation has broken through the collegial surface of Brazil’s Supreme Court. André Mendonça released a Federal Police report containing messages between fellow justice Alexandre de Moraes and imprisoned banker Daniel Vorcaro. Moraes answered by asking court president Edson Fachin to investigate Mendonça for abuse of authority, administrative misconduct and an impeachable offence, alleging political direction of the inquiries into Master and fraud at the national social-security institute. One faction of the court now wants Mendonça temporarily removed from the Master case while that accusation is examined. (Meio, Folha)

Fachin has begun separating the institutional response from the feud. He removed Moraes’s request from the long-running fake-news inquiry and ordered Mendonça, Moraes, prosecutor general Paulo Gonet and Federal Police chief Andrei Rodrigues to explain within five days their contacts or handling of Vorcaro’s messages. The document Moraes relied on in seeking action against Mendonça was anonymous and classified by police as having no evidential value. Meanwhile, an institute founded by Mendonça received R$11.5m through 68 no-bid contracts with public bodies in 15 states since 2024; the justice says he withdrew from it in 2018 and has no involvement in its management. (Folha, Folha, Folha)

The rupture arrives one month before the presidential election, giving every camp an incentive to turn judicial scrutiny into campaign material. Lula has called for deep reform of the judiciary and wants the Master allegations pursued regardless of whom they hurt. Flávio Bolsonaro’s campaign sees the disclosures as a way to remobilise its base, and Ronaldo Caiado has brought the court crisis into television campaigning. Evidence still has to be tested case by case. The court’s immediate task is to build a process credible enough to investigate its own members while they accuse one another of misusing the power to investigate. (Meio, Folha)

The Falklands return to the space between Britain and America

Britain has restated its commitment to the Falkland Islanders’ right to determine their future after Javier Milei said global conditions increasingly favoured Argentina’s sovereignty claim. The islands voted by 1,513 to three to remain a British overseas territory in 2013. Foreign secretary Ed Miliband described that choice as grounded in international law, while defence secretary Wes Streeting said Milei’s intervention revealed more about Argentine domestic politics than the islands. Buenos Aires has also threatened to exclude companies developing the Sea Lion oilfield north of the archipelago from future business in Argentina. (Guardian, BBC)

The uncertainty comes from Washington. The United States has traditionally recognised British administration while remaining neutral on sovereignty, but Donald Trump suggested he could review that position to press Britain over defence spending. He later linked the question to London’s refusal to provide British bases for his opening attack on Iran and doubted whether today’s Britain could repeat its 1982 military response. Milei presents himself as a reliable western ally and sees leverage in his close relationship with Trump. A territorial dispute governed by the islanders’ expressed choice is being pulled into a larger bargain over loyalty, oil and military support. (BBC, Guardian)

Britain’s food system carries four narrow gateways

More than a third of Britain’s food comes from abroad, and most of those imports pass through Dover, Felixstowe, Southampton and London Gateway. That concentration sits at the centre of a National Audit Office warning that preparation has not kept pace with the rising risk from extreme weather, disease, cyberattack and war. Defra has improved some emergency arrangements since the pandemic and Russia’s invasion of Ukraine, yet it has reduced contact with food companies and has given local resilience forums too little clarity about their role. The last national exercise for a catastrophic food disruption took place in 2023 and excluded most supply-chain businesses. (Guardian, BBC)

The government classifies food as critical national infrastructure, but cold stores and transport hubs do not receive the same protection for their electricity supplies. Industry groups want those links added, and the watchdog wants emergency plans tested with the businesses expected to carry them out. Households are also less prepared for severe disruption than those in several European countries. Ministers are encouraging people to hold several days of long-life food, drinking water, medication and batteries. A cupboard can soften a short interruption; functioning ports, refrigerated warehouses and credible local plans determine whether it stays short. (Guardian, BBC)

Farm costs are already showing how quickly a distant conflict reaches the food chain. Renewed US-Iran fighting and Russia’s extension of its diesel-export ban pushed British red diesel up by 10p to 14p a litre in a few days, roughly a 10% rise, during a period when farmers are planting and irrigating. Standard diesel averaged 183.5p a litre on Thursday, up from 164.5p in mid-July. Producers cannot easily decide when to fill tanks while wholesale prices jump through the day, and higher energy costs eventually travel into growing, processing and delivery. Resilience therefore begins before an empty shelf: it is the capacity to absorb the next fuel bill without losing farms and suppliers along the way. (Guardian)

The world map makes room for Africa

The UN general assembly has voted 164 to one to phase out the Mercator projection in its work and favour equal-area maps based on the 2018 Equal Earth design. Six countries abstained and the United States cast the only vote against. The resolution is non-binding, yet UN adoption should influence classrooms, official publications and digital products. Mercator was designed in 1569 for navigation and enlarges land near the poles: Greenland appears similar in size to Africa even though Africa is fourteen times larger. (Guardian)

Togo led the campaign for the African Union, arguing that habitual distortion diminishes the continent in the global imagination. France will move to the Eckert IV equal-area projection, and Togo plans to bring Unesco, the AU and technology companies together next year to discuss implementation. Every flat map compromises shape, distance or area; the choice depends on purpose. The UN’s decision asks institutions to stop treating a centuries-old sailing tool as a neutral portrait of the world. (Guardian)

Technology

Government requests reach deeper into Apple accounts

Governments and private parties made 29,948 requests to Apple for device data worldwide between July and December 2025, down from 34,563 in the comparable 2024 period. The number of accounts named in requests moved the other way, rising from 28,745 to 32,240. Emergency demands involving an imminent risk of death or physical injury increased from 2,814 to 3,343. Apple’s latest transparency report records what authorities asked the company to supply and how often some information was provided; it does not show that every person attached to a request was suspected of wrongdoing. (MacMagazine)

Brazil ranked third behind the United States and Germany when requests across devices, accounts, financial identifiers, emergencies, preservation and digital content were combined. Its authorities submitted 11,740 demands. Apple provided data for about 78% of Brazilian device and account requests and 68% of emergency requests. The figures matter because phones now gather communications, movements, payments and relationships in one place. Transparency reports give the public a view of state demand, but useful scrutiny also needs clear legal grounds, the number of people affected and independent checks on whether each disclosure was proportionate. (MacMagazine)

Audacity rebuilds the familiar free studio

Audacity 4 has arrived with the audio editor’s largest redesign in years. Its interface now runs on Qt 6, supports high-density displays, dark and high-contrast themes, and brings Audio Units, Nyquist and VST3 plugins to the Mac version. Editing becomes non-destructive, so a trimmed clip can be restored without returning to an earlier file. Users can group and edit several clips, move material between mono and stereo projects, export loop regions and work with a redesigned spectrogram. (Verge, MacMagazine)

Projects now use the .aup4 format. Version 3 files can be opened and converted, though a converted project cannot be saved back to the older format. The application remains free and open source, an unusual position for software used across podcasts, music, education and community radio. The update gives people a more forgiving workflow while preserving the ability to keep their recordings and project files outside a subscription service. (Verge, MacMagazine)

Tetris finds itself inside a White House arcade

The Trump administration has opened Arcade.gov with five browser games built around its political programme. One, Build the Wall, copies the falling-block form of Tetris and asks players to protect the border from an approaching group. Other titles borrow from Flappy Bird and Snake while turning deportation, border patrol and children’s Trump investment accounts into game mechanics. Official Tetris channels disavowed the project, stressed that the company had no role in it and said the apparent copyright infringement was under review. No legal action had been announced by Friday night. (IGN, Verge)

The site extends the administration’s use of gaming imagery after earlier official posts borrowed from Halo and Pokémon. Rights holders have responded unevenly, leaving a practical question over how quickly a government can appropriate recognisable commercial worlds before a legal objection catches up. Games turn rules into instinct through repetition. Using a familiar puzzle to present a group of people as a hostile mass makes the political message playable, shareable and deceptively light. That is why the dispute reaches beyond ownership of blocks on a screen. (IGN)

Culture

George Lucas builds a museum for the pictures between categories

The Lucas Museum of Narrative Art will open in Los Angeles on 22 September, eight years after construction began and about two decades after George Lucas first pursued the idea. The spaceship-like building beside the University of Southern California holds more than 40,000 works collected by Lucas and his wife, Mellody Hobson. Its galleries move from cave paintings and religious mosaics to comic strips, children’s illustration, manga, photography, street art and cinema. Frida Kahlo, Norman Rockwell, Banksy, Beatrix Potter, Picasso, Astro Boy and Spider-Man share an institution devoted to images that tell stories. (Guardian)

The museum took shape after earlier plans in San Francisco and Chicago fell through. Architect Ma Yansong says trees and clouds informed the design, although the building’s curves, round elevators and sky-facing oculus inevitably carry a trace of Star Wars. Two cinemas and a research library sit alongside galleries organised by medium, theme and individual artist. Production designs, costumes and vehicle models from Lucas’s films form one strand in the larger collection. A mural room includes work by Judith Baca, JR and Banksy, including images shaped by immigration and the US-Mexico border. (Guardian)

Labels give the basic facts, while visitors can scan works for further context. Curators deliberately avoided prescribing an interpretation, reflecting Lucas and Hobson’s argument that advertising, illustration and popular entertainment deserve the same close encounter routinely offered to fine art. The combination will sometimes feel unruly. That is part of its value. Museums have often separated the images people live with from the art they are taught to respect; this one puts both into the same visual history and lets the visitor decide what belongs. (Guardian)

Aardman celebrates fifty years one fingerprint at a time

Aardman Animations is marking its fiftieth year with exhibitions, an auction and a reminder that some of its best decisions began as practical improvisation. Nick Park first imagined Gromit as a cat but found the model too difficult and made him a dog; Shaun the Sheep nearly spoke before silence gave the character a universal comic language. Peter Lord and David Sproxton founded the Bristol studio in 1976 around Morph for the children’s programme Take Hart. It later carried Wallace and Gromit, Chicken Run and Shaun from careful clay movement into a global audience. (Guardian)

More than 150 objects are on show at Bristol’s M Shed, and the Young V&A in Bethnal Green has models, sets and storyboards from the studio’s archive. An auction beginning this weekend offers over 220 lots, including puppets of Wallace, Gromit, Feathers McGraw and Shaun, with proceeds supporting bursaries and training for emerging film-makers. Aardman’s characters carry visible fingerprints because the method records the hands that moved them. Fifty years of technical refinement have kept that human evidence on screen, giving even the most elaborate chase the warmth of something made frame by frame. (Guardian)

Sports

Isak gives Liverpool’s new era its first win

Alexander Isak scored twice in the opening ten minutes as Liverpool beat Ipswich 2–0, delivering Andoni Iraola’s first victory as head coach. Cody Gakpo created both goals, and Isak had a third ruled out for offside before half-time. The forward had endured a difficult debut season after becoming Liverpool’s costliest signing under outgoing sporting director Richard Hughes, but has now followed his goal against Nottingham Forest with a pair of sharp finishes. Ipswich recovered from their chaotic start and found space around Liverpool’s midfield without producing the quality to change the result. (Guardian, BBC)

The travelling supporters also saw the debut of £123m winger Bradley Barcola, who came from the bench after playing no competitive minutes since the World Cup. His acceleration from the left immediately stretched Ipswich and created a late chance for Gakpo. Liverpool’s summer spending now places Isak, Barcola and Florian Wirtz in the same attack, with Gakpo retained despite interest from Manchester City and Tottenham. Iraola still lacks an obvious commanding defensive midfielder, and stronger opponents will test the open spaces. At Portman Road, the speed and invention promised by his appointment finally appeared together. (Guardian)
