Two cheques force Westminster’s hand

The government is preparing to rewrite political donation rules in a way that could prevent Reform UK from accepting the two £36m gifts announced at the weekend. Amendments to the representation of the people bill are expected in the House of Lords within weeks. They would require an individual donor to prove a genuine, continuing physical presence in Britain, aligning political finance more closely with residence tests used elsewhere in government. The measure is planned to have retrospective force. That puts Ben Delo, who has been based in Hong Kong, and Christopher Harborne, previously based in Thailand, directly in its path. Together their donations would give one party £72m from two people, an amount without precedent in British politics. (Guardian)

The bill already limits donations from British citizens living overseas to £100,000 and applies the same ceiling during their first 12 months back in the country, with the clock backdated to 25 March. Ministers have added a minimum residence period covering the rest of the calendar year in which someone returns and the whole of the next year. On that timetable, Reform may have to wait until January 2028 before taking the money. Nigel Farage maintains that both gifts comply with current law and the version of the bill approved by MPs, though he acknowledged that further government action could catch them. Harborne registered to vote in Hampshire in June; Delo said in April that he intended to return to the UK. Their precise movements now matter as much as the figures on their cheques. (Guardian)

Residency leaves the largest question open. Britain still has no general cap on how much a person based here may give, and Labour MPs and peers are pressing for powers to impose one later. Unite has backed a ceiling for individual donations, while other unions fear that a future government could use the same principle against the collective political funds built from members’ subscriptions. Ministers are also considering lower party spending limits and a longer period in which those limits apply before a general election. Each choice has to distinguish between millions supplied by one individual and small contributions aggregated through an organisation, without writing rules that conveniently favour whoever is in office. (Guardian)

The immediate response is inherently awkward: legislation aimed at donations already announced will be accused of targeting a rival party, however broad its wording. Delay carries its own cost. A national campaign operation can be built years before an election, while the formal spending controls are concentrated near polling day. The government says work on stronger rules began weeks ago, after pressure in the Commons, and has created a cross-departmental team to consider political finance and illegitimate influence. It now has to produce a rule that survives the obvious test: would it still look fair if the same £72m were heading to Labour or the Conservatives? The answer will decide whether this is remembered as a partisan intervention or the moment two extraordinary gifts finally made Britain set a durable boundary around private political power. (Guardian)

News

The crown’s mark emerges from the ledger

A new historical register has placed Britain’s monarchs inside the machinery of the transatlantic slave trade with names, investments and administrative records. Its most arresting document is a 1714 instruction from the South Sea Company board to agents in Buenos Aires. A doctor had warned that the company’s brand was large enough to endanger the people on whom it was burned. The directors ordered the practice to continue and sent a smaller silver mark shaped as a crown, to be heated red hot and applied to the left shoulder. The image was a statement of ownership and of royal authority, decided in a London boardroom and inflicted across an ocean. (Guardian)

The Register of British Slave Traders documents thousands of investors in an enterprise that trafficked more than three million African people over almost 250 years. Royal participation ran from Elizabeth I’s support for early voyages in the 1560s through Stuart companies and into the reign of William IV, who opposed abolition for decades. Charles II established the Company of Royal Adventurers and its successor, the Royal African Company. His brother James, Duke of York and later James II, governed the company, owned a substantial stake and held meetings in his apartments at Whitehall Palace. The company trafficked more people than any other single organisation in the history of the trade and used brands bearing “DY” and “RACE”, including on children from the age of three. (Guardian)

Queen Anne secured the asiento in the 1713 peace settlement with Spain: an agreement for Britain to supply 4,800 enslaved African men each year to Spanish colonies in the Americas. She gave the contract to the South Sea Company and held rights to almost a quarter of its profits. The company carried 43,220 captives across the Atlantic between 1714 and 1740 and moved more than 50,000 people within the Americas; almost 7,000 died aboard its ships. Charles III has expressed sorrow over slavery and supported research into the monarchy’s links, but neither the crown nor successive governments have issued the formal acknowledgement and apology sought by Caribbean nations. Jamaica delivered a petition to the king last week asking whether Britain owes restitution for slavery and its lasting effects. The register gives that argument a harder foundation. It turns a general inheritance into a record of decisions, beneficiaries and human consequences. (Guardian)

Pressure gathers at Europe’s border and Arabia’s straits

Nato and European leaders have answered Russia’s drone strike on a passenger train near the Polish border with promises of greater support for Ukraine. The attack on Sunday came shortly after a train carrying Boris Johnson, former Swedish prime minister Carl Bildt and European security advisers passed through the crossing. Ukrainian Railways says that service left ahead of schedule and may have been the intended target. A separate train carrying former CIA director David Petraeus was still at Yahodyn station when the drone hit. No one on the struck train was injured, but the locomotive was destroyed on a civilian route that connects Ukraine with the European Union. (Guardian)

The strike belonged to an overnight assault of more than 450 drones and missiles, which killed at least six people and injured more than 40 across Ukraine. The air force says it intercepted 405 drones and four missiles. Western regions that have generally been quieter than Kyiv and the eastern front were hit especially hard, and Poland issued air-raid warnings. Donald Tusk said allied intelligence had anticipated an attempt to disrupt or destroy Ukrainian border crossings and warned that Russian escalation could reach Polish territory in the coming weeks and months. At the same time, EU diplomats were trying to renew sanctions on more than 2,600 Russian individuals and organisations before their expiry, with the decision held up by a dispute over removing one name. The military and political clocks are moving together: the route for people and aid is exposed while Europe negotiates the machinery meant to raise the cost for Moscow. (Guardian)

Further south, the routes around the Arabian peninsula are tightening as Yemen’s renewed war spills across borders. Gulf states postponed a long-prepared meeting with Iran on the Strait of Hormuz after failing to agree among themselves and as Saudi anger over Tehran’s support for the Houthis grew. The rebels launched another wave of missiles and drones at Saudi military targets, claiming strikes on King Khalid airbase. An Iran-backed militia in Iraq had already forced Saudi Arabia to close an important east–west oil pipeline, while Iran has effectively closed Hormuz. Houthi forces then took Yemen’s Red Sea coast and the Greater and Lesser Hanish islands, giving them control around Bab al-Mandab, the other narrow maritime gate. (Guardian)

The postponed Gulf meeting was intended to formalise a temporary route for commercial ships once Hormuz reopened. It would have run entirely through Iranian waters, recognising Tehran’s practical control, while Iran also demands relief at its oil ports, the release of frozen assets and a ceasefire in Lebanon. Saudi crown prince Mohammed bin Salman held urgent talks with the head of US Central Command in Jeddah; Washington has so far provided logistics and intelligence rather than direct strikes against the Houthis. With a pipeline disabled and both maritime exits under pressure, the conflict can raise transport costs and constrain energy supplies far beyond the battlefield. Diplomacy still offers the only route to reopen Hormuz safely, but the states expected to negotiate it are entering the room with fewer shared assumptions and more targets under fire. (Guardian)

Brazil’s Supreme Court enters its own trial

Brazil’s Supreme Court meets today for an extraordinary session on the conduct of justice Alexandre de Moraes and the police material linking him to former Banco Master owner Daniel Vorcaro. Chief justice Edson Fachin will speak first, followed by Moraes and then the remaining ministers in seniority order. The court can open an investigation into Moraes or annul the federal police report, as the prosecutor general has requested. Fachin has set out only a general procedure, leaving the precise votes and even the session’s path uncertain. A case built around one justice’s contacts has expanded into a test of how the whole court handles evidence involving its own members, only weeks before the presidential election. (Folha, Meio)

The federal police delivered complete copies of Vorcaro’s phone extraction to the offices of Cristiano Zanin, Gilmar Mendes and Moraes on Monday morning. The original device and extraction remain sealed inside the agency under a documented chain of custody, with digital integrity checks. Zanin said access was essential to forming his view and stressed that there is no hierarchy among Supreme Court justices. André Mendonça opposed distributing the full material, arguing that it could disrupt today’s hearing and damage other investigations. That disagreement carries the weight of the wider Banco Master inquiry: the evidence may illuminate Moraes’s position, yet it also contains information about people and cases outside the question immediately before the court. (Folha, Meio)

The crisis is already spreading beyond the bench. Police material alleges the sale of influence involving cases at the Supreme Court, the Superior Court of Justice and the electoral court; separate inquiries concern payments and the financing of the film Dark Horse. Politicians fear a cascade of disclosures, while presidential candidates are turning the dispute into campaign material. Institutional confidence will depend on a visible method: clear limits on what today’s session decides, equal access to the evidence used for that decision, and protection for unrelated investigations. The court cannot remove politics from the moment. It can show that scrutiny of a justice follows rules strong enough to withstand the election around it. (Folha, Meio)

Britain becomes the buyer of last resort for speciality steel

Speciality Steel UK is moving towards public ownership after a private sale failed, preserving 1,300 jobs across Rotherham, Stocksbridge, Brinsworth and Wednesbury. The company entered administration last year after the collapse of its former owner Sanjeev Gupta’s industrial empire and years of financial strain following the failure of Greensill Capital. At the time, a judge described it as hopelessly insolvent; only £650,000 remained in the bank. The state has since paid about £3.5m a month to keep workers employed and equipment maintained, although the plants have produced no steel for a year. Talks with Norwegian startup Blastr Green Steel passed a June deadline without a completed deal. (Guardian)

The intervention keeps capacity that ministers consider strategic for defence, energy infrastructure and advanced manufacturing. SSUK is Britain’s largest producer of lower-emission steel made in electric arc furnaces, giving the sites a role in the government’s industrial and climate plans as well as their local economies. Blastr insists it had a fully financed proposal that could have closed within 12 weeks at no cost to taxpayers and says it was not warned of the decision. The government has not disclosed why the offer failed its test for long-term stability. That answer matters because public ownership begins with two obligations: secure the skills and furnaces that would be difficult to rebuild, then establish how they can produce and compete again. Paying wages to preserve an idle asset has bought time. Acquisition turns that time into responsibility. (Guardian)

America removes a brake as electricity demand climbs

The US Environmental Protection Agency is eliminating federal limits on greenhouse-gas emissions from coal and gas power stations and surrendering its authority to regulate those emissions under the Clean Air Act. Its own calculation says the change will add 123m metric tonnes of carbon over the next decade while saving plant operators $370m in direct compliance costs. Power generation produces about a quarter of US greenhouse gases. The discarded 2024 standards were projected to prevent one billion tonnes of emissions by 2047, produce $370bn in net benefits, and improve air quality enough to avoid 1,200 premature deaths and 360,000 asthma attacks in 2035. Environmental groups have promised a court challenge. (Guardian, Verge)

The timing links climate policy to the race for computing power. Data centres are driving sharp forecasts for electricity demand, and the administration wants ageing coal stations and new gas capacity available to meet it. Earlier this year it also revoked the EPA’s 2009 finding that greenhouse gases endanger public health, the legal foundation for many federal climate rules, and removed standards for cars and trucks. Courts will decide how much of that structure can be dismantled. The practical result in the meantime is a strong incentive to build AI infrastructure around the cheapest quickly available generation, with its pollution shifted into the atmosphere and surrounding communities. Faster chips may reduce the energy used for an individual task. A sector expanding this quickly can overwhelm those gains. The power source therefore belongs inside every serious account of AI’s cost. (Guardian, Verge)

Technology

Apple’s quieter update puts Siri at the centre

iOS 27 and iPadOS 27 are now available, leading a full day of Apple software releases that also included macOS 27 Golden Gate, watchOS 27, visionOS 27, tvOS 27 and new HomePod and AirPods firmware. The headline addition is Siri AI, a more conversational assistant able to use personal context from messages, email and photos, understand material on screen and take actions across supported apps. A dedicated app preserves conversations across Apple devices, while a camera mode applies visual intelligence to objects around the user. These features require an iPhone 15 Pro or newer compatible model, and access is initially managed through a waitlist. Owners of older phones still receive the broader system update, with iOS 26.7 offered to those who prefer to stay on the previous version. (9to5Mac, MacMagazine)

The release gives users more control over last year’s Liquid Glass design, including a slider from clear to fully tinted, and spreads smaller repairs across the system. Photos can extend an image beyond its original frame, alter composition through spatial reframing and remove larger objects. Safari groups tabs by subject and can notify a user when a watched page changes. Alarms and timers gain separate volume controls; parental tools can approve individual sites and schedule apps; accessibility updates extend VoiceOver, Magnifier, Reader and Voice Control. Apple also says apps launch faster, new photos load sooner and AirDrop transfers improve. MacStories’ months of testing found the release unusually stable and valued the many small corrections across Notes, Music, Photos and Shortcuts as much as the assistant. (MacStories, 9to5Mac)

Siri AI also sharpens Apple’s privacy bargain. The system can reach into a person’s private information because it is integrated with the operating system, an advantage competing chatbots cannot easily reproduce. Apple is separately asking permission before user data is shared to improve its foundation models, and code in the new systems points towards limited replacement of Siri AI with Claude or ChatGPT in some circumstances. The assistant’s general reasoning still trails the leading standalone models in demanding tasks, while its access to apps makes ordinary requests more useful. That combination will define the update for most people: an AI service woven more deeply into the phone, accompanied by a clearer need to understand which model answers, what information it sees and whether anything leaves the device. Backing up before installation remains the sensible first step. Reading the data prompts comes next. (MacMagazine, 9to5Mac, MacStories)

Culture

Oasis book another return to Knebworth

Oasis will carry their reunion into 2027 with a tour that begins at Glasgow’s Celtic Park on 21 May and ends with four September nights at Knebworth. The itinerary includes 11 shows at Manchester’s Etihad Stadium, two nights each in Munich, Barcelona, Amsterdam, Paris and Rome, three in Boston, three in Las Vegas and two at Slane Castle. Knebworth supplies the anniversary and the scale: the band played to 250,000 people there across two nights in August 1996, while an estimated 2.5 million applied for tickets. Next year’s licence allows crowds of 125,000, bringing the Gallaghers back to the place most closely identified with their first peak after a 41-date reunion tour in 2025. (Guardian, BBC)

The ticket process carries evidence of what changed after that reunion. UK prices, ranging from £71.50 to £266.60, have been published in advance. Ticketmaster must now disclose at least 24 hours beforehand whether tiered pricing will be used, which seats it covers and how it works, then keep the available range visible as tickets sell. In 2024, buyers expecting a £148.50 ticket sometimes reached checkout to find a £350 price and only minutes to decide. Fans must register by 4pm BST on 17 September in an attempt to reduce queues and organised touting. Nostalgia remains an immense commercial engine: five Heaton Park dates last year helped music-tourist spending in north-west England rise 16% to £1.4bn. This return will test whether the machinery around the spectacle can become as carefully rehearsed as the songs. (Guardian, BBC)

Sports

Leeds turn a good start into a statement

Leeds United are third in the Premier League after dismantling Newcastle 4–1 at Elland Road. Ao Tanaka’s shot took two deflections for the opener, Jayden Bogle headed in James Justin’s cross two minutes later and Dominic Calvert-Lewin’s measured finish made it 3–0 before half-time. Noah Okafor drove the fourth through a crowded area after the break; Bazoumana Touré supplied Newcastle’s late consolation. Daniel Farke’s change to a back three last November helped save his job and carry Leeds to safety, and a strong summer has turned that recovery into a faster start than expected. Calvert-Lewin and Bogle now have credible cases for the England squad due this week. Newcastle’s new manager Matthias Jaissle met his first serious setback in a match where his side never matched the home team’s intensity. (Guardian, BBC)

The weekend’s other major Premier League decision continued after the whistle. The video officials who confirmed Manchester City’s disputed winner at Old Trafford have been removed from this weekend’s fixtures. Pro Ref accepts that Enzo Fernández interfered from an offside position as Erling Haaland scored and that the referee should have been sent to the monitor. The admission cannot return Manchester United’s point or change City’s place level with Arsenal at the top. Standing the officials down provides accountability; explaining the visual judgement would offer more. Offside interference remains one of football’s least intuitive rules because a player can affect a defender without touching the ball. Clear public reasoning, applied consistently, is the route back from another result whose final review arrived a day too late. (Guardian, BBC)
