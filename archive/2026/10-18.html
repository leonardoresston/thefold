News

Diplomats attacked during West Bank olive harvest

Masked Israeli settlers attacked European diplomats, Palestinian civilians and a French mayor near Taybeh on Saturday, bringing international condemnation and fresh demands for action against settler violence. Belgium’s consul general, French and Polish diplomats and Catholic representatives were among those targeted in the predominantly Christian Palestinian village. Bernard Baudoux, mayor of Aulnoye-Aymeries in northern France, described being knocked to the ground while taking part in the olive harvest alongside local residents. (Sky News)

A Belgian vehicle was damaged. Belgium’s foreign minister, Maxime Prévot, said his diplomatic team was safe and pledged to keep pressing for European Union sanctions against violent settlers. Poland also condemned the targeting of the diplomatic convoy. Israel’s foreign minister, Gideon Sa’ar, denounced the violence and confirmed a police investigation, while criticising the lack of advance coordination with the Israeli military. The attack followed Friday’s assault on Washington Post photographer Heidi Levine, who was hospitalised after covering Palestinians gathering olives. (Sky News)

Israel’s military confirmed the assault and said no soldiers had been at the scene. Police promised to pursue those responsible. Benjamin Netanyahu’s office subsequently warned against drawing premature conclusions and raised the prospect of a campaign to discredit Israel. The violence comes shortly before an Israeli election in which security and the government’s treatment of Palestinians are under scrutiny. Opposition politician Gadi Eisenkot sharply criticised the attackers and the damage they were doing to Israel’s standing. (AP)

The harvest has become a particularly dangerous period for Palestinian communities. At least four people have been killed and more than 100 injured since it began earlier this month, according to the Palestinian health ministry. The West Bank has been under Israeli occupation since 1967; the UN and much of the international community regard the settlements as illegal. Saturday’s assault put visiting diplomats alongside the farmers, activists and journalists already exposed to attacks, with European governments now demanding protection and accountability. (AP)

Brazil’s runoff becomes a test of turnout

Lula and Flávio Bolsonaro are entering the final week of Brazil’s presidential campaign with a shared problem: getting supporters back to polling stations where the local election is already over. Only six states will hold a second round for governor. São Paulo, Minas Gerais and Rio de Janeiro, the country’s three largest electorates, settled their gubernatorial contests on 4 October, and all three gave Flávio an advantage in the presidential first round. Both campaigns are leaning on local allies to sustain the effort until the national runoff on 25 October. (Folha)

Flávio’s team is calling on governors and other elected politicians to mobilise their bases, while Lula’s campaign has set local targets for allies, including candidates who lost their own races. In the north-east, where Lula is strongest, only Rio Grande do Norte has a gubernatorial runoff. That makes maintaining campaign activity and access to the polls especially important. The candidates also have a direct opportunity to reach undecided voters tonight: both have confirmed their participation in the Band debate in São Paulo. (Folha, Folha)

The debate is scheduled to begin at 8pm in São Paulo, midnight in London, with about two hours of broadcasting across television, radio and digital channels. Eduardo Oinegue and Adriana Araújo will moderate. Its opening block includes 30 minutes in which the candidates can confront each other directly, followed by further questions and exchanges. Neither took part in a first-round debate, so this will be their first shared presidential encounter of the campaign. A final debate is planned for TV Globo on 23 October; Flávio’s participation there was still under evaluation in the account setting out tonight’s rules. (Folha)

New Datafolha findings help explain what each campaign is trying to activate. Among Flávio’s supporters, 41% cited change and alternation in power as a reason for their choice, followed by rejection of Lula and the Workers’ party at 31%. Among Lula’s supporters, social protection was the leading motive, at 48%, with his record and legacy cited by 22%. Respondents could give more than one reason. The survey interviewed 2,520 people in 153 municipalities on 13 and 14 October and has a two-point margin of error. It put valid voting intentions at 51% for Flávio and 49% for Lula, a narrow gap that leaves the final days consequential. The findings describe different priorities within the two camps; they do not establish how many supporters will actually turn out next Sunday. (Folha)

Brazil’s electoral court orders equal treatment by Meta

Brazil’s electoral court has unanimously upheld an order requiring Meta to give Lula and Flávio Bolsonaro equal treatment across its platforms until the runoff ends. The ruling bars artificial restrictions on the distribution of campaign content and requires human review when automated systems restrict candidate accounts or paid promotion. Where an automated restriction is applied, Meta must review it within two hours and tell the court what happened, why it happened and the outcome. (Folha)

The order also covers equal criteria for the company’s image-generation tool and restricts election advertising that has not been commissioned by a candidate, their national party or coalition, including advertising paid for from abroad. Non-compliance can bring a daily fine of R$500,000. Lula’s campaign brought the case after alleging repeated problems with distribution and promotion. Meta has disputed allegations of partisan interference and attributed problems to technical failures. The ruling puts the handling of those systems under close scrutiny for the campaign’s remaining days. (Folha)

Swinney renews the independence campaign

John Swinney launched a renewed push to persuade Scotland to leave the UK at the SNP conference in Aberdeen. The Choose Scotland campaign has a six-figure budget. The first minister is trying to turn his party’s fifth consecutive Holyrood election victory into momentum for another referendum, although the SNP fell short of the overall majority he had set as his target. Nationalist first ministers in Wales and Northern Ireland have added to his argument that the UK’s constitutional relationship is changing. (Guardian, PA / Scotland Press)

The immediate route to a vote remains uncertain. Westminster would have to agree to a referendum, and Swinney’s address also stressed delivery through the powers Scotland already has. He confirmed that the £2 bus fare cap would extend to the north-east from April, following its introduction in the Highlands and west central Scotland. He wants the cap available across Scotland by the end of the parliamentary term and the bus network brought under public control through regulation and franchising. (BBC)

Swinney also promised action to make misogyny a specific criminal offence and funding for community groups tackling racism. His speech became personal when he linked his commitment to independence to the future of his 15-year-old son. Andy Burnham had used his visit to Motherwell a day earlier to offer cooperation on deeper devolution to local communities. The two leaders remain open to working together on that agenda, even as they seek very different constitutional futures. (BBC, PA / Scotland Press)

Yemen’s war threatens airports and shipping

The UAE is set to resume funding Yemeni government forces, with Saudi air cover, as the two Gulf states respond to Houthi advances. The Iran-backed movement has repeatedly struck Saudi Arabia’s main international airport and gained territory near an important shipping chokepoint. Saudi Arabia’s superior weaponry has struggled to prevent the attacks. The Houthis’ ability to disperse fighters and equipment across mountainous territory helps them survive air campaigns while retaining the capacity to threaten energy facilities and trade routes. (Guardian, AP)

Economy

Economic resilience has a growing bill

The world economy is continuing to withstand war and disruption, but the room for governments to absorb further shocks is narrowing. At Saturday’s IMF meetings in Bangkok, the fund’s leadership warned about the combined pressure from conflict, high debt, inflation and climate risks. Disruption to shipping routes threatens food and energy security, with poorer countries and fragile states especially exposed. Governments face the difficult task of rebuilding their finances while protecting vulnerable people and preserving investment that can support future growth. (IMF)

Artificial intelligence is helping sustain investment and optimism, while also creating a concentration risk. IMF managing director Kristalina Georgieva warned that relying heavily on one engine of growth leaves the economy vulnerable. The fund sees scope for productivity gains, alongside the possibility that the benefits will be distributed unevenly across countries and workers. Its message to governments was to strengthen institutions and cooperate with technology companies on safeguards while there is still time to shape how the technology spreads. (IMF)

The debate over household support shows why those choices are becoming harder. IMF research published earlier this month found that broad consumer subsidies can require three to six times as much public money as targeted transfers to provide the same protection to poorer households. Producer subsidies can be more expensive still. During Europe’s 2022–23 energy crisis, less than 20 cents of every euro spent holding down electricity, gas and petrol prices reached the poorest fifth of households. Wealthier households also benefited from those schemes because they consumed the subsidised goods. (IMF)

Temporary payments directed through social protection systems can therefore stretch limited budgets further. The fund recognises that governments may need wider measures during acute food insecurity or when they cannot deliver targeted help quickly. Clear expiry dates and careful design then matter. Food and energy shocks take a disproportionate share of poorer families’ income, and wages can remain depressed long after the first price surge has passed. That makes the way support is delivered a central part of managing the crisis. (IMF)

Apprenticeship policy shifts towards younger recruits

Ministers are considering higher employer contributions for older apprentices in England, creating a stronger incentive to recruit younger workers. The proposal comes as the existing funding system is already being redirected towards people aged 16 to 24. Under rules introduced this summer, government covers eligible training and assessment costs up to the funding limit for that age group at employers outside the levy system. It also covers them where a levy-paying employer has exhausted its available funds. (Guardian, Department for Work and Pensions)

A £2,000 hiring payment is available from October to eligible employers outside the levy when they recruit apprentices aged 16 to 24. Other changes have shortened the period in which employers can use newly credited levy funds from two years to one, and removed the automatic 10% top-up. Employers still have to cover costs above a course’s funding band. These details matter for the next stage of reform: hiring support, the amount available for training and the employer’s own contribution each affect the decision to offer a place. The new proposal concerns England; the other UK nations run separate apprenticeship arrangements. (Department for Work and Pensions)

Technology

The web puts limits on AI’s new assistants

AI assistants built to shop, book travel and handle everyday administration are meeting resistance from the websites they need to use. Yelp limits agents to participants in its paid data-licensing programme, Delta is blocking them from booking flights, and JPMorgan Chase and Charles Schwab have cited security concerns in restricting account access. Some failures also come from ordinary anti-bot systems, which can struggle to distinguish a customer’s chosen assistant from unwanted automated traffic. (The Verge)

The dispute raises a practical question for anyone delegating a task: an assistant’s capabilities depend on the services willing to let it act. Retail partnerships offer one route around the barriers, while the legal fight between Amazon and Perplexity continues. Even where commercial agreements exist, checkout and identity checks can still interrupt the process. Companies building agents want websites to treat them as acting for customers; website operators retain concerns about security, control over transactions and the commercial value of the visit. (The Verge)

Meta has meanwhile removed wording from its commercial terms that appeared to allow the use of material from linked websites to train AI. Legal and publishing observers had read the language as potentially overriding a site owner’s instructions against crawling. Meta disputed that interpretation and then deleted the passage. The company says the consolidated crawling terms concern advertising and business products, and that content owners can continue to indicate their preferences through robots.txt. (The Verge)

The change draws attention to two separate permissions: allowing a bot to visit a website and allowing material from that visit to be used for training. Meta says the revision gives it no new rights to disregard website owners’ preferences. For publishers, clear terms are consequential because a link shared through a commercial service can point to a much larger body of work. The removal settles the disputed wording, while wider disagreements over access and the use of online content remain. (The Verge)

Apple tightens rules on sexual deepfake apps

Apple has strengthened its App Store rules to address non-consensual sexual imagery, including material produced with AI. Its user-generated content requirements now explicitly cover this abuse, with developers facing removal if they fail to prevent it. The changes make responsibility for what an app enables more explicit as image-generation tools become easier to distribute. Apple already prohibits pornography in the store; the updated language addresses ways newer tools can be used to create abusive material. (MacMagazine, Apple)

A separate revision targets developers who wait until an app passes review before activating prohibited features. Those developers can lose their apps and membership of the developer programme. Apple has also clarified that apps facilitating human trafficking or child exploitation will be removed and barred from alternative distribution. These are changes to the rules and the sanctions available to enforce them. Their practical effect will depend on identifying violations and responding when developers change an app after approval. (Apple, MacMagazine)

EE sets out the cost of sharing a number across iPhones

EE will include Apple’s iPhone Handoff feature in its Full Works Plus plan and offer it for £5 a month on other plans. The service allows a customer to move between two iPhones while keeping the same mobile number, with calls and messages following the device in use. It requires the network’s support, so its availability and cost are part of the mobile contract as well as the phone’s feature list. (9to5Mac)

The UK service is still listed as coming soon, with Apple having indicated availability by the end of the year. EE is the first UK carrier to announce its pricing. In the US, T-Mobile has set a $5 monthly charge and Verizon a $10 charge. Those differences mean the convenience of using a lighter or spare phone can carry a recurring cost. Customers considering the feature will need to check their plan’s eligibility and the network’s launch date before relying on it. (9to5Mac, 9to5Mac)

A new macOS upgrade exposes old software dependencies

Organisations managing Macs are facing a transition problem around Rosetta, Apple’s compatibility layer for Intel software on Apple silicon. Installing macOS 27 removes Rosetta, although it can still be reinstalled; support is due to end with macOS 28. Some conventional apps prompt users to restore the component when needed. Testing has also found menu-bar utilities that fail without a useful prompt, leaving the person using the Mac with little explanation of what has stopped working. (9to5Mac)

That makes an inventory of older software important before a broad rollout. Reinstalling Rosetta through device-management tools can provide a temporary bridge, while administrators check that vendors have native Apple-silicon versions and test replacements where necessary. Background utilities deserve particular attention because a failure can be easy to miss during a quick application check. The immediate upgrade still allows a workaround. The following macOS release is the deadline that gives the audit its urgency, especially for workplaces where one small utility supports a larger daily task. (9to5Mac)

Sports

City make it seven, Liverpool recover at Brentford

Manchester City beat Ipswich 3–2 to move six points clear at the top of the Premier League. Erling Haaland scored twice and Phil Foden also found the net as Enzo Maresca became the first manager to win his opening seven matches in the competition. City have taken every available point, making the transition from Pep Guardiola exceptionally productive on the pitch. The home crowd’s support was vocal on a day when fans also protested against the Premier League following the club’s financial-rules verdict. (BBC, BBC)

Liverpool came from behind to win 2–1 at Brentford, who finished with nine players. Florian Wirtz created nine chances from open play and supplied the pass for the start of Liverpool’s recovery. Alexander Isak returned from injury to complete the comeback. The result restored momentum after the defeat to City and took Andoni Iraola’s side to fifth, with three victories from his first seven league games in charge. (BBC, BBC)

The win came with fresh injury concerns involving Dominik Szoboszlai and Jeremie Frimpong. Liverpool face Villarreal in the Champions League on Tuesday during a stretch of four matches in ten days. Wirtz’s creativity gave them a route through Brentford, but the balance of a side playing two wingers and a number ten remains a concern. Liverpool created 21 attempts, including 14 after the interval, and still had to protect a narrow lead in the closing stages. (BBC)

Arsenal held again at the Emirates

Arsenal drew 1–1 with Birmingham City in the Women’s Super League, with Olivia Smith’s equaliser cancelling out Océane Hurtré’s opener. Arsenal had controlled much of the ball and Alessia Russo struck the post with a first-half header, but Birmingham went ahead after the break when Hurtré ran through to finish. Smith levelled in the 66th minute with a shot from outside the area. A second Arsenal goal never arrived. (Arseblog)

The match statistics underline the frustration: Arsenal had 76% possession and 27 shots to Birmingham’s five, yet put only four attempts on target. Sophie Baggaley made important saves and late Arsenal chances went begging. The result leaves Renée Slegers’s team with one victory in six league matches. With so much possession producing a single goal, finishing and the quality of the final action remain immediate problems to solve. Birmingham left with a point earned through disciplined defending, their goalkeeper’s interventions and the counterattack that put them ahead. (Arseblog, Sky Sports)
