LEAD STORY

Starmer issues 48-hour ultimatum to resident doctors as six-day Easter strike looms

Prime Minister Keir Starmer gave resident doctors in England a stark 48-hour deadline on Thursday to call off their planned six-day strike — threatening the permanent withdrawal of a landmark pay and training offer if they refuse. The strike, which begins at 7am on Tuesday 7 April and runs until 6:59am on Sunday 13 April, would represent the 15th walkout since the dispute began in March 2023, and the longest to date. NHS hospitals are already planning emergency-only operations, with surgeries, outpatient appointments and diagnostic procedures expected to be cancelled across England. (BMA, Open Access Government, The Canary)

The British Medical Association has rejected the government's offer of a 7.1% salary increase and 4,500 new specialist training places, arguing the deal still leaves resident doctors' pay roughly 20% lower in real terms than in 2008. The government's independent Review Body has recommended 3.5% — below the current RPI figure of 3.6%. The BMA says nothing short of full pay restoration is acceptable; Starmer has repeatedly called the BMA's position "unreasonable" given public finances. The timing is brutal: the strike starts the day after the NHS returns from the Easter bank holiday, when staffing levels are already depleted by annual leave. (BMA, Health Media DHSC Blog, PSL Hub)

NHS England chief executive Amanda Pritchard urged patients to use NHS 111 online as their first port of call over the Easter weekend for non-emergency care, and stressed that A&E and 999 services will remain fully staffed. But with Good Friday already placing strain on GP surgeries and walk-in centres, public health campaigners warned the combination of the bank holiday and the incoming strike amounts to a "catastrophic two-week squeeze" on a health service that has still not recovered from pandemic-era backlogs. More than 7.5 million people are on NHS waiting lists. (Isle of Thanet News, Richmond Council)

"We have made a fair and serious offer. If the BMA chooses to walk away from it to go on strike, that offer will not be on the table again."

— PM Keir Starmer, Downing Street, 2 April 2026

Health Secretary Wes Streeting has said the government will explore "all legal options" to manage the strike impact, including mutual aid arrangements between NHS trusts and a volunteer bank first deployed during the 2023–24 junior doctor disputes. The BMA's resident doctors committee chair responded that the ultimatum was "a political threat dressed up as negotiation" and that members would be balloted on whether to extend industrial action if the government withdrew the offer. Talks hosted by ACAS — the conciliation service — collapsed last week without agreement. (BMA, Open Access Government)

For patients, the arithmetic is stark. NHS England estimates that every day of resident doctor strike action cancels approximately 20,000 appointments and procedures. A six-day walkout could result in around 120,000 cancellations — adding further pressure to a waiting list that has barely moved despite the government's flagship 18-week target campaign. The strike will also hit patients in the last stages of cancer treatment pathways, though the BMA says it maintains minimum service agreements for critical and emergency care throughout. (QEH Media Hub, PSL Hub)

UK POLITICS

Trump hikes UK tariff to 15% — Starmer's deal in tatters

The government's carefully negotiated preferential trade arrangement with Washington has effectively collapsed. President Trump raised the blanket global tariff rate to 15% — up from the 10% rate Britain thought it had secured — within 24 hours of signing the original order. For an economy already projected to grow at below 1% this year, the additional tariff burden on UK exporters could prove severe. Manufacturers, car makers and financial services firms — the UK's most exposed sectors — immediately began reassessing their US pricing. (GB News, Fortune, LBC)

Chancellor Rachel Reeves convened an emergency Treasury briefing on Thursday with senior officials from the Confederation of British Industry (CBI) and the Federation of Small Businesses to assess the damage. Her aides briefed journalists that she is prepared to offer the US further concessions on defence procurement and financial regulation in a bid to negotiate the rate back down. But with Trump's trade policy increasingly driven by political whim rather than economic logic, Whitehall officials privately expressed scepticism that any formal deal would stick. The government faces a difficult political problem: having loudly claimed the original 10% deal as a diplomatic success, the 15% hike is a pointed embarrassment. (Oxford Economics, Chatham House)

Easter 2026: Good Friday, closed Britain, and the longer game

Banks, most shops, and government offices are closed today across England, Wales and Northern Ireland as the country enters the Easter long weekend. Britons planning travel abroad face an unexpected headache: new EU Entry/Exit System (EES) rules came into full force this week, requiring biometric registration at borders for UK passport holders entering the Schengen area for the first time. Long queues are expected at Dover, Folkestone and Heathrow's European departure terminals. The Home Office issued last-minute guidance urging travellers to arrive "at least two hours earlier than usual." (GOV.UK, Sunday Guardian Live)

At home, petrol station prices have nudged upward for the third consecutive week as the disruption to Gulf shipping routes — stemming from the Iran-US conflict that has kept the Strait of Hormuz partially closed since February — continues to filter through to forecourt pump prices. AA data shows the average UK petrol price hit 163p per litre on Thursday, a 12-month high. The RAC warned that with Easter weekend being one of the busiest travel periods of the year, the combination of higher fuel costs and motorway congestion means many drivers face an expensive and frustrating holiday journey. (AA, RAC)

Local elections four weeks away — Labour braces for losses

With England's local elections now exactly four weeks away on 7 May — including all 32 London boroughs — internal Labour polling seen by political journalists suggests the party could lose control of several councils it won in 2022. The tariff embarrassment, the doctors' strike, and cost-of-living pressures have combined to drag Labour's approval numbers in key swing areas. Reform UK is expected to make significant gains in towns outside London. Keir Starmer is spending the Easter recess in his Holborn & St Pancras constituency rather than travelling abroad, a signal that the campaign is in a more precarious state than public statements suggest. (Institute for Government, Politics UK)

Terminally Ill Adults Bill: final Lords amendments sent back

The House of Lords sent a final package of amendments to the Terminally Ill Adults (End of Life) Bill back to the Commons on Thursday before Parliament rose for the Easter recess. The Lords' changes focus on strengthening the judicial oversight process and adding an explicit requirement that patients receive independent palliative care advice before a request is approved. MPs will consider the amendments in the week beginning 14 April. Supporters of the bill say it retains sufficient safeguards; the disability rights coalition "Not Dead Yet UK" called the Lords changes "cosmetic" and renewed calls for the bill to be paused altogether. (UK Parliament)

Ofcom presses platforms on child safety — real deadline now set

Ofcom formally confirmed that Facebook, Instagram, Roblox, Snapchat, TikTok and YouTube have until 30 April to submit binding action plans detailing exactly how they will prevent children under 13 from accessing their services. The regulator said Thursday it will begin formal enforcement proceedings — potentially including fines of up to 10% of global annual turnover — against any platform that fails to submit adequate plans. The move is the most concrete exercise of power under the Online Safety Act since it came into force. Tech lobbying groups said the timeline was "extremely tight" but declined to challenge it publicly. (Ofcom, Taylor Wessing)

WORLD

Trump's tariff "Liberation Day" reshapes global trade

Wednesday's announcement of sweeping global tariffs — since compounded to 15% across the board — has sent shockwaves through international markets. The US Supreme Court ruled 6-3 in February that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) does not authorise tariffs. In response, Trump shifted the legal basis to separate legislation and imposed a new baseline 10% tariff on every country before hiking it further to 15%. US courts are now reviewing multiple legal challenges. In the meantime, the practical effects are cascading: the FTSE 100 fell 1.8% on Thursday, European indices were similarly lower, and the IMF revised its 2026 global growth forecast down to 2.4% — the weakest since the 2020 pandemic shock. (Tax Foundation, GB News, Oxford Economics)

Artemis II: humans are going around the Moon again

NASA's Artemis II mission launched successfully on Wednesday, placing four astronauts on a 10-day journey around the Moon — the first crewed lunar flyby in more than 50 years. The crew of three Americans and one Canadian departed Cape Canaveral on the Space Launch System rocket at 12:34 EDT. They will not land but will fly within 8,900km of the lunar surface, testing life support, navigation, and communication systems ahead of the planned Artemis III Moon landing later this decade. The mission was watched by an estimated 8 million people via NASA's live stream. The UK Space Agency contributed navigation software to the mission. (NASA, Tech Startups)

SpaceX files confidentially for what could be history's largest IPO

SpaceX submitted a confidential IPO filing to the US Securities and Exchange Commission on Wednesday, potentially launching what analysts expect could be the first company to go public above a $1 trillion valuation. The filing is preliminary and does not commit SpaceX to a public listing — but it suggests Elon Musk is at least considering the step. SpaceX's Starship programme, Starlink satellite internet service, and the NASA Artemis contract make it arguably the most commercially valuable private company in history. Any flotation would likely take place in the second half of 2026. (Tech Startups)

Rohingya food cuts: aid agencies warn of "unsurvivable" situation

The World Food Programme confirmed this week that food rations for hundreds of thousands of Rohingya refugees in Bangladesh's Cox's Bazar camps — already cut significantly in 2024 — will be reduced further from April, citing funding shortfalls driven by US and UK aid reductions. The combination of the Trump administration's USAID cuts and the UK's decision to trim its overseas development budget by more than half over three years has removed more than $500m from WFP's Bangladesh operation. Aid agencies called the combined effect "unsurvivable" for the most vulnerable families. The UK government said it "remains committed to protecting the world's most vulnerable" but did not reverse the cuts. (The Guardian, NPR)

Iran-US conflict: tanker strikes, Gulf disruption persists

Iranian forces struck two more vessels in the Gulf region on Wednesday, including a Panama-flagged tanker sustaining serious hull damage north of Ras Laffan in Qatar. The Strait of Hormuz remains subject to Iran's conditional transit regime, with roughly a fifth of global oil supply continuing to be disrupted. Brent crude closed above $102 per barrel on Thursday. Britain's 40-nation coalition diplomatic effort — launched last week under Foreign Secretary Yvette Cooper — is pushing for a UN Security Council vote, but US absence from the coalition and Russian and Chinese vetoes complicate any resolution. (Al Jazeera, NPR)

England men — World Cup squad crisis and Tuchel's dilemma

Thomas Tuchel has a problem. England's two final pre-tournament friendlies — a 1–1 draw with Uruguay and a 1–0 defeat to Japan — provided no great confidence ahead of naming the 26-man World Cup squad due in mid-May. Tuchel's expanded 35-man audition group notably excluded Trent Alexander-Arnold (now at Real Madrid), Ollie Watkins, and Luke Shaw, while recalling Harry Maguire and Kobbie Mainoo. Neither result was alarming — but neither was encouraging for a squad that will open its tournament campaign against Croatia on 13 June. (Sky Sports, ESPN UK)

England are in Group L alongside Croatia, Ghana and Panama at the expanded 48-team tournament, which opens on 11 June across the US, Canada and Mexico. Reece James is a doubt with a hamstring injury, which creates an unresolved right-back problem. England have not won a major trophy since 1966; their most recent near-miss was the 2024 Euros final, lost on penalties to Spain. Tuchel insists the squad is "more settled than it looks from the outside." (FIFA, Goal.com, ESPN)

DIGITAL LIFE

NASA goes to the Moon; SpaceX may go public at $1 trillion

The same week saw two extraordinary space milestones. Artemis II's successful Wednesday launch — the first crewed lunar mission in 54 years — marks a genuine inflection point in humanity's return to deep space. Within hours, SpaceX had filed confidentially for a potential IPO that could make it the first company to float above a $1 trillion valuation. The juxtaposition says something about where American ambition now sits: one achievement powered by a public agency, the other by the most commercially successful private space company in history. Elon Musk is simultaneously the company's founder, a senior White House adviser, and Twitter/X's owner — a concentration of influence that UK regulators are watching with increasing unease. (Tech Startups, NASA)

OpenAI surpasses $25bn revenue; Anthropic closes in

OpenAI has surpassed $25 billion in annualized revenue and is reportedly taking early steps toward a public listing, potentially as soon as late 2026. Having raised $122 billion at an $852 billion valuation — figures that would have seemed absurd two years ago — the company is narrowing its strategic focus toward coding tools and enterprise AI products rather than broader experiments. Anthropic, the Claude-maker and OpenAI rival, is itself approaching $19 billion in annualized revenue. The AI economy is no longer a promise: it is generating money at a scale that is reshaping corporate priorities across every sector. UK technology investment strategy is struggling to keep pace. (Tech Startups)

Google warns: quantum computers could crack encryption faster than expected

Google's security research team published a significant warning on Wednesday: quantum computers may be capable of breaking current encryption standards significantly faster than existing projections. The implications for banking, government communications, and digital security infrastructure are serious. Google urged organisations to accelerate migration to "post-quantum cryptographic" standards — a process that typically takes years in complex enterprise environments. The UK's National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) has been advocating quantum-readiness since 2023 but concedes that most SMEs have not yet begun the transition. (Tech Startups)

Meta launches prescription Ray-Ban smart glasses at £499

Meta launched prescription-compatible Ray-Ban smart glasses in the UK on Thursday, priced at £499 with upgraded cameras, on-device AI for translation and live visual search. The glasses allow users to ask Meta AI questions about what they're looking at — menus, street signs, products — and receive spoken answers through discreet open-ear speakers. Nothing, the London-based consumer tech firm, announced competing AI smart glasses for H1 2027, with cameras, microphones and cloud-connected AI. The smart glasses category, long overshadowed by failed predecessors like Google Glass, is beginning to look like a genuine mass market. (Tech Startups)

North Korean hackers in open-source AI supply chain attack

A sophisticated supply-chain attack linked to North Korean state hackers compromised LiteLLM — a widely used open-source library that allows developers to connect applications to multiple AI models — and used it to target AI startup Mercor. The attack involved inserting malicious code into the library that could expose credentials across a range of systems. The incident is a reminder that the AI stack is being built at such speed that security practices have not kept pace. The UK's NCSC said it was "monitoring the situation" and urged developers using LiteLLM to audit their implementations immediately. (Tech Startups)
