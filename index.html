LEAD STORY

Power moved from speeches into systems

Monday's strongest news was about who gets to run the machinery people now depend on. Andy Burnham used a major Manchester speech to promise a “No 10 North”, deeper devolution and public control of essential services, turning a future leadership pitch into a practical argument about where power and growth should sit. In Brazil, Lula and Flávio Bolsonaro returned to a statistical tie in a BTG/Nexus second-round poll while the Brazilian right argued over its own coalition. In technology, WhatsApp prepared usernames, lawmakers moved against the sale of sensitive chatbot data, and Apple dealt with both software fixes and supply-chain leaks. (The Guardian, BBC, Folha Poder, The Verge)

The through-line was not abstract. Devolution would change who allocates money and services. Brazil's polling affects the courts, Congress and campaign strategy. Platform identity changes how ordinary users share phone numbers, health worries and creative work. Even sport carried the same pressure: Brazil escaped Japan in stoppage time, while today's World Cup slate puts France, Mexico and Ivory Coast into knockout-stage tests. The day's lesson was blunt enough: institutions are redesigning access, and people will feel the redesign first as rules, prices, logins, fixtures and bills. (Canal Meio, The Verge, The Guardian)

“Monday was a day of control surfaces: who gets access, who sets the price, and which institutions can still make their promises work in public.”

NEWS

Burnham's “No 10 North” pitch made devolution the governing test

Andy Burnham promised to rebalance power away from Whitehall, create a northern centre of government and put more essential services under public control. The political point was clear: his likely national offer is not only a change of leader but a change in where decisions are made. The harder test is economic. BBC Verify examined whether devolution has enough evidence behind it to raise growth, while the Guardian's editorial argued that the plan only becomes real if Westminster surrenders power rather than branding it differently. (The Guardian, BBC, The Guardian editorial)

The US Supreme Court gave Trump a major power win, then checked him elsewhere

The BBC's Washington coverage described a dramatic court day in which Donald Trump won expanded authority over independent regulators, a decision that could make it easier for presidents to remove and replace officials. But the same set of rulings also produced defeats for him, keeping the day from becoming a clean presidential sweep. The practical result is still a larger argument about executive power: how far the White House can reach into institutions built to resist direct political pressure. (BBC analysis, BBC rulings roundup)

Venezuela's earthquake response became a test of state capacity

Rescue teams in Venezuela kept searching through collapsed buildings with dogs, drones and sound detectors after the La Guaira disaster, with the BBC reporting both the technical effort and the agonising silence of crews listening for survivors. The Guardian's editorial placed that rescue work inside a larger political frame: disasters punish weak infrastructure, slow institutions and diplomatic isolation. The news was immediate and physical, but the underlying issue was whether the state could still protect people fast enough. (BBC rescue report, BBC scene report, The Guardian editorial)

Brazil's presidential race tightened while representation stayed thin

Folha and Canal Meio led with a BTG/Nexus poll showing Lula at 47% and Flávio Bolsonaro at 44% in a simulated second round, a statistical tie that keeps 2026 volatile. Folha also reported that messages in right-wing groups show Michelle Bolsonaro's clash with Flávio wearing down the right before the campaign has fully formed. Beneath the presidential contest, Datafolha found most Brazilians cannot name a sitting federal deputy or senator. That is the day's deeper democratic warning: polarisation at the top is not the same as recognition below. (Folha Poder, Canal Meio, Folha on the right, Datafolha)

Devolution is being sold as a growth policy, not just a constitutional fix

Burnham's speech mattered economically because it tied local control to wages, transport, skills and “good growth in every postcode”. BBC Verify treated the claim as measurable rather than ceremonial, asking what evidence exists that deeper devolution can raise output. That makes the coming fight less about northern symbolism and more about whether the UK is willing to let cities and regions control enough money, planning and infrastructure to change productivity rather than merely describe it. (BBC Verify, The Guardian)

For households, the day's money advice was defensive

The Guardian's personal finance feed pushed readers toward tracker funds, arguing that broad, low-cost exposure can beat the exhausting search for individual winners. MacMagazine supplied the consumer-price version from Brazil, reporting another Disney+ increase that could take the service to R$70 a month. The two items sit together cleanly: households are being told to simplify investing at the same time subscription bills keep finding new room to rise. (The Guardian money, MacMagazine)

TECHNOLOGY

WhatsApp is turning phone-number privacy into a mainstream feature

WhatsApp's username rollout will let people reserve handles and start chats without handing over a phone number. That is a large privacy change because WhatsApp sits inside family life, work groups, commerce and politics rather than a niche social network. The Verge, BBC and MacMagazine all treated the feature as a global product shift: identity on the platform is moving from a telecom credential to a chosen public label. (The Verge, BBC, MacMagazine)

AI's next fight is over sensitive data and synthetic work

US lawmakers prepared a bill to ban the sale of health and location information disclosed to AI chatbots, including data that could otherwise reach brokers. Tidal, meanwhile, said it will label fully AI-generated tracks and refuse to pay royalties on them while stopping short of an outright ban. The policy line is narrowing: platforms still want AI products, but the old habit of treating personal data and creative labour as cheap input is meeting harder rules. (The Verge on data, The Verge on Tidal)

Apple patched current devices while leaks dragged future hardware into view

MacMagazine reported iOS 26.5.2, iPadOS 26.5.2 and macOS 26.5.2 bug-fix releases, the routine work of keeping today's devices stable. At the same time, The Verge and MacMagazine followed reports that iPhone 18 Pro photos and parts information appeared on the dark web after a supplier breach. Apple also faced antitrust tension in India. The company's day was split between maintenance, secrecy and regulatory pressure. (MacMagazine updates, The Verge leak report, MacMagazine leak report)

Legacy tech is being pushed off the books

T-Mobile began notifying customers that some of its oldest plans will be retired and moved to current rate plans. It is a familiar technology-company maneuver: a service promise made under one market eventually becomes an accounting problem under another. The practical news is for affected customers, but the wider pattern is about how platforms close old doors once the growth era has passed. (The Verge)

CULTURE

Britain's festival boom has become a full summer economy

The Guardian traced how Britain moved from unruly festival culture to a dense calendar of boutique, family, food, wellness and music events. The useful news is not simply that there are more festivals; it is that summer leisure has become a segmented market, with a costume and community for almost every taste. The old countercultural residue now sits inside a professionalised system of tickets, travel, brand partnerships and local disruption. (The Guardian culture)

London theatre put star power back on the calendar

James Norton is set to play Hamlet in the West End in 2027 under Thomas Ostermeier, giving London a prestige Shakespeare event well before tickets become the story. The wider culture feed also marked Penelope Keith's comic stage legacy and the sale of Nicholas Hytner's Bridge theatre to Trafalgar Entertainment, a reminder that British theatre is being shaped by both familiar names and changing ownership. (The Guardian on Hamlet)

Music and film stories leaned into craft rather than spectacle

Jacob Collier's account of making In My Room put solitary technical experimentation at the centre of pop production, while the Guardian's Groundhog Day essay treated repeat viewing as emotional maintenance. Together they made a quieter culture argument: the works that last are often built through loops, revisions and rituals rather than one clean moment of novelty. (The Guardian on Jacob Collier, The Guardian film)

SPORTS

Brazil survived Japan and kept Carlo Ancelotti's tournament alive

Brazil reached the last 16 after Gabriel Martinelli struck in stoppage time against Japan, rescuing a performance that had looked close to another early exit. The news is straightforward: Brazil advanced. The warning is also straightforward: the team is still winning by nerve and timing rather than sustained control, which raises the stakes for the next knockout test. (The Guardian match report)

The World Cup's football remains compelling despite the tournament's wider case file

Jonathan Wilson argued that the tournament's on-field quality has been strong enough to pull attention back to the football, without erasing valid criticism of how the event is staged. Canada's win and the broader hosting story underline the same tension: the World Cup can thrill, transform local soccer and still carry political and commercial compromises that should not disappear once the matches improve. (The Guardian analysis, The Guardian on Canada)

LIFESTYLE

A Parkinson's de-diagnosis showed how hard it is to exit a medical identity

Mike Bell spent eight years living with a Parkinson's diagnosis before doctors told him he did not have the disease. The relief was real, but so was the disorientation: benefits, expectations, relationships and self-understanding had all formed around a condition that was suddenly removed. It was the most affecting lifestyle story of the day because it treated health news as a life structure, not just a clinical label. (The Guardian)

Summer travel now requires verification before excitement

Holidaymakers were warned to check social-media accommodation listings before paying, including by reverse-image-searching properties and looking for signs of reuse or fraud. The Picos de Europa travel piece supplied the opposite image of summer: slow walking through a mountain culture with food, animals and local resilience in view. Put together, they make the season's practical rule clear: dream carefully, then verify the booking. (The Guardian money, The Guardian travel)

Beach life came with an environmental bill and a relationship deadline

The Guardian's swimwear guide reminded readers that most bathing suits are plastic products, even when sold through aspirational summer imagery. Its beach-food feature supplied the pleasure side of the season, while the “sunset clause” relationship piece asked whether couples should agree a deadline to commit or move on. Lifestyle was sunny on the surface, but the advice was unusually practical: check the material, check the meal, check the future. (The Guardian swimwear, The Guardian food, The Guardian relationships)
