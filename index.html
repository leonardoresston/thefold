Nvidia boss Jensen Huang faces FBI probe and shareholder lawsuit over suspected Chinese spy

Nvidia and its co-founder and chief executive, Jensen Huang, have been hit with a federal shareholder lawsuit in California alongside confirmation from the White House that the FBI is investigating his romantic relationship with a woman whom American intelligence officials believe to be an agent for China’s Ministry of State Security. Shares in the $4.3tn chipmaker fell by more than 4% on Monday, wiping roughly $190bn to $250bn from its market value in a single session. Five minutes after the closing bell, retail investors led by Mark Kistenmacher filed a complaint in San Jose federal court accusing Huang and the company of defrauding shareholders by concealing a personal entanglement that posed acute national security and commercial risks. (Guardian, BBC)

At the centre of the investigation is Alice Huang, also known as Huang Le and unrelated to the Nvidia chief, a former television reporter who became managing director at Megaspeed, a Singapore-based computing firm that rents access to graphics processors. Around the time the pair were photographed together at a Taipei bar in 2024, Megaspeed acquired $4.8bn worth of scarce Nvidia hardware in a sudden expansion that startled rivals. Over the weekend, Jensen Huang told employees in a memo that he had been unfaithful to his wife, while Nvidia’s board announced an independent inquiry into reports that he had been targeted by foreign intelligence. Nvidia maintains that neither corporate secrets nor American national security were compromised, and Beijing dismissed the allegations as groundless smears. (The Verge, BBC)

Speaking at a White House briefing on Monday after telephoning the executive, Donald Trump confirmed the FBI inquiry while defending Huang’s character and expressing sympathy for his family. Trump said he had asked Huang directly whether he knew the woman was a spy, accepted his denial, and expected him to emerge in good shape. Even so, the affair has raised uncomfortable questions for Washington: intelligence agencies reportedly briefed Trump roughly a year ago on the relationship, and Secretary of State Marco Rubio helped draft a warning memo to senior officials ahead of the president’s trip to China in May. Because Nvidia supplies the frontier chips powering modern artificial intelligence and holds $99bn in equity across the sector, Huang has spent the past year pressing Trump to lift export bans on advanced H200 processors to China, arguing that AI advancement between the superpowers need not be a zero-sum contest. (The Verge, BBC, Guardian)

US evacuates tankers from RAF Mildenhall over Iran threat as war reshapes global emissions

The United States military evacuated aerial refuelling tankers from RAF Mildenhall in Suffolk on Monday and raised defensive alert levels across its East Anglian bases following intelligence of an Iran-linked threat. Two tankers inbound to Mildenhall diverted to Germany early on Monday before several more aircraft stationed at the base were flown out to Germany and other European airfields later in the day. American commanders simultaneously upgraded the security posture at RAF Mildenhall and nearby RAF Lakenheath to Force Protection Condition Charlie, the Pentagon’s second-highest threat level, indicating intelligence of a likely terrorist or sabotage action. The move comes weeks after Washington withdrew its B-1 bombers from RAF Fairford in Gloucestershire after suspicious vans were seen near the perimeter in September, an incident that led counter-terrorism police to arrest eight men and that Prime Minister Andy Burnham said last month had probably been coordinated from Tehran. (Guardian, BBC)

In Washington, however, Donald Trump struck a de-escalatory tone after Republicans suffered heavy midterm losses last week that senior figures, including Vice-President JD Vance, blamed in part on voter frustration over the nine-month Iran conflict. After meeting Qatari Prime Minister Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdul Rahman Al Thani at the White House to discuss mediation, Trump declared Iran militarily finished and said fresh American strikes were highly unlikely, though US forces would maintain their naval blockade on Iranian ports. Brent crude slipped past $91.50 a barrel on Tuesday following his remarks. Meanwhile, scientists presenting the annual Global Carbon Budget at Cop31 in Antalya, Turkey, reported that the war-driven oil and gas price surge has nudged global fossil fuel emissions down by 0.4% in 2026, with oil emissions falling by 2% and Chinese carbon output dipping by nearly 1% as drivers switch to electric vehicles and solar and wind capacity expands. Researchers cautioned that deforestation and south-east Asian wildfires still pushed total carbon dioxide emissions up from 42.8bn to 43.3bn tonnes, leaving less than four years of the world’s 1.5C carbon budget at current rates. (BBC, Guardian)

Abu Dhabi hosts first trilateral Ukraine peace talks since February amid European security nerves

Senior delegations from Washington, Kyiv and Moscow convened in Abu Dhabi on Tuesday for their first substantive trilateral negotiations since February, with US officials pressing for a settlement as the war in Ukraine nears its fifth anniversary. Led on the American side by Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner, the talks link territorial questions in eastern Ukraine—particularly control over Donetsk, where Moscow demands full Ukrainian withdrawal while Kyiv seeks a frozen contact line—to a proposal for a new post-war security architecture between Russia and Europe. US officials said both warring sides had privately shown slight flexibility on territory, and floated offering Ukraine a security guarantee modelled on Nato’s mutual-defence clause while reviving the Nato-Russia Council and lifting sanctions on Moscow. (Guardian, BBC)

European capitals greeted the blueprint with apprehension, fearing that a White House eager for a diplomatic prize after its midterm setback could entertain Vladimir Putin’s demand that Nato roll back its military footprint in eastern Europe. Envoys from Britain, France, Germany, Italy and the EU travelled to the United Arab Emirates to secure a seat at the table, while EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas noted that Europe already has a security architecture whose treaties Moscow has chosen to break. Elsewhere on Tuesday, Hungarian Prime Minister Péter Magyar announced that anti-graft investigators under Operation Purgatory had detained Áron Orbán, the 49-year-old brother of former prime minister Viktor Orbán, alongside three others over an alleged visa-smuggling scheme at Hungary’s missions in Vietnam; in Jerusalem, more than 160 former Israeli security officials warned of far-right plots to disrupt the swearing-in of the new Knesset as opposition leader Gadi Eisenkot moves to replace Benjamin Netanyahu’s ally Amir Ohana as speaker. (Guardian, Guardian, Guardian)

England bans school phones from bags as Jemima Goldsmith speaks out after Russell Brand sentencing

Pupils across England will be barred from carrying mobile phones in their pockets or schoolbags from September 2027 under tightened statutory guidance announced by Education Secretary Lucy Powell. While rules introduced in June required schools to be phone-free throughout the day, many relied on policies allowing students to keep devices switched off in their bags; the updated framework instructs schools to use lockable pouches, secure lockers, morning collection points or site-wide bans. Headteachers’ unions welcomed the clarity on distraction but warned that providing magnetic pouches or secure storage for every pupil will require dedicated government funding as school leaders weigh early departure over reformed Ofsted inspections. (BBC, Guardian)

In the courts, the sentencing of former comedian Russell Brand to ten years and six months in prison for raping a fan and sexually assaulting three women between 2004 and 2009 prompted television producer Jemima Goldsmith to reveal that she had called police to remove him from her house during a violent incident in 2014. Goldsmith, who previously stayed silent to protect her teenage children, disclosed that she had assisted the journalists who exposed Brand in 2023 and promised to testify if the newspapers were sued. Meanwhile, British travellers face potential two-year Schengen bans and €10,000 fines because glitches in the European Union’s biometric Entry/Exit System have logged arrivals without recording departures; novelist Jojo Moyes was detained by border officers at Marseille and told her 90-day post-Brexit allowance had expired after the database failed to record her exit from Portugal in May, prompting Brussels to sue IBM and two European contractors. (Guardian, BBC, Guardian)

Brazil’s Supreme Court ends seven-year fake news inquiry as Federal Police rewrite Master reports

Brazil’s Supreme Federal Court has closed its fake news inquiry after 2,797 days, ending one of the most consequential proceedings in the country’s recent legal history. Acting on a request from Prosecutor-General Paulo Gonet, Chief Justice Edson Fachin signed a 31-page order on Monday archiving the probe, which was opened without a prosecutorial petition by then-chief justice Dias Toffoli in March 2019 and assigned without a draw to Justice Alexandre de Moraes. Across more than seven years and 12 deadline extensions, the inquiry served as the court’s central shield against digital militias and coup plots during Jair Bolsonaro’s presidency, generating roughly 1,600 criminal cases—including prosecutions from the 8 January 2023 uprising—while drawing criticism from jurists and the Bar Association for concentrating investigative and judicial powers in one magistrate. Fachin, who removed Moraes as rapporteur two months ago after Moraes sought to use the dossier to investigate fellow Justice André Mendonça, ruled that a preliminary inquiry cannot remain open indefinitely, though all past decisions and spin-off actions remain valid. (Folha, Folha)

The closure comes amid friction over ties between Supreme Court justices and Daniel Vorcaro, former controller of Banco Master. Federal Police leadership under director-general Andrei Rodrigues ordered officers on Monday to rewrite three reports requested by Fachin on interactions between Vorcaro and Justices Moraes, Toffoli and Kassio Nunes Marques, while removing commissioners Daniel Mostardeiro Cola and Wedson Cajé Lopes from their leadership posts in the anti-corruption and Supreme Court inquiries unit. Phone records showed Vorcaro flew to Brasília twice in November last year right before his arrest to meet Moraes and messaged him seeking delays to police operations before searching online for the Tremembé prison; police chiefs concluded the initial drafts contained analytical cross-referencing that could be viewed as an unauthorised probe of sitting justices, and ordered purely factual individual files by month’s end. Meanwhile, President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva set three year-end priorities for Congress—a third phase of the Desenrola debt-forgiveness programme for micro-entrepreneurs, a provisional measure banning online betting, and big-tech regulation—while planning a cabinet reshuffle to return elected senators such as Rui Costa and Wellington Dias to the upper house to counter a 32-seat bolsonarista opposition bloc in 2027. (Folha, Meio, Folha, Meio)

Economy

Bank of England warns of ‘low-gear economy’ hitting young workers as December rate call stays live

Young people entering the British labour market are bearing the brunt of a recruitment freeze triggered by the nine-month war in Iran and higher business costs, Bank of England deputy governor Clare Lombardelli has warned. Speaking ahead of a London speech on Tuesday, the deputy governor for monetary policy described a low-gear economy defined by a low-hire, low-fire dynamic: employers are avoiding redundancies and retaining staff, but have paused recruitment while waiting to see whether energy prices and geopolitical uncertainty settle. That caution has pushed UK unemployment to 4.7% this year and lifted youth joblessness to its highest level outside the Covid pandemic since 2015, swelling the number of young people outside education, employment or training to nearly one million ahead of Alan Milburn’s review next week. (Guardian)

Lombardelli, who voted with the Monetary Policy Committee majority last week to lift the base rate from 3.75% to 4%—the first rate rise in three years—said December’s policy meeting remains live, with markets pricing even odds of another quarter-point increase. Across the Channel, Jens Eskelund, head of the European Union Chamber of Commerce in China, warned officials in Brussels that with the bloc’s trade deficit with Beijing running at €1.18bn (£1bn) a day and Chinese exports forecast by Goldman Sachs to cut German GDP by up to 0.9% by 2030, European car, chemical and clean-tech firms may soon stop trying to compete head-on and instead join Chinese supply chains. In Britain, troubled utility Thames Water asked its creditors—including Elliott Management, Silver Point Capital, BlackRock and M&G—to extend the repayment deadline on part of its £20bn debt pile from 2027 to March 2028 as it raises the first £600m of a £2.4bn emergency loan to avoid running out of cash around New Year, leaving Prime Minister Andy Burnham under pressure to choose between a creditor restructuring and special administration. (Guardian, Guardian, Guardian)

Anthropic debuts Claude 5.2 models as Apple rolls out OLED touchscreen MacBook Pro and paid AI packs

Anthropic has launched two upgraded frontier artificial intelligence models, Claude Fable 5.2 and Claude Mythos 5.2, built to handle complex software migrations, multi-agent orchestration and scientific reasoning faster than September’s 5.1 releases. According to the company, prerelease versions designed three working electronic devices from scratch, solved two open problems in black-hole physics, advanced robotics evaluations and formalised the proof of Fermat’s Last Theorem in 30 hours; Fable 5.2 is out immediately, while Mythos 5.2 is restricted to vetted institutions. The launch coincides with Apple’s first move to charge for server-based AI tools: a Monday update to Logic Pro introduces a $49.99 one-time Intelligence Usage Pack for users who exhaust their free allowance, a model set to expand across Final Cut Pro, Pixelmator Pro and iWork this month, even as Apple completed regulatory filings to launch Apple Intelligence on iPads and Macs in China in December and released iOS 27.2.1 and macOS 27.2.1 Golden Gate to fix eMudhra certificate vulnerabilities. (The Verge, 9to5Mac, 9to5Mac, 9to5Mac)

Reviews also arrived on Tuesday for Apple’s first laptop redesign in five years, the MacBook Pro with PROLED XDR display, priced from £2,899—a £400 premium over equivalent LCD models amid high memory costs. Sold in 14-inch (1.38kg) and 16-inch (1.82kg) M5 Pro and M5 Max versions, the laptop cuts weight by roughly 15%, restores the glowing Apple lid logo dropped in 2015, and adds the first touchscreen on a Mac, complete with a Dynamic Island around the webcam and a tandem OLED panel reaching 2,000 nits. While reviewers praised the display and touch gestures for iPhone mirroring and photo editing, Apple’s foldable iPhone Duo faces scrutiny after reports blamed hairline cracks along the inner crease of some units on defective display material. Even so, Apple shares closed above $350 on Monday for a record high, days after SEC 10-K disclosures showed that booking overseas profits in Singapore ($2.7bn) and Ireland ($2.6bn) saved Apple $5.3bn compared with the 21% US corporate tax rate in fiscal 2026, despite the OECD’s 15% minimum tax adding $2.5bn to its Singapore bill. (Guardian, MacMagazine, MacMagazine, 9to5Mac)

Palantir lobbied health minister via WhatsApp to bring NHS GP records into its AI platform

Freedom of Information disclosures show that US data firm Palantir privately lobbied former health minister Zubir Ahmed via WhatsApp between July 2025 and March 2026 to extend its artificial intelligence software into NHS family doctor records, despite explicit pledges when its £330m Federated Data Platform contract was signed in 2023 that GP data would stay out of scope. In messages obtained by campaign group Foxglove, Palantir’s UK chief, Louis Mosley, argued that linking primary and secondary care data inside its Foundry software was vital for managing frail patients and urged the minister to get ahead of political opposition from the British Medical Association, the Green Party and Jeremy Corbyn’s Your Party. Although Health Secretary Yvette Cooper told MPs last week that the government sees long-term value in building British sovereign AI when Palantir’s contract reaches a break point in 2029, ministers are expected to extend the deal in the meantime, while the BMA warned that scope creep into GP records risked damaging patient trust. (Guardian)

Take-Two fortifies GTA 6 against AI reverse-engineering as Switch 2 Zelda remake trips up veterans

With ten days left before Rockstar Games launches Grand Theft Auto VI on PlayStation 5 and Xbox on 19 November, parent company Take-Two Interactive has stepped up technical encryption and legal preparations against the threat of artificial intelligence tools being used to decompile console code into unofficial PC ports. After viral episodes last month in which modders made Grand Theft Auto V playable in a browser and hackers released jailbreaks for older PS5 firmware, Anthropic updated Claude to block prompts attempting to reverse-engineer video games; Take-Two chief executive Strauss Zelnick welcomed that move on Monday while warning AI firms to respect third-party copyright. Reaffirming an $8bn to $8.2bn revenue forecast for fiscal 2027, Zelnick noted that anticipation for GTA 6 had prompted rival publishers to crowd into September and caused gamers to hold off on buying other releases, including Take-Two’s NBA 2K27, which sold four million units but missed internal forecasts. Meanwhile, Nintendo’s Switch 2 remake of The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time has sparked debate by replacing the 1998 Nintendo 64 original’s automatic ledge-jumping with a manual jump button, sending decades of player muscle memory—and Young Link—repeatedly into rivers and boulders. (IGN, IGN, IGN)

M John Harrison wins Booker Prize at 81 with post-apocalyptic novel The End of Everything

M John Harrison has won the 2026 Booker Prize for his slim, unsettling novel The End of Everything, becoming at 81 the oldest laureate in the award’s history—surpassing Margaret Atwood, who was 79 when she shared the prize in 2019—and the first winner to emerge directly from science fiction. Awarded the £50,000 prize in London on Monday night after a unanimous vote by judges chaired by classicist Mary Beard alongside Jarvis Cocker, Patricia Lockwood, Raymond Antrobus and Rebecca Liu, Harrison prevailed over a shortlist featuring Marlon James, Douglas Stuart, Elizabeth Strout, Luke Kennard and Rebecca Perry. At 198 pages and published by independent imprint Serpent’s Tail, the novel follows Phillip Tennent and his ageing aunt Marnie as they muddle through on a near-future Kent coast after an unexplained collapse and the arrival of shape-shifting beings called the iGhetti. (Guardian, BBC)

Born in Warwickshire in 1945, Harrison sold his first story in 1965 and helped shape British speculative fiction at New Worlds magazine in the 1970s, winning the Boardman Tasker prize for Climbers, the Arthur C Clarke Award for Nova Swing and the Goldsmiths Prize for The Sunken Land Begins to Rise Again before earning his first Booker nomination this year. He has described The End of Everything as a disaster story in which the catastrophe is humanity’s failure to comprehend a shifting reality, withholding explanation so readers share the characters’ disorientation amid the collapse of rationality in the internet and AI age. Beard praised the book for turning readers upside down while leaving an unexpected trace of optimism, while Booker chief executive Gaby Wood hailed the recognition of a writer who has spent six decades showing that speculative fiction belongs at the centre of literary culture. (Guardian, BBC)

Manchester United edge towards sacking Michael Carrick as City slump and Arsenal’s Ben White returns

Manchester United are moving closer to dismissing head coach Michael Carrick during the November international break after Sunday’s 4-4 draw with Aston Villa left the club 18th in the Premier League and enduring their worst domestic start in 40 years. Having spared Carrick after a 3-0 home defeat by Bournemouth on 25 October on the condition that performances improved across three matches against Chelsea, Roma and Villa, director of football Jason Wilcox and chief executive Omar Berrada are expected to recommend a change to Sir Jim Ratcliffe and the Glazer family after seeing United win three of 15 games in all competitions and repeatedly concede early goals. United have sounded out Eddie Howe and Julian Nagelsmann, while USA coach Mauricio Pochettino has a contract clause allowing a move to Old Trafford after next week’s Nations League play-off against Haiti. Across town, Manchester City manager Enzo Maresca is using the break to reassess his high defensive line and lightweight midfield pairing of £125m signing Enzo Fernández and Rayan Cherki after Saturday’s loss at Nottingham Forest condemned City to three straight league defeats following seven opening wins—a sequence last seen in the top flight in 1904—just as the club awaits potential sanctions after being found guilty last month of nearly all of the Premier League’s financial charges. (Guardian, BBC, Guardian)

In the England camp ahead of this week’s Nations League matches against Croatia and Spain, Arsenal defender Ben White said he held no complaints over the Wembley fans who booed him when he ended his nearly four-year self-imposed international exile against Uruguay earlier this year. White, who left the 2022 World Cup in Qatar for personal reasons and stayed unavailable for the rest of Gareth Southgate’s tenure, was persuaded back by Thomas Tuchel before a knee injury kept him out of last summer’s World Cup. Having made 12 appearances in strong form for Arsenal this term, the 29-year-old said he would never publicly blame anyone over his 2022 exit and was focused on earning a place in Tuchel’s squad for Euro 2028. (Guardian)
