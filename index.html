Lula’s fourth term begins with a fight over Brazil’s direction

Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva used his first celebrations after winning a fourth presidential term to promise that Brazil would remain a democracy. Tens of thousands of supporters gathered in central São Paulo early on Monday after a second-round comeback that overturned his defeat in the first round. Lula secured 64.2 million votes to Flávio Bolsonaro’s 59.8 million, a margin of 51.79% to 48.21%. No candidate who lost the first round had previously won the runoff since Brazil returned to democracy after its 1964–85 military dictatorship. (Guardian)

Recognition of the result widened during Monday, even as Bolsonaro continued to reject it. His Liberal party acknowledged the outcome almost a day after the vote, while seeking clarification of issues it had raised. Parties that backed Bolsonaro, including Republicanos and MDB, also congratulated Lula. That leaves an important distinction between the defeated candidate’s objections and the response of organisations whose representatives will take their seats and negotiate with the returning president. The presidents of Brazil’s electoral court, Chamber of Deputies and Senate were among those recognising the victory. (Guardian, Folha)

Donald Trump questioned the election’s credibility without providing evidence of wrongdoing in his remarks. Other governments moved quickly to recognise Lula, including rightwing leaders in Chile, Peru and Colombia as well as Mexico, Spain, the UK, China and the European Commission. The spread of those acknowledgements matters for Brazil’s diplomacy: governments with sharply different politics are preparing to deal with the elected president. During the campaign, the US had faced accusations of using economic and diplomatic pressure to influence the outcome. Lula’s win now requires both countries to manage a relationship that extends well beyond their leaders’ preferences. (Guardian)

At home, the next stage is assembling support for another administration in a country still closely divided. Lula gained more than 10 million votes between the two rounds, helped by mobilisation on the streets and online and lower abstention. Environmental policy is one immediate area of consequence. His former environment minister Marina Silva welcomed the result after warning that another Bolsonaro presidency would threaten Indigenous territories and the Amazon. The election has settled who will govern; decisions over ministers, congressional alliances and public spending will determine how much of the campaign programme becomes policy. Lula’s age also keeps succession firmly on the agenda: his fourth term would end when he is 85. (Guardian, Meio, Folha)

Serbia’s students prepare to turn a majority into a government

Serbia’s Student List began preparing for government talks after an election victory ended Aleksandar Vučić’s 14-year domination of the country’s politics. Projections based on all ballot samples put the movement on 53% of the vote and 141 of parliament’s 250 seats. Vučić’s Serbian Progressive party was projected to take 103 seats on 39%, with the remainder going to national minority lists. Vučić conceded on Sunday. By Monday, supporters were celebrating a result that had grown out of two years of protests following the Novi Sad railway station canopy collapse, which killed 16 people. (Guardian)

The movement now has to choose a prime minister and cabinet through a collective structure deliberately built without a single leader. Its candidates include academics, actors and farmers alongside students. Prominent figures include cardiologist Ilija Srdanović, former central bank governor Dejan Šoškić and chemistry professor Radomir Saičić. The list’s approach helped unite people around corruption and failing institutions. Running a government will require quicker decisions, named officeholders and a clear division of responsibility. Its parliamentary majority gives it room to act, while its lack of governing experience makes the transition a substantial undertaking. (Guardian)

Proposals include an independent police unit accountable to prosecutors, removing officers linked to organised crime, preventing party appointments to state companies and opening major infrastructure contracts to independent scrutiny. Implementing those changes means working through institutions staffed under the outgoing administration. Foreign policy questions, including Serbia’s relationships with Russia and the EU, were kept in the background during the campaign. Moscow expressed hope that cooperation would continue; Germany offered support for Serbia’s European path. The constitutional timetable now requires parliament to convene within 30 days of the official result, followed by a 90-day deadline to form a government. A separate presidential election is due before 27 December because Vučić stepped down to seek the premiership. (Guardian)

UK sanctions commanders over atrocities in Sudan

Britain imposed travel bans and asset freezes on 15 commanders from Sudan’s warring forces on the first anniversary of the fall of El Fasher. The measures target figures from both the Rapid Support Forces and the Sudanese Armed Forces suspected of involvement in sexual violence during the siege. Those named include RSF colonel Hussein Abu Jalak and Al-Nour Ahmed Adam, also known as Al-Qubba. The sanctions seek to restrict the commanders’ access to money and travel while adding to efforts to establish responsibility for abuses. (Guardian)

El Fasher’s capture followed an 18-month starvation siege. As many as 10,000 people are believed to have been killed over two days, and another 40,000 remain unaccounted for. Fewer than 70,000 people are thought to remain in a city whose prewar population exceeded a million. Survivors’ accounts describe detention, torture and unlawful killings. Across Sudan, the conflict has forced 14 million people from their homes and increasingly involves drone attacks on infrastructure. The latest RSF strikes on power stations caused widespread outages in the east. The UAE continues to deny allegations that it supports the RSF. The siege forced civilians to survive on animal feed, and the scale of the missing population continues to complicate the accounting of deaths. Sanctions are one instrument available to Britain, while humanitarian access and the protection of people still inside the city remain urgent practical problems. (Guardian)

Downing Street clarifies the limits of Burnham’s EU opening

Downing Street clarified that Andy Burnham was proposing a public discussion about Britain’s long-term relationship with the EU, without committing the government to restoring freedom of movement. The prime minister’s weekend comments had prompted questions about which Brexit restrictions he might be willing to revisit. On Monday he ruled out rejoining the EU during this parliament and said the government would respect Labour’s manifesto commitments. He also said Britain would not join the freedom of movement scheme without a referendum. (Guardian)

Burnham nevertheless left open the possibility of a future election fought on a pledge to rejoin, saying he would personally like to see Britain return to the EU in his lifetime. That separates his longer-term preference from the agreements ministers are currently seeking. He is due in Brussels for a UK–EU summit on 20 November, where the focus is on practical changes to the existing relationship. Downing Street has yet to establish how the public would decide on any more ambitious proposals. For businesses and people affected by cross-border rules, the immediate question is what the summit produces; a broader change would require a further political mandate and negotiations with the bloc. (Guardian)

Economy

Carers’ allowance is set to lose its earnings cliff edge

Unpaid carers are to be offered a tapered earnings system under plans for Wednesday’s budget, ending a rule that can wipe out a whole week’s benefit when wages exceed the limit by a penny. Chancellor John Healey plans to replace the cliff edge with a £1 reduction in allowance for each additional £1 earned above the threshold. The change addresses a longstanding trap for people fitting paid work around intensive caring responsibilities. The proposed £1-for-£1 withdrawal would allow additional wages to replace benefit gradually over the taper range. Carers would still need clear guidance on which earnings count and when the new rules take effect, particularly while the existing system remains in operation. Implementation will take time: the digital system needed to operate the taper is not expected before the end of the decade. (Guardian)

The current weekly earnings limit is £204 and the allowance is £86.45. Someone exceeding the limit by a penny every week for a year can therefore face a demand to repay £4,495.40, despite having earned only 52p above the permitted amount. Small pay rises, overtime and changes to the minimum wage have pushed carers over the threshold, sometimes without them realising. The Department for Work and Pensions had information about breaches but placed responsibility on claimants to report changes. Debts could accumulate for months or years before a repayment demand arrived. (Guardian)

An independent review led by disability rights expert Liz Sayce found that around 180,000 carers had accumulated £300 million in overpayment debts between 2019 and 2025. One case involved a minimum-wage increase that lifted earnings 15p a week above the limit without any change in hours. The carer eventually faced a £2,000 repayment demand after earning just £5 above the threshold. Campaigners welcomed the planned reform while expressing disappointment at the wait. For households managing at least 35 hours of caring each week, the eventual taper should remove the risk of a small extra shift leaving them substantially poorer. (Guardian)

Other measures outlined ahead of the budget include additional parental support for 150,000 people, including self-employed fathers and partners who previously missed out. Payments could be worth up to £194 a week. Healey also plans to make higher tax-free mileage rates permanent, at 55p a mile for the first 10,000 business miles and 35p thereafter. The Treasury estimates that around three million employees and self-employed people use their own vehicles for work. The budget’s wider limits remain significant: pressure on the public finances, including the effects of the Iran conflict, has reduced the chancellor’s room for larger spending commitments. (Guardian)

Visa and Mastercard face a proposed £5 billion fees claim

An application at the Competition Appeal Tribunal seeks permission for a group claim against Visa and Mastercard over the fees UK organisations pay when customers use cards. Law firms Harcus Parker and Hausfeld allege that the companies restricted competition and allowed excessive multilateral interchange fees. These are set by the card networks and passed on to businesses through their banks. The proposed case covers charges since October 2020, plus interest, and could be worth about £5 billion. That figure is an estimate of the claim’s potential value, with liability still to be determined. (Guardian)

If approved, the action would automatically include an estimated one million UK businesses, organisations and charities with revenue below £100 million unless they opt out. Larger businesses would have to join actively. Individual recoveries would depend on the organisation and its payment history, and a conclusion could take years. Bench Walk Advisors is funding the litigation, which is led by businessman Stephen Allen. Mastercard disputes the basis of the case and describes interchange as important to secure, efficient payments. Visa likewise defends the fees as a central part of the digital payments system. The tribunal must first decide whether the proposed collective action can proceed. (Guardian)

Electric-car incentives could reward batteries that support the grid

Ministers are considering credits for carmakers that install vehicle-to-grid technology as part of changes to the zero-emission vehicle mandate. Bidirectional charging allows a car to draw power when it is plentiful and return some to the grid when demand is high. A compatible vehicle can also supply a home. Used widely, parked cars could help balance a system with more wind and solar generation, reducing the need to start expensive gas-fired plants during periods of low renewable output. Similar services already operate on a limited scale in Britain and elsewhere in Europe. (Guardian)

The proposed incentive raises a separate question about the number of petrol cars manufacturers would be allowed to sell. Extra credits for fitting bidirectional equipment could help a company meet its obligations while selling fewer fully electric vehicles. New AutoMotive argues that rewards should depend on the technology actually being used, especially while compatible home chargers remain expensive and uncommon. Driver representatives also question changing sales requirements before the service has been tested at scale. The National Energy System Operator estimates that participating cars could provide 1.2 gigawatts of power on demand by 2030, potentially rising to 41 gigawatts by 2050. Those forecasts depend on adoption: installing the capability, connecting cars and persuading drivers to participate are all necessary steps. (Guardian)

Anthropic’s public filing opens its finances to scrutiny

Anthropic has publicly filed for a US stock-market listing, giving investors a detailed look at the costs and revenue behind Claude. The company plans to trade under ANT after submitting a confidential filing in June. Estimates from analysts and investors have placed its value above $1.5 trillion, making the offering a major test of confidence in the AI industry. A final valuation and successful listing remain ahead. The prospectus makes it possible to examine the business against audited figures alongside the ambitious claims surrounding the technology. (Guardian)

Revenue rose from $41 million in 2023 to $386 million in 2024 and $4.585 billion in 2025, then reached $16.286 billion in the first half of 2026. The same six-month period produced a disclosed net loss of $80 billion. Anthropic had raised $141.1 billion by the end of June and disclosed $6.2 billion in debt. Its focus on business customers has brought rapid growth, but the top five customers account for a quarter of revenue. Losing a large account could therefore have a material effect even while the total customer base expands. Spending on computing capacity is another major demand on its finances. (Guardian)

More than 90 pages of risk disclosures cover commercial, political and safety concerns. The company acknowledges that safeguards against misuse can fail or be circumvented, including where systems could assist cyberattacks or dangerous weapons development. It also faces a dispute with the Trump administration after being designated a supply-chain risk, preventing military agencies from contracting with it; Anthropic is challenging the designation. Those issues now sit alongside conventional investor questions about customer concentration, debt and losses. The offering will test how buyers price a fast-growing company whose infrastructure commitments and exposure to government decisions remain substantial. (Guardian)

Microsoft offers buyouts as divisions reshape their workforces

Microsoft is offering voluntary buyouts to about 60,000 employees, with divisions including Xbox, Windows and Copilot participating. Employees in some areas, including cybersecurity, data centres and marketing, are excluded. The company describes the offer as a supported route for eligible staff considering leaving and a way for participating organisations to adjust to changing needs. The number eligible is not a count of confirmed departures: take-up will determine the eventual reduction. (Guardian)

The programme follows an April offer of voluntary retirement to around 7% of Microsoft’s US workforce. Its global workforce totals about 223,000. Other large technology employers have also reduced staffing while increasing AI investment, although evidence that the changes are delivering productivity gains remains mixed. A recent National Bureau of Economic Research working paper covering 9,000 senior executives found that 90% had yet to see greater productivity from AI at their companies. Microsoft’s next decisions will show how far it intends to shift resources among established products, AI development and the infrastructure needed to run those services. (Guardian)

Apple updates software and acknowledges folding-screen cracks

Apple released iOS and iPadOS 27.2, adding support for the USB-C Apple Pencil on the iPhone Duo alongside further Siri improvements. It also announced a revised iPad mini with an 8.5-inch OLED display and a landscape Center Stage camera. The launches expand the uses of Apple’s smaller portable devices, with handwriting support reaching its folding phone and the mini receiving a larger screen. (MacStories, MacStories)

Separately, Apple acknowledged an inner-display problem affecting a small number of iPhone Duo units after users described cracks along the hinge. Affected owners can contact AppleCare for a replacement. The company’s statement does not establish how widespread the fault is or identify its cause, so the reports should be treated as an emerging hardware issue. The response gives owners a route to support while leaving questions about the pattern and frequency of failures unresolved. (9to5Mac)

Carrick waits as United consider their next move

Manchester United’s football leadership discussed Michael Carrick’s position on Monday after a 3–0 home defeat by Bournemouth left the club 16th in the Premier League, one point above the relegation zone. United have gone eight games without a win and have only two victories all season. Carrick attended Carrington to watch an under-21 match while director of football Jason Wilcox and chief executive Omar Berrada considered his future. No dismissal had been confirmed in the Monday account of those discussions. (Guardian)

Eddie Howe is Wilcox’s preferred replacement if United make a change, with contact understood to have taken place. Unai Emery and Julian Nagelsmann are also admired, although recruiting Emery from Aston Villa would involve substantial compensation. The uncertainty follows a 4–1 Champions League defeat at Como and Carrick’s decision to leave Bruno Fernandes on the bench against Bournemouth. Elsewhere, Arsenal’s 2–0 win over Everton brought Mikel Arteta a second booking of the season, leaving him one further caution from a touchline suspension. Aston Villa beat Manchester City 2–1, with goalkeeper Zion Suzuki helping protect the result. These developments leave United facing an immediate management decision while rivals assess a weekend that shifted pressure across several benches. (Guardian)
