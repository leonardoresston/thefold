Jackdaw gets the go-ahead, with no promise of lower winter bills

The government has approved production from the Jackdaw gasfield in the North Sea, opening the way for supplies to reach homes and businesses before the end of October. Energy secretary Miatta Fahnbulleh acknowledged that the decision would not lower bills this winter. Ministers are presenting the project as a way to sustain domestic supply and employment while Britain moves towards renewable energy and nuclear power. Environmental groups argue that the additional emissions undermine that transition. The announcement leaves a much larger decision, on the Rosebank oilfield, unresolved. (BBC)

Jackdaw lies about 150 miles east of Aberdeen and is operated by Adura, the Shell and Equinor joint venture. Its gas will travel through the existing Shearwater platform to the St Fergus terminal in Aberdeenshire. The operator expects it to account for roughly 6% of UK gas production, equivalent to about 2% of total demand once imports are included. Ministers put its economic contribution at £2.9bn over an expected 11-year life and say it will support hundreds of jobs. The distinction between domestic production and total consumption helps explain why the project can matter to the offshore industry without producing a noticeable reduction in household costs. (BBC)

The consent follows a legal reversal. In January 2025, Scotland’s Court of Session ruled that earlier approvals for Jackdaw and Rosebank were unlawful because their environmental assessments had failed to account adequately for emissions from burning the fuels. Preparatory work was allowed to continue while new assessments were drawn up. Labour’s ban on new exploration licences remains in place: these projects involve licences already granted. Alongside Wednesday’s approval, the government announced an energy wealth fund with an initial £2bn to invest in renewables, nuclear and other low-carbon projects. The budget is due to clarify which oil and gas tax revenues will support it. (BBC, Guardian)

Rosebank could produce a sharper argument within Labour. Fahnbulleh is understood to be leaning towards refusing approval, although no final decision has been taken and prime minister Andy Burnham could intervene. The department describes accounts of her position as speculation and says each project will be assessed individually. Rosebank contains an estimated 300m–500m barrels of oil, much of which would be exported, and its projected emissions are substantially greater than Jackdaw’s. Several Labour MPs have signalled opposition. A decision is expected before the end of the year, giving ministers another test of how they apply Britain’s carbon budgets to projects on existing licences. Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch welcomed Jackdaw’s approval but urged Burnham to lift the ban on new licences. Scottish first minister John Swinney welcomed greater certainty for the north-east and called for the wealth fund to prioritise offshore renewables there. Unions and Aberdeen business groups also welcomed the production decision, while Greenpeace and Oxfam opposed it. (Guardian)

Kyiv comes under attack as a new round of talks is floated

Russian missiles hit Kyiv on Wednesday night, injuring six people and striking several houses, according to mayor Vitaliy Klitschko. The attack followed a meeting between Vladimir Putin and US envoy Steve Witkoff in St Petersburg. Russia indicated that it was ready in principle to resume negotiations, with Putin’s aide Yuri Ushakov suggesting talks around 10 November in Abu Dhabi. There was no announced ceasefire. Earlier three-way negotiations had stalled, and Russia has intensified attacks while Ukraine faces shortages of the interceptors used to defend its cities. (BBC)

Volodymyr Zelensky said he would accept a freeze along the current front line as a minimum step towards stopping the killing. Security guarantees and a possible halt to attacks on energy infrastructure had featured in his discussions with Witkoff in Oslo a day earlier. The positions remain far apart: Moscow claims its forces hold the strategic initiative, while Ukraine and European allies dispute that description. Russia occupies about a fifth of Ukrainian territory. Washington’s renewed push also comes amid friction over sanctions and military intelligence. Zelensky said intelligence sharing was working again after Kyiv had accused the US of withholding warnings of ballistic missile launches. A proposed meeting date leaves the immediate question of civilian protection unanswered. Russia also released Stephen Hubbard, a 74-year-old American who had been imprisoned for allegedly fighting for Ukraine. Trump said his release involved no prisoner exchange. That brought a tangible result from the diplomatic contact, while the broader negotiations still lacked an agreed route to ending the fighting or protecting Ukraine from another attack after a settlement. (BBC)

Care-home families describe the lasting cost of isolation

Bereaved families have described renewed anger following the Covid inquiry’s findings on social care. The inquiry concluded that the sector had been treated as an afterthought, that discharging untested hospital patients into care homes helped the virus spread, and that blanket restrictions on contact caused severe emotional harm. Relatives who accepted separation as a necessary protection now have to reckon with the finding that residents endured profound isolation while infection still reached them. The consequences extended across years, with some families unable to resume meaningful contact before their loved ones died. (Guardian)

Their accounts describe the practical limits of the arrangements intended to replace visits. Some residents could speak to family only when staff found time to bring a telephone; others struggled with conversations through windows or glass partitions. Families described withdrawal from meals and activities, fear and a sense of abandonment. Care workers also faced the loss of residents they knew well, shortages of protective equipment and anxiety about carrying infection home. These experiences give substance to the inquiry’s institutional findings. Future preparedness has to account for the support residents receive from regular human contact, alongside the measures used to limit transmission. Families are still living with the time they lost. (Guardian)

Brazil’s runoff remains close as work and religion divide voters

Flávio Bolsonaro and Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva remain within the margin of error in a Meio/Ideia presidential runoff poll released on Wednesday. Bolsonaro has 48% of voting intentions and Lula 46%, with a margin of error of 2.5 percentage points. Blank and invalid votes account for 3.4%, and 2.6% are undecided. Once those responses are excluded, the figures become 51.1% and 48.9%. The survey interviewed 1,500 voters by telephone between 15 and 19 October, with a stated confidence level of 95%. It offers a close snapshot rather than a secure prediction of the result. (Meio)

The survey also asked what improving one’s life meant. About 43% chose owning a business or working independently; 32% preferred formal employment with guaranteed rights. Among people who aspired to their own business, Bolsonaro led by roughly 62% to 34%. Among those favouring formal employment, Lula led by about 65% to 30%. Religious affiliation produced another strong division: evangelical respondents heavily favoured Bolsonaro, while Catholics favoured Lula overall. Those breakdowns describe patterns within the sample and do not establish why individual voters choose a candidate. They nevertheless show the different expectations the campaigns are addressing. With almost 84% saying their choice is settled, the remaining contest is taking place within an electorate where many people already have firm commitments. The poll found that the two groups defined by their ambitions had broadly similar income distributions. Among people already self-employed or running a business, Bolsonaro’s lead was smaller than among those who wanted to do so. Lula led in the North and Northeast, while Bolsonaro was ahead in the Centre-West, Southeast and South. These subgroup estimates carry additional uncertainty beyond the headline result. (Meio)

Booking.com faces an EU investigation into fraud safeguards

The European Commission has opened an investigation into whether Booking.com does enough to prevent fraudulent accommodation listings and payment scams. Officials are examining its risk assessments and safeguards under the Digital Services Act, as well as whether paid promotion of accommodation is clearly identified. Opening the case does not establish a breach. Booking.com says it is engaging constructively with the commission and working with authorities and industry partners to protect travellers, accommodation providers and personal information. (Guardian)

The inquiry covers two distinct ways a holiday booking can go wrong. A fraudulent listing can leave a traveller arriving at a property that was never available to rent. In a reservation hijack, criminals use compromised booking information to impersonate a legitimate accommodation provider and demand another payment, sometimes through the platform’s own messaging system. Consumer researchers have demonstrated how a fake listing could accept payment for a stay at 10 Downing Street. Regulators will assess whether the platform’s systems adequately address such risks. The case also tests how its advertising disclosures help travellers understand why a property appears prominently. A breach of the DSA can carry a fine of up to 6% of worldwide annual turnover, though the investigation’s outcome remains open. (Guardian)

Technology

An emergency PHP update takes parts of the web offline

Governments and website operators took services offline on Wednesday as they raced to address two critical vulnerabilities in PHP, a programming language used across web hosting and content-management systems. A fix was released in the afternoon after details leaked ahead of the planned disclosure. Finland suspended public government web services, a Dutch cybersecurity agency took its own site down, and the Wikimedia Foundation removed some systems as a precaution while patching them. The interruptions do not, by themselves, mean that the affected organisations were hacked. Some chose downtime to reduce their exposure while installing the update. (BBC, The Verge)

The flaws can allow an attacker to run code on a vulnerable server without an administrator’s login. That creates risks for databases, credentials and other information stored there. Researchers at Calif and Wiz used advanced AI systems in finding and developing the vulnerabilities, which had remained hidden for years. Cloudflare’s chief technology officer said early attacks were emerging. Britain’s National Cyber Security Centre and counterparts abroad urged organisations to apply the fixes and consider taking systems offline when an immediate remedy was unavailable. For operators, the task includes locating PHP inside hosting services and third-party products as well as obvious websites. Older, unsupported installations add another complication. The PHP Foundation released updates for supported versions, leaving organisations on PHP 8.1 or earlier needing to move to a newer release. The vulnerabilities concern software running on servers, so ordinary visitors cannot repair them by updating a browser. For a small business using managed hosting, the provider’s response is part of the recovery; larger organisations must also check products that quietly include PHP. The scale of exposure remains uncertain; widely circulated estimates about the share of the internet affected should be treated cautiously. (BBC, The Verge)

Meta plans non-voting shares to help finance its AI expansion

Meta plans a new class of non-voting shares to help finance AI investment, acquisitions and employee compensation, with trading expected to begin in 2027. The announcement accompanied third-quarter results and another enormous investment forecast: capital spending is now expected to reach $135bn–$145bn this year, raising the lower end of its previous range. Data centres, computing capacity and recruitment are absorbing funds as the company competes to build models and consumer services. The proposed shares would provide an additional financing tool without giving their holders voting rights. (The Verge)

The results also show the financial weight of Meta’s legal settlements. Its quarterly costs included $9.89bn associated with the nationwide child-safety agreement reached with US attorneys general. Revenue reached $64.60bn, up 26% from a year earlier. The company put operating income before the settlement charge at $22.89bn, a figure that needs to be read alongside the substantial expense actually incurred. Meta’s new Muse agent has attracted millions of downloads, but privacy problems have accompanied the launch, including the suspension of a conversation-sharing feature after researchers found chats publicly accessible. Its expansion therefore combines a large construction and financing programme with continuing pressure over how its products handle personal information and protect users. (The Verge, The Verge)

The data-centre buildout puts cleaner air under pressure

A study of proposed data-centre expansion in Texas warns that the electricity needed could reverse years of progress on air pollution. The Houston Advanced Research Center modelled several paths for demand and power supply. In a scenario where all planned projects connect to the grid and use its anticipated generation mix, power-sector fine-particle pollution would more than triple within five years. Nitrogen oxide emissions would rise 136%, while carbon dioxide emissions would climb 90% above 2025 levels. These are conditional projections, dependent on which projects are built and how they are powered. (The Verge)

That distinction matters because many proposed facilities remain speculative. Developers also have choices about generation. Pairing grid electricity with renewable power generated on site could substantially reduce pollution; building gas engines to bypass connection delays could make it worse. Texas already has a large concentration of operating and under-construction data-centre capacity, giving those decisions consequences for surrounding communities as well as the national AI industry. Soot and smog-forming emissions affect local air quality, adding a separate concern to the carbon footprint of electricity use. The report makes the choice of power supply central to the environmental outcome. A rush to bring computing capacity online can lock in that choice for years. (The Verge)

EA closes BioWare and cancels the next Mass Effect

Electronic Arts is shutting BioWare and ending development of the next Mass Effect, bringing an abrupt end to a studio closely associated with story-led role-playing games. The closure forms part of cuts affecting about 400 EA employees, including roughly 50 BioWare roles. EA’s staff memo describes a decision to concentrate investment on a smaller set of opportunities. The company will close BioWare’s Edmonton workspace. For players who had followed years of previews and hints, the next game’s promised return to the series’ universe has ended before release. (IGN)

The decision follows disappointing performances from Anthem and Dragon Age: The Veilguard, and arrives a few months after EA’s $55bn leveraged buyout. EA is continuing major sports franchises, Battlefield, Apex Legends and selected action-adventure projects, including the next Star Wars Jedi game and Marvel’s Iron Man. Its executives envisage fewer large projects running simultaneously. The restructuring illustrates how a publisher can keep investing heavily while narrowing the range of games it makes. Mass Effect’s planned television adaptation remains in development at Amazon MGM Studios, but that does not provide a replacement for the cancelled game or resolve the future of the gaming franchise. The immediate, confirmed consequences are the project’s cancellation and the loss of the studio and its jobs. (IGN)

Sports

Rice earns Arsenal a point in Munich as Saka goes off injured

Declan Rice scored in the 82nd minute to earn Arsenal a 2–2 draw at Bayern Munich, finishing low after a pass from substitute Viktor Gyökeres. Eberechi Eze had put Arsenal ahead following Martin Ødegaard’s through ball, with a video review overturning the initial offside decision. Harry Kane then helped turn the match: his pass set up Alphonso Davies for Bayern’s equaliser, and he converted a penalty after Jurriën Timber handled. Rice’s late intervention gave Arsenal a point at a ground where Bayern have been exceptionally difficult to beat in the competition’s opening phase. (Guardian)

The evening also brought concern over Bukayo Saka, who left the pitch after ten minutes with a quadriceps injury. Mikel Arteta described the early signs as worrying, with further assessment needed to establish its severity and the likely absence. Eze came on to the left and Noni Madueke moved to the right, giving Arsenal a workable response during the game. Saka has already dealt with several interruptions to his fitness, including an Achilles problem last season and a hamstring injury that required surgery in 2024. His recovery timetable will matter as Arsenal balance domestic fixtures with their European campaign. Both goalkeepers made important saves: Manuel Neuer denied Bruno Guimarães from a corner, while David Raya stopped a close-range Dayot Upamecano header. Arsenal threatened a winner late on, but the draw leaves attention divided between a strong away performance and the fitness of one of their most influential attackers. (Guardian, BBC)

Villa keep winning; Como deepen United’s troubles

Aston Villa beat Viking 3–1 to make it three wins from three in the Champions League. Ross Barkley headed the opener from Ibrahim Mbaye’s corner and Emiliano Buendía added a penalty before Henrik Heggheim’s own goal put Villa three ahead. Peter Christiansen’s volley gave Viking a consolation after a video review. Villa’s first-half control did most of the work, although the visitors became more dangerous after the interval. Unai Emery’s team have nine points and 12 consecutive European home victories; a trip to Barcelona will offer a demanding next examination. (Guardian)

Manchester United’s night was much worse. Como won 4–1, with Nico Paz scoring twice and Tasos Douvikas and Jacobo Ramón also on target before Joshua Zirkzee’s late reply. Michael Carrick apologised to travelling supporters, while captain Bruno Fernandes publicly questioned the decision to hold off pressing Como. Carrick defended aspects of the approach and criticised his side’s effort. United have only two wins from 11 matches in all competitions and sit 13th in the Premier League, with Bournemouth due at Old Trafford on Sunday. The result intensifies scrutiny of the manager and leaves a visible disagreement over tactics between the bench and the captain. (Guardian)
