Britain asks households to prepare for three days of disruption

Households are being asked to prepare for three days without the everyday services they rely on, as ministers warn about severe weather, cyber-attacks and sabotage. The new Be Ready campaign covers food, drinking water, warmth and ways to receive information during an outage. Defence secretary Wes Streeting said families and businesses should think through how they would cope if supply chains or payment systems stopped working. He said the advice was intended to improve general readiness, with no particular imminent incident behind its release. (BBC)

The checklist starts with three litres of drinking water per person each day, with extra needed for cooking and washing, and food that can be eaten without refrigeration or cooking. A torch and spare batteries, warm clothes, blankets, a charged power bank and a battery-powered or wind-up FM radio are among the suggested supplies. Written phone numbers and radio frequencies give people a way to stay informed when a phone has run out of power. The guidance also covers copies of important documents and a mix of cash and cards. (GOV.UK)

People are encouraged to start with what is already in their cupboards and buy missing essentials gradually. The list needs to fit the household: regular medicines, baby supplies, equipment and food for pets may all matter. Ready-to-feed baby formula is suggested because boiling water could become difficult. Households can review their supplies every six months, replacing expired items and changing the kit as their needs change. The campaign acknowledges that people have different resources and will be able to take different steps. (GOV.UK)

Preparation also means agreeing who will check on someone who lives alone, choosing a meeting place if family members cannot contact each other, and sharing details with neighbours. Eligible households can register for extra support from their utility providers. Streeting linked the campaign to this summer’s fires and the prospect of winter flooding, while security minister Dan Jarvis said better household readiness would let emergency services concentrate on people who need the most help. The advice arrives as public bodies are dealing with a major software security alert. (GOV.UK, BBC)

Military aircraft join Britain’s wildfire response

RAF Chinook helicopters and A400M planes will be available to help civilian firefighters tackle wildfires across the UK, under plans announced ahead of Friday’s first national wildfire summit. Fire services will be able to request support for difficult terrain, including steep hillsides and remote moorland. There have been 1,350 wildfires in England and Wales this year, according to the National Fire Chiefs Council, following Britain’s hottest summer on record. MPs have warned that arrangements for aerial support and water supplies need improvement. (BBC)

The government also plans £45m for lighter protective equipment designed for prolonged work outdoors, and a £25m programme running from April 2027 to April 2029 to restore dried-out peatlands in England. Rewetting peat can help create natural firebreaks. Support for farmers to make land more resistant to fire and closer work with devolved governments are also planned. The summit will bring together fire chiefs, local leaders and technology specialists as ministers consider stronger restrictions on activities and products that can start fires during periods of high risk. (BBC)

Ukraine waits for clarity over satellite access as peace diplomacy resumes

Donald Trump said Elon Musk had restored Ukraine’s Starlink internet access after restrictions hampered its armed forces. Earlier on Thursday, Volodymyr Zelenskyy said the military service had still not returned to its full operating speed. Musk has denied responsibility for the slowdown, and the White House has denied ordering restrictions. Ukraine uses civilian Starlink alongside the more secure Starshield network for military communications, including drones. Changes to connectivity can therefore affect both attacks and defence against incoming weapons. (BBC)

Trump declined to confirm whether he had personally asked Musk to remove limits. The competing accounts leave important details unresolved: who imposed the restrictions, precisely which services were affected and how fully access has recovered. Zelenskyy said he was waiting for information from US secretary of state Marco Rubio. The dispute comes as Washington presses Kyiv to stop strikes on Russian oil refineries, with Trump arguing that the attacks have pushed energy costs higher. Ukraine and European allies have criticised his easing of some Russian sanctions. (BBC)

Britain meanwhile rejected US envoy Steve Witkoff’s praise of Vladimir Putin after their meeting in St Petersburg. Downing Street pointed to the deaths caused by Russia’s invasion and its attacks on Ukrainian energy infrastructure. Russia agreed in principle to resume talks with Ukraine and the US, while missile and drone attacks continued. Trump suggested a settlement might be closer. The UK has continued to back Zelenskyy, whom Andy Burnham received at Downing Street last week. Further negotiations would take place with fighting still under way and substantial differences over the terms of any settlement. (Guardian)

Brazil steps back from a WhatsApp restriction before the runoff

Brazil’s electoral court president, Kassio Nunes Marques, said a proposal to suspend large WhatsApp channels around the presidential runoff was unlikely to proceed for this election. The draft would have affected channels with more than 200,000 followers during the 48 hours before voting and 24 hours afterwards. Channels used by both Lula’s and Flávio Bolsonaro’s campaigns would have fallen within its scope. The proposal could return for the 2028 municipal elections. (Meio)

A group of 110 legal specialists, researchers and civil society representatives opposed the measure, arguing that it would restrict political expression and change the conditions of the contest shortly before voting. The draft envisaged preventive suspension based on audience size, without requiring a proven breach. Existing rules already allow punishment of unlawful digital campaigning. The debate has exposed divisions over how electoral authorities should respond to powerful online channels during a close presidential race. (Meio)

The campaigns are also trading demands for the release of financial and telephone records before the Globo debate. Lula challenged Bolsonaro to disclose eight years of records concerning himself and relatives, offering reciprocal disclosure. Bolsonaro responded with additional conditions and argued that the proposal would miss possible activity involving third parties. Lula accepted the conditions and promised to file an authorisation with the electoral court. Those public commitments still need to be distinguished from the records themselves: the exchange has created a test of what each campaign will actually make available. (Meio)

A global assessment puts coral reefs at severe risk

Almost all the world’s shallow-water coral reefs face a high risk of ecosystem collapse by 2075 when projected warming is included in a new assessment coordinated by the International Union for Conservation of Nature. It examined 127 reef regions across more than 80 countries. About 24% are already classed as critically endangered; the share rises to 96% when future climate impacts are taken into account. The projection assumes an emissions pathway leading to roughly 2.7C of warming by the end of the century. (Guardian)

Collapse means a reef can no longer sustain itself after a near-total or complete loss of living reef-building corals. That would affect fisheries, coastal protection and tourism, alongside the many marine species that depend on reefs. The assessment does not fully capture the latest global mass bleaching event. Scientists stress that rapid emissions cuts and local conservation could still improve the outlook. Reducing pollution and overfishing can help reefs withstand pressure, while the scale of ocean warming remains central to their survival. (Guardian)

Economy

City regulators propose narrower accountability checks

The Bank of England and Financial Conduct Authority have proposed reducing the number of financial services staff covered by enhanced personal checks. Around 140,000 managers would be freed from the additional requirements, while the most senior executives would still need to demonstrate that they are fit and proper annually and before taking a new job. The changes concern rules introduced after the financial crisis, when public anger centred on how few senior bankers had been held personally accountable. (Guardian)

Reporting requirements for lower-level misconduct would also be eased. Firms would generally report serious misconduct, final warnings and dismissals, while initial misconduct involving senior executives would still need to be flagged. Regulators estimate that removing the requirements could save businesses about £2bn over ten years. They say standards of conduct and accountability would remain in place. The proposals continue a broader push to make UK finance more competitive, following earlier changes including removal of the cap on bankers’ bonuses. Their practical effect will depend on how firms identify and escalate problems under the narrower regime. (Guardian)

AI investment costs weigh on technology companies

Technology shares fell sharply on Thursday as investors questioned the cost of financing the AI expansion, even where companies delivered revenue growth. Building datacentres and buying advanced chips has become more expensive, while borrowing costs have raised questions about the returns those investments will produce. Meta fell after revenue exceeded expectations but profit disappointed, and it announced a new class of stock to help fund AI spending and other costs. Chip equipment maker Lam Research also dropped despite slightly better-than-expected results. (Guardian)

The selling spread across major technology markets in Asia and the US. Nvidia and heavily indebted cloud provider CoreWeave were among the companies under pressure. Most companies in the S&P 500 actually rose, showing how strongly the largest technology businesses can determine the index’s direction. Higher oil costs added to concerns as the Iran war continued to disrupt expectations for energy supply. The combination puts corporate spending plans under closer scrutiny: businesses face expensive expansion projects at the same time as customers and investors are becoming more demanding about their payoff. (Guardian)

Technology

The race to patch PHP reaches public services and small businesses

Organisations around the world are checking and updating their systems after critical flaws were found in PHP, the programming language behind a large share of the web. Britain’s technology minister told the Lords that 32 government services were unavailable and four were partially unavailable, largely because operators had taken precautionary steps while applying updates. The government said major public-facing critical services were unaffected. The National Cyber Security Centre had no indication of successful breaches at the time of its statement. An unavailable website alone does not establish that an attacker gained access. (BBC)

The disruption has nevertheless reached everyday tasks. A garage owner in Chepstow described having to record MOT work on paper because its online system was unavailable. Websites and services in several countries were temporarily withdrawn while their operators checked exposure. Cloudflare described a rapid increase in attempted exploitation after the disclosure. The scale of attempted attacks and the number of confirmed compromises are separate questions, and the full impact may take time to establish. (BBC)

The vulnerabilities sit in PHP’s core engine, so a website can be exposed even when its own application code has no corresponding flaw. Research company Wiz demonstrated remote execution of code and disclosure of sensitive memory, which can contain passwords or authentication tokens. Its tests used WordPress, Drupal and Joomla as examples. A patch was released on Wednesday after coordinated work involving researchers, PHP developers and infrastructure providers. Updating the underlying software is central to the response. (Wiz)

Researchers also described how AI helped them connect longstanding weaknesses into working exploits and test variations across different systems. That can help defenders uncover problems that have survived years of examination, while reducing the specialist effort needed to develop attacks. The immediate challenge is the web’s varied ownership: large platforms, small charities and independently hosted sites all rely on shared software but have very different resources for maintaining it. The repair work reaches far beyond the organisations whose names appear on the biggest websites. (Wiz)

Higher component costs hit PC demand

Global PC shipments fell 21% year on year in the third quarter, according to Counterpoint Research, ending a long period of growth. Higher prices for memory, storage and processors have fed through to finished computers, squeezing buyers with smaller budgets. Manufacturers are shifting towards configurations that can absorb those costs more easily, leaving fewer affordable choices. Some are bringing older components back into use to keep prices down. (The Verge)

The comparison is also affected by last year’s purchases ahead of the Windows 10 support deadline and inventory changes associated with tariffs. Those factors make the drop larger, although the pressure on affordability remains. Counterpoint expects another sharp decline in the final quarter and broadly flat shipments in 2027. A stable shipment total would therefore leave the industry operating at a lower level. The figures describe units shipped rather than a direct count of people replacing computers, but they show how component inflation can slow demand across the wider hardware market. (The Verge)

Arc sets a December closure date

The Browser Company will shut down Arc on 22 December, ending the browser’s remaining support as it concentrates on Dia. New account creation has already stopped. On 23 November, Browse for Me and other AI features are due to be disabled, along with login and tab synchronisation. The final phase includes deactivating user accounts and removing Arc Search from Google Play. Security updates will also end. (MacMagazine)

The timetable gives existing users two separate deadlines to consider, with account and synchronisation functions disappearing a month before the final shutdown. Arc had already stopped receiving active development, although security maintenance had continued. The company considered making it open source and decided against doing so because its software development kit remained valuable to the business. The Browser Company is now owned by Atlassian. The announcement closes a product that built a following around a distinctive approach to tabs and browsing, as its developer puts its resources into an AI-focused successor. (MacMagazine)

1Password builds a sign-in route for AI agents

1Password has announced a platform that lets AI agents request access to accounts while keeping the credentials themselves out of the agent’s hands. When an agent encounters a login form, it identifies itself to 1Password and asks to use a credential. If the user approves, the password manager fills the details directly into the destination website. The service builds on an earlier Claude integration and is intended to support several agent providers. (MacMagazine)

The feature is optional and included without an extra charge for personal and family subscribers, with a waiting list for interested users. Agent connectors must be enabled before they can request access to a vault. That makes approval and account access part of the same workflow as the task the agent is carrying out. The design addresses a practical obstacle for assistants that act on websites: signing in can otherwise require handing sensitive credentials to another system or repeatedly asking a person to take over. (MacMagazine)

Australian court orders changes to iPhone app distribution

Australia’s Federal Court has ordered Apple to allow competing app stores, alternative payment systems and direct downloads on iOS following its antitrust battle with Epic Games. Developers using alternative distribution must receive access to equivalent tools and terms, and Apple is barred from penalising them for that choice. The order also limits attempts to collect commissions on purchases made through outside payment services or websites. (MacMagazine)

Apple can retain security and privacy checks, including notarisation, and can remove or disable apps that pose security risks or breach legal requirements. It cannot use those checks to obstruct alternative installation. The rules are due to take effect on 1 March next year, with the possibility of a court-approved extension to 3 May. Apple plans to appeal, citing intellectual property and safety concerns. The implementation timetable and appeal mean the ruling sets out required changes whose final operation still has to be worked through. (MacMagazine)

Apple adds an assistant to Pages, Numbers and Keynote

Apple has updated its office apps with an Intelligent Assistant that responds to ordinary language while taking account of the document being edited. Pages can help draft and reorganise text; Numbers can interpret data and help with formulas and charts; Keynote can generate slides and presentation notes. The assistant is in beta, works only in English and requires a device compatible with Apple Intelligence. (MacMagazine)

The AI features and new premium content are for Apple Creator Studio subscribers. Other users receive general fixes and performance improvements. Keynote also gains beta support for importing and exporting KEYX, an open interchange format intended to make presentation content easier to use elsewhere. All three apps add support for the iPhone Duo’s larger unfolded screen. The subscription and language requirements will determine who can use the most prominent additions when updating. (MacMagazine)

Sports

Guimarães settles into Arsenal’s midfield

Bruno Guimarães has described the emotional difficulty of leaving Newcastle for Arsenal in a £75m summer transfer, while welcoming the demands of his new club. The Brazil midfielder has already scored important goals against Sunderland and Leeds and is developing a partnership with Declan Rice and Martin Ødegaard. Mikel Arteta is still working through the balance of a midfield whose players can occupy several roles. (Guardian)

In Wednesday’s 2-2 Champions League draw at Bayern Munich, Guimarães played deeper while Rice operated further forward. Manuel Neuer denied the Brazilian with a close-range save before Rice scored Arsenal’s late equaliser. Guimarães described a squad determined to win even away at Bayern, with Everton next at home on Saturday. His return to Newcastle is scheduled for 21 November. The move has brought an early contribution on the pitch as he works to build the relationships he valued at his former club. (Guardian)

Carrick faces pressure after Manchester United’s difficult start

Unai Emery and Julian Nagelsmann have emerged as leading possibilities if Manchester United decide to replace Michael Carrick. Carrick is expected to remain in charge for Sunday’s match against Bournemouth, with no change announced. United have won two of their first 11 games this season, and Wednesday’s 4-1 Champions League defeat at Como has intensified scrutiny of the coach. (Guardian)

Emery’s contract at Aston Villa runs until 2029, making compensation a potential obstacle. Nagelsmann is available after leaving Germany following their World Cup exit. Other possible candidates include Antonio Conte, Eddie Howe and Kieran McKenna, while Brighton’s Fabian Hürzeler is understood to be unwilling to leave at this stage. Bruno Fernandes is not expected to face disciplinary action for criticising Carrick’s tactics after the Como defeat. United must now prepare for a Premier League opponent arriving after a strong European result of its own. (Guardian)
