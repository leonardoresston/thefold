News

Amazon clearance falls to its lowest level in 38 years

Brazil has recorded the smallest annual loss of Amazon rainforest since its government began measuring clearance in 1988. The Prodes satellite system found that 3,335 sq km was cut in the latest year, a fall of 41.8% from the year before. That total is 1,236 sq km below the previous record low, saving an area roughly the size of Rio de Janeiro in a single year. Since Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva returned to office in 2022, the rate has fallen by 71.2%, helped by stronger enforcement and renewed investment in environmental agencies. The numbers give Brazil something unusually concrete to take into the final days of a bitter presidential campaign: public policy has changed what is happening on the ground. (Guardian)

The improvement reaches beyond the rainforest. Clearance in the Cerrado, the vast savanna beside the Amazon, dropped to 5,516 sq km, its lowest level since 2001 and 48.4% below the rate four years ago. The Cerrado has often absorbed the pressure displaced by tighter rules in the forest because soy and beef producers may legally clear more land there. Within the Amazon, Pará, Mato Grosso and Amazonas still accounted for three quarters of all loss, though every one recorded a decline of more than 45%. Roraima, a stronghold of the Bolsonaro family, was the only state where the figure rose, by 0.4%. Progress remains uneven, but the direction is now visible across two ecosystems, extending beyond a single protected boundary. (Guardian)

The result lands nine days before Lula faces Flávio Bolsonaro in a runoff on 25 October. Lula treats the forest as a sovereign asset, a home for Indigenous peoples and a central part of the global climate response. Bolsonaro has courted landowners and extractive interests, followed his father’s pro-development politics and left the climate crisis out of his manifesto. Jair Bolsonaro’s term brought a 59.5% rise in Amazon deforestation as protection agencies lost staff and illegal miners, loggers and land grabbers encountered weaker enforcement. His son has promised to free him from prison and allow him a role in government. A narrow election will therefore decide whether the machinery behind this year’s record is strengthened, dismantled or redirected. (Guardian)

London wants the power to put limits on robotaxis

More than 100,000 people have joined Uber’s London waiting list for an autonomous ride since its first services with Wayve began in September. For now, only a handful of cars operate and each carries a human safety driver. The trial has already expanded to Manchester, while Uber plans to put 100,000 Nvidia robotaxis into service around the world from next year. That pace has sharpened the question of who controls the rollout. Transport for London can license a passenger service, yet the Department for Transport decides whether an autonomous vehicle may operate. Under the framework for future driverless services, TfL could grant or refuse consent without gaining the detailed power to set numbers, conditions or suspensions on London’s streets. (Guardian)

The London assembly’s transport committee wants that authority devolved. Its concerns include congestion, pressure on public transport, safeguarding, accessibility for disabled passengers and the livelihoods of more than 100,000 licensed drivers. The App Drivers and Couriers Union is due to protest outside Wayve’s London base this weekend. San Francisco offers an early glimpse of the scale that could follow: Waymo now supplies more than a quarter of rides there. London brings narrower roads, less predictable pedestrian movement and one of Europe’s largest private-hire markets. The immediate service may be small, but the rules written during this trial will determine how quickly it can grow and whether the city can intervene when the effects extend beyond the people inside the car. (Guardian)

Australia tries to give everyone an off-switch for the feed

Australia’s Digital Duty of Care bill would require social platforms to offer a persistent switch from recommendations to a feed made only from accounts a user follows. The option would have to be obvious, retain the ordinary functions of the app and stay selected after the user leaves. That last detail matters. Following-only feeds already exist in parts of Europe, but some platforms hide them behind menus, remove access to features or quietly return to algorithmic ranking at the next visit. A Dutch court forced Meta to preserve the choice after finding that design breached the EU’s Digital Services Act. Australia is attempting to put that standard directly into national law. (BBC)

The wider duty would cover social media, gaming services and chatbots. Children would have to be protected from material and design choices that encourage eating disorders, misogyny or exposure to pornography; adults would receive a narrower protection against illegal and seriously harmful content. Breaches could bring fines of A$100m, about £52m. The government’s existing ban on social media for children has struggled in practice, and surveys suggest most young users who were online before it remain there. The new approach shifts attention from proving a user’s age to changing the machinery presented to everyone. Nearly half of Australians say they would turn recommendations off, although two-thirds of Gen Z respondents would keep them. Choice alone will not repair every harm. It can still make endless optimisation for attention something a person actively accepts. (BBC)

Economy

Britain’s food exporters have lost more than a third of their EU trade

British food and drink exports to the European Union have fallen by more than a third since 2019, according to National Farmers’ Union analysis of HMRC data. Sugar exports were down 70% in the first seven months of this year against the same period before Brexit; poultry fell 53% and egg products 65%. Across the full years from 2019 to 2025, egg and egg-product sales dropped 74%, while horticultural exports declined by more than half. The EU still bought 68% of Britain’s agricultural food exports last year, so the collapse reaches far beyond a marginal market. Producers have lost volume where distance is short, tastes are familiar and supply chains once ran with comparatively little friction. (Guardian)

Customs codes and health certificates now follow every variation of a product across the border. A carton and its eggs need separate proof; fresh, dried, steamed and sweetened egg products each bring different paperwork. Scottish seed-potato exports to the EU, previously about 20,000 tonnes a year, remain banned because negotiators did not secure recognition of equivalent standards. Scottish salmon, Britain’s largest food export, faces document checks on every consignment, with the friction estimated to cost about £100m in lost sales. Fresh British burgers cannot be sold into the EU, and Welsh shellfish remains barred. The UK and EU largely agreed in May to reduce sanitary and phytosanitary checks from next summer, though important details and transition rules are unfinished. That deal could remove costs and delays for current exporters. Rebuilding customers and specialist supply chains that moved away during six years of barriers will take longer. (Guardian)

Britain’s route into ‘Made in Europe’ runs through Chinese cars

France has dropped its opposition to Britain joining the EU’s coming Made in Europe programme, opening a route back into industrial supply chains that matter deeply to UK carmakers. The proposed price is alignment with European trade defences against Chinese electric vehicles. Downing Street has left tariffs under review while insisting that the terms are still being negotiated; Beijing has warned against discriminatory restrictions. The calculation is difficult because about 80% of British-built cars are exported, more than half to the EU and 6% to China. Exclusion from preferential European procurement could cost billions of pounds in trade. Tariffs might protect domestic factories from subsidised imports, then raise prices for drivers and invite retaliation against the vehicles Britain sells abroad. (Guardian)

Chinese brands are becoming harder to treat as a distant concern. BYD’s UK sales rose 78% year on year in September, Chery’s rose 660% and Changan’s by more than 3,000%. The Franco-German proposal would let countries that were inside the single market on 1 January 2020 opt into the Industrial Accelerator Act if they accept dynamic alignment on investment conditions and relevant trade measures. European lawmakers have tabled 5,100 amendments, and final rules are expected near the end of this year or early 2027. Quotas could be introduced within months under World Trade Organization safeguards; formal anti-subsidy tariffs would require a longer investigation. Britain therefore has time to bargain, but the basic choice is already visible: preserve a permissive market for cheaper imports or secure a more protected place inside Europe’s manufacturing system. (Guardian)

Technology

Apple tightens the gate after harmful apps learned how to slip through

Apple has rewritten parts of its App Review Guidelines after a year of investigations found AI tools for creating non-consensual intimate images, disguised gambling services and other harmful features inside approved apps. The rules for user-generated content now name non-consensual intimate imagery directly and place developers who ignore safeguards or fail to correct known violations in breach of the Developer Code of Conduct. Another new section bars hidden, dormant or undocumented functions, including features concealed through code obfuscation, and makes removal from the store and termination of the developer account explicit penalties. Apps that facilitate child exploitation or human trafficking will also be blocked from alternative distribution as well as removed from the App Store. (9to5Mac, The Verge)

The change follows repeated evidence that review could be fooled by geography or timing. Dozens of gambling apps presented themselves as harmless games and utilities when opened outside Brazil, then exposed betting platforms to Brazilian users, including children. Separate inquiries found that App Store search suggestions and advertising directed people toward AI “undressing” services; San Francisco officials ordered eight of those apps removed in July. The sharper wording gives reviewers a clearer basis for enforcement, but it also underlines the gap between a rulebook and a live store containing millions of changing products. Apple controls the review process, payments and distribution for most iPhone software. Its next test is whether harmful functions are found before users and regulators document them. (9to5Mac)

Apple’s first folding phone sells out into next year

The iPhone Duo became backordered within minutes of pre-orders opening on Friday. By 8.30am on the US east coast, every colour and storage configuration showed an estimated delivery date in late January; customers in Brazil were seeing early February. Apple called demand extraordinary and kept orders open, while launch-day units are due on 23 October. The company’s first folding phone starts at $1,999 with 256GB of storage and rises to $3,199 for 2TB. It has a 5.4-inch display when closed, a 7.6-inch inner screen, an A20 Pro processor, two 48-megapixel rear cameras and Touch ID in place of Face ID. (9to5Mac)

The sellout is an imperfect measure of total demand. Production reports had already pointed to constrained hinge supplies and an initial run of roughly six to ten million devices for the rest of 2026, far below the volumes of Apple’s conventional flagship phones. Some early buyers immediately offered reservations online for thousands of dollars above retail. Even so, selling through a $2,000 experimental model within half an hour gives Apple the clearest possible signal that a folding iPhone can reach beyond a small demonstration market. The question now moves from product design to manufacturing: every delivery date pushed into 2027 is a customer Apple persuaded and cannot yet supply. (9to5Mac)

Ocarina of Time returns, while Remedy finds its fastest seller

Nintendo’s remake of The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time arrives on Switch 2 on 5 November, and its first extended preview suggests a careful modernisation of the 1998 original. Link can sprint and jump manually, the camera rotates freely, items sit in a scrolling bar and aiming can use motion controls. The rebuilt Forest Temple keeps its interlocking rooms, central chamber and deliberately sparse clues, so the old spatial design carries the experience without a trail of instructions taking over. Two Japanese television spots released on Friday also gave the first brief look at Dark Link in the Water Temple, along with Ganondorf, the Master Sword and late-game scenes. New players are being offered a piece of game history with fewer layers of old hardware between them and the ideas that shaped later combat, dungeon and world design. (IGN, IGN)

Remedy, meanwhile, has reached one million sales of Control Resonant in three weeks, the fastest start in the Finnish studio’s history. Alan Wake 2, the previous record holder, needed two months to reach the same point. The result came despite the digital release being cracked and pirated shortly after launch; physical editions have only just gone on sale, leaving room for the total to grow. Remedy’s games have often earned critical admiration while selling more slowly than their budgets would prefer. A million copies this early gives the studio a stronger commercial base for its connected world of Control and Alan Wake, and shows that distinctive single-player games can still expand their audience without sanding away the strangeness that made people notice them. (IGN)

Manchester City’s appeal has a high legal bar to clear

Manchester City will appeal after being found guilty of every charge concerning breaches of Premier League financial rules between 2009-10 and 2017-18, including the use of sham contracts to inflate sponsorship revenue by £830m. The club maintains that the money came from the United Arab Emirates government as a shareholder in sponsors and that City’s owners did not provide it. It also argues that the commission made material errors of law, fact and principle. An appeal is a narrower legal exercise than a new hearing. City must identify a decisive legal or factual mistake, or serious procedural unfairness that changed the result. The commission had already examined extensive witness testimony and documents before concluding that sponsorship arrangements disguised owner funding. (BBC)

City’s lawyers are expected to challenge how leaked emails were interpreted and may introduce government records, bank statements or evidence from Etihad Airways. Fresh documents could establish a clearer trail for the disputed payments; material already considered by the commission will be harder to use as the foundation for reversal. Etihad says it was not given an opportunity to participate or correct the evidence, though the sponsor was not itself the respondent and had no automatic right to appear. League rules call for the appeal within 12 weeks, which points to Christmas Eve, but the scale of the case may stretch the timetable. The decision will shape City’s future and the authority of the financial system the rest of the league has been required to follow. (BBC)

Arsenal’s title has not spared the club from job cuts

Arsenal are considering up to 60 redundancies after a structural review begun by Boston Consulting Group before the end of last season. Catering and travel are among the departments affected, while the men’s and women’s first-team coaching and performance staff, academy, community operation, foundation and site security are exempt. The club hopes to redeploy some employees and reduce the final number of departures. Scrutiny has intensified because the review follows Arsenal’s Premier League title, a rise in annual operating costs from £148m to £201m and Mikel Arteta’s new contract worth more than £20m a year. Arteta described the process as part of improving the organisation, then left the detail to executives. Success has given Arsenal more income and ambition; it has also made the distinction between investment on the pitch and savings behind it much harder for the club to explain. (Guardian)
